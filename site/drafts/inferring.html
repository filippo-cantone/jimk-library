Inferring

Strategy guide Reading · Comprehension Early years + Upper paths 10 packs · 73 mentor texts · 8 min read
Inferring is “filling in, in your head, what is not written on the page.” Readers combine Text Clues + Background Knowledge = Inference — and there can be more than one correct answer, as long as students can support it from the text.

1Why teach it?

  • It is the foundation under every other reading strategy — predicting, questioning and summarising all depend on it.
  • It moves readers past literal recall into meaning: feelings, motives, themes, word meanings.
  • It transfers to writing: students who infer well leave better clues in their own stories.
Key question for students: “What do you know that the author didn't actually say — and what makes you think so?”
From INFERRING 1 (definitions: Keene, Gear) and INFERRING 8.

2How to teach it

  1. Model it aloud — think aloud through a short text, charting clues and inferences where students can see.
  2. Practise it guided — sticky notes, two-column charts, partner talk; students show the clue behind every inference.
  3. Release it — students infer in their own books, then defend inferences in conferences and share time.
Four portable routines (use with any book): It Says – I Say – And So (4 steps, blank table included) · Observe – Wonder – Infer (photos first, then texts) · Picture Book Inferences (7-step chart routine) · Pairs Read: Read to Analyse (reader, responder, coach).
Release model: INFERRING 3. Routines: INFERRING 2 and INFERRING 7.

3Five kinds of inferring to teach

What a character feels, wants and is like — from what they say, do, and how they change. Start with feelings (role play, charades), then traits with evidence, then change across the book.

“Natasha is selfish” — proved from what she does with the doll.— Babushka's Doll (INFERRING 1). Try next: Willy the Wimp, Owl Moon

The central message must be inferred — it is never stated. Fables first (short, one clear lesson), then folktales, then novels. Themes must be inferred; the theme is the message, not the subject.

Lobel's fables → theme anchor chart (anger, self-control…), then Seven Blind Mice: “wisdom comes from seeing the whole.”— INFERRING 2 (fables) and INFERRING 3 (theme statements)

Hold the question in your head and read on. Reread for clues, check the sentence before and after, look for the root word, connect text-to-text.

“What are tight times?” — inferred from the cover boy, his plate of beans, and the dog episode.— Tight Times (INFERRING 2). Chart: Word / What we infer / What helped us (INFERRING 3)

Many inferences come from illustrations. Wordless books force the skill: predict from the cover, track pictorial evidence, retell, rename the book.

Flotsam unit: predict “flotsam” from the first pages → prove the title from layered-photo evidence → vote a better title.— INFERRING 2 and INFERRING 4 (Fox, wordless graphic novels)

Questions that go beyond the literal are a direct pathway to inferring. Three tools: QAR (Right There / Think and Search / Author and You / On Your Own), Question the Author (queries at stopping points), O.W.I. (Observe, Wonder, Infer).

Tuesday (Wiesner): Right There — the setting. Artist and You — the frog's feelings. On My Own — the falling lily pads.— INFERRING 1 (P-QAR) and INFERRING 7 (QtA with I'm in Charge of Celebrations)

4Worked example: Owl Moon

“As quiet as a dream” — what does it mean to go owling? Clues plus knowledge, combined in the open:

Text cluesBackground knowledgeInference
Night, snow, father and child whispering; “as quiet as a dream”Dreams are silent; owls hunt by night; you must be still to see wildlifeOwling means moving through the night woods in near-silence, hoping — not certain — an owl will answer
Try this: cover the ending, collect “I infer ___ because ___” on sticky notes, then read on to confirm or revise.
— Owl Moon unit (INFERRING 1). 70+ more demonstration books carry the inferring tag — filter Mentor Texts by trait.

5Two paths: early years vs upper

Early (P–2)

Picture books, wordless books, oral and modelled work. Feelings first, then character and setting. Minimal writing — sticky notes, talk, drawing.

Upper (3–6)

Graphic novels (gutter inference), the Chasing Vermeer novel study (clues, codes, 24 chapters of prompts), written response journals, theme work.

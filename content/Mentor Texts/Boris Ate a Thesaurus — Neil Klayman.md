# Boris Ate a Thesaurus

*Neil Klayman*

I’ll never forget the day I ate a thesaurus. It was during my first
visit to our school library. Miss Simms was teaching our class about
synonyms.

“Who knows what a synonym is?” Miss Simms asked.

I shouted out, “They’re those sticky things that taste good.”

“No, those are cinnamon buns,” she laughed. “A synonym is a word that
means the same thing as another word like buddy, friend, pal.”

“Who can tell me what a thesaurus is?” Miss Simms asked.

I hollered again, “It’s a giant, green monster.”

She chuckled, “No, that’s a tyrannosaurus, Boris. A thesaurus is a big
book where all of the synonyms live.”

I had never seen such a big book before. It was filled with hundreds,
no, thousands, maybe millions of words. There were too many words to
read. Besides, it was almost time for lunch, and my stomach was
growling. I just couldn’t stop thinking about those cinnamon buns.

That’s when it happened. I picked up the thesaurus and devoured each and
every word. I started with the A’s and chewed my way through the
alphabet. I didn’t even stop for a drink of water. L, M, N, oh my! I
just kept going. By the time I reached the X, Y, and ZZZ’s. I was ready
for a nap. I put my head down on the desk, and suddenly, I belted out
the world’s biggest BURRRRRP!

“Excuse me, forgive me, pardon me,” I said.

When I came home that night, I skipped dinner. I wasn’t hungry. My mum
asked if I had eaten something at the library. “Just a thesaurus,” I
replied. My mum said that she didn’t remember packing one in my lunch
box.

I asked if I could depart, go, leave to do my homework. At first, no one
noticed what was happening. But then, it became apparent, clear, obvious
with each passing word. I was suddenly speaking in synonyms.

My mum rushed me to the doctor. Never before had the doctor seen such a
case. She looked inside my stomach and saw hundreds, no, thousands,
maybe millions of words. I explained I never really aimed, intended,
planned to eat a thesaurus. It just sort of developed, happened,
occurred.

My mum was afraid that all of these words would make me sick. But, the
doctor told her not to worry. She said that I just had a mild case of
worditis.

“What’s that?” my mum asked.

“I’m chatty, gabby, talkative,” I rattled off.

Word quickly spread about me. People everywhere were talking about that
kid Boris, who ate a thesaurus. I was delighted, excited, thrilled that
I was so famous.

It wasn’t long before a television news reporter interviewed me.

“When did you first get hungry for a thesaurus?” he asked.

“I said, “It was by accident, chance, fluke that I ate, consumed,
devoured the book.”

“What words tasted the best? Which were the hardest to swallow?”

The reported asked hundreds, no, thousands, maybe millions of questions.

The next day, a letter arrived from the President of the United States.
He invited me to visit the White House and make a speech. It was a big
job for a little kid, but I was certain, confident, positive that I
could do it.

I was packing my suitcase for the big trip when suddenly I noticed
something was missing. My words were starting to disappear, evaporate,
vanish.

I couldn’t disappoint the President and let down my country. I knew I
had to eat another thesaurus. I just couldn’t conceive, imagine,
visualise not being the most celebrated, famous, popular kid in the
world.

That’s when it happened. I began eating one thesaurus after another. I
put one in my cereal and gobbled it up for breakfast. I carried a
thesaurus with me to school and chewed, nibbled, munched on it for
lunch. I even hid one in the freezer and heated it in the microwave for
dinner.

The day finally came for me to meet the President. I put on my new pair
of sneakers and even combed my hair. I was prepared, ready, set for the
best day of my life.

The President was waiting for me in the Oval Office. “You’re an amazing
young man,” he remarked. “I can hardly wait to hear your speech.”

“Thank you,” I replied. “I’m sure I’ll always recall, recollect,
remember this day.”

The auditorium was filled with hundreds, no, thousands, maybe millions
of people. I stepped up to the podium and reached for the microphone.
That’s when it happened. Nothing came out of my mouth – not a word. I
was speechless. I was so ashamed, embarrassed, humiliated that I ran off
the stage and out of the White House.

I ran as far as my new sneakers would take me. Soon, I wandered into a
huge, gigantic, immense library. It was the biggest library I had ever
seen.

“Welcome to the Library of Congress,” the librarian said. I was too
troubled, upset, worried to even notice her. The librarian quickly
recognised me. “Why, you’re the little boy who ate a thesaurus!” she
remarked. I nodded, too sad to speak. “I take it your speech didn’t go
so well?” she asked.

What speech?” I mumbled, muttered, whispered. “I ran out of words.”

“Well, then you’ve come to the right place,” she said. “All of the words
in the world are right here in these books. The secret to remember is
that books are for reading, not for eating. Every time you read, you
discover new words that last a lifetime.”

Suddenly, it all made sense.

“Books are for reading, not for eating,” I repeated. I was eager,
excited, enthusiastic to learn new words that would last forever. I
picked up a book and started reading. It was a delicious story.

The conclusion, end, finish.

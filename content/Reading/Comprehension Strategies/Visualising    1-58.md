## 1. What visualising is

Proficient readers create mental images during and after reading — using all
five senses and emotions, and (of necessity) drawing on prior knowledge. They
use background knowledge and the words in the text; they are personal to the
reader.

Images: provide rich detail that makes reading more memorable · help interpret
the text and recall it · are adapted as new information arrives · enhance
comprehension · are adapted again after sharing images with others · help
clarify reading · help writing (vivid images in reading → vivid images in
writing) · strengthen inferential thinking (when we visualise we infer with
mental images).

## 2. Evoking images — gradual release of responsibility

- **Teacher models** during read-aloud, think-aloud, showing how mental images
  improve comprehension.
- **Mini-lesson:** students share images — awareness of one's own images,
  elaborating on them, and the idea that this enhances comprehension.
- **Reading conferences:** children think aloud to the teacher.
- **Share time; small-group work; different response options** — art, drama,
  writing, speaking.

Poetry is excellent for visualising — short, thought-provoking, full of images.

## 3. Visualising with wordless picture books

E.g.: *Good Dog Carl* (Alexandra Day); *Pancakes for Breakfast* (Tomie
dePaola). *"We take clues revealed in the illustrations and combine them with
the missing pictures we create in our minds to make meaning."* — S. Harvey

Readers build meaning as they read. Prompts: *What do the characters look
like? What does the setting look like? What sounds could be heard here? What
could be touched or felt — what does it feel like? What smells can you
imagine?*

**Inspiration for sensory listing** (harvested from the books above):

- In *Owl Moon*: a bright moon · a train whistle blowing · dogs howling ·
  chilly air · black shadows · snow whiter than milk · an owl calling.
- In *Night in the Country*: frogs singing · a dog's chain clinking · a
  screen door creaking · an apple falling · rabbits pattering · a raccoon
  licking.

## 4. Image-making pathway

A path from a reader's rough first picture to a Saturday-morning draw:
**sense → schema → meaning.**

- Awareness of image. Noticing your mental picture as you read.
- Elaboration. Adding **the senses and emotion** — sight, sound, touch,
  smell, taste — **plus mood** to the first picture.
- Application. Testing the image against new information; adapting as you
  read, adjusting to the new.
- Connection. Sharing what you saw with others; combining your image with
  theirs, hearing theirs and reshaping.
- Purpose. Why? To clarify thinking, to draw conclusions, to understand.

### What "showing" usually looks like (the books he used)

Jim's "making images" and "using all the senses" lessons lean on books that
achievers elsewhere call **modest**: tactile, stable, plainly-read. He kept
to them for good reason.

| Book | What's showing ⇄ asking | Frame |
|---|---|---|
| *Pancakes for Breakfast* (Tomi dePaola) | Showing: picnic breakfast in the snow | No text — picture does the talk |
| *Owl Moon* (Jane Yolen) | Showing: cold winter night, a train whistling, a father calling owls, "make your own heat" | Tactile, slow, hopeful |
| *Night in the Country* (Cynthia Rylant) | Showing: sound after dark, "listen" blocks, quiet sleep | Soundscape as image |
| *Fireflies* (Julie Brinckloe) | Showing → asking: caught hundreds, then the light dimming — feelings named | Feeling emerges from image |
| *Charlotte's Web* barn (E.B. White) | Showing: barn by smell, by tool, by who likes it | Multi-sense inventory |
| *The Hobbit* hobbit (Tolkien) | Showing: a hobbit by never being magical, by feet, by hair, by appetite, by laugh | Character by texture |
| *Water Dance* (Thomas Locker) | Showing: water by shape, by state, by light | Repetition as show |
| *The Paperboy* (Dav Pilkey) | Showing: dark morning, paperboy by route-by-heart, his dog by sniffing/chasing/growling | Performer by routine and taste |
| *Dream Weaver* (Jonathan London) | Showing: spider by thread-like legs, web like wind on a pond | Small-gentle, then large |
| *The Seashore Book* (Charlotte Zolotow) | Showing: seashore by pretend, light shifting, sand, shells, a clam snapping, castle-building, swimmer-cold, a lighthouse "golden gleam on, golden gleam gone" | Going-there-by-imagination |
| *The Tenth Good Thing About Barney* (Judith Viorst) | Question/answer frame (ten good things; why the cat died/ground/heaven), asks reader "what good things" — and at the end renders death and its grief through a frame of ten | Question frame + feeling-rendering asking |

## 5. Reading deposit

#### Charlotte's Web — barn description (E.B. White), Chapter 3 "Escape" lead

The barn was very large. It was very old. It smelled of hay... it smelled of
the perspiration of tired horses and the wonderful sweet breath of patient
cows... it smelled of grain and of harness dressing, and of axle grease and
of rubber boots and of new rope... it was full of all sorts of things that
you find in barns: ladders, grindstones, pitch forks, monkey wrenches,
scythes, lawn mowers, snow shovels, axe handles, milk pails, water buckets,
empty grain sacks, and rusty rat traps. It was the kind of barn that swallows
like to build their nests in. It was the kind of barn that children like to
play in.

#### The Hobbit — description of a hobbit (J.R.R. Tolkien)

The mother of our particular hobbit — what is a hobbit? I suppose hobbits
need some description nowadays, since they have become rare and shy of the
Big People, as they call us. They are (or were) a little people, about half
our height, and smaller than the bearded dwarves. Hobbits have no beards.
There is little or no magic about them, except the ordinary everyday sort
which helps them to disappear quietly and quickly when large stupid folk like
you and me come blustering along, making a noise like elephants which they
can hear a mile off. They are inclined to be fat in the stomach; they dress
in bright colors (chiefly green and yellow); wear no shoes, because their
feet grow natural leathery soles and thick warm brown hair like the stuff on
their heads (which is curly); have long clever brown fingers, good natured
faces, and laugh deep fruity laughs (especially after dinner, which they have
twice a day when they can get it). Now you know enough to go on with.

#### From *All the Small Poems and Fourteen More* — Valerie Worth
*(selected, with their sketch subjects)*

**porches** — chairs sit still; the table waits for summer drinks; the
back-porch tools spill (an empty basket leaning; the watering can rusting
among friends). **aquarium** — goldfish flash gold-and-silver, flick and slip
away under green weed; round brown snails stick to the glass and stay.
**duck** — a toy-walking duck on yellow rubberskinned feet, wet sounds,
wooden yellow-painted beak, wagging tail flicking glass water-drops from his
flat china back — you'd like to pick him up and keep him on a shelf.
**caterpillar** — its feet don't patter like a squirrel's quick paws, they
ripple, one pair after another after another, travelling with its whole long
caravan of bristles down the brown twig to a greener midsummer dinner.
**acorn** — fits perfectly into its shingled cup with a stick attached at the
top; its polished nut curves like a drop drawn down to a thorn at the tip,
and its heart holds folded thick white fat from which a tree grows up — the
best invention or mechanical trick that could ever be bought in a shop.
**fireworks** — a far thud, then the rocket climbs the air, a dull red flare
hanging invisible a moment before its shut black shell cracks, claps against
the ears, breaks and billows into bloom, spilling clear green sparks, gold
spears, silent sliding silver waterfalls and stars. **frog** — the spotted
frog sits quite still on a wet stone; green with a luster of water on his
skin, back mossy, spots green like moss on a stone; gold-circled eyes stare
hard like bright metal rings; when he leaps he is like a stone thrown into
the pond, and water rings spread after him — bright circles of green, circles
of gold. **soap bubble** — a great soft sphere bends out of shape on the air,
leans, rounds again, rises shivering, heavy — a planet revolving hollow and
clear, mapped with rainbows, curled, seeming a world too splendid to snap,
dribble, and disappear. **slug** — slides sly by night to nibble the new
green shoot, to riddle the weak white root — hated by all but the moon, who
smiles on his scenes of crime and silvers his trails of slime. **web** — the
spider weaves it, swinging and switching her thread, running it round its
frame, closing the last flat spaces in, waiting out her purpose of flies;
but at dawn, when it hangs spangled with silver water, frail crystals of wet
light caught so neatly and needlessly, it is not her web but ours.

## 7. Learning structures (organised)

- **Partner Talk:** turn to partner and discuss issue or question.
- **Think, Pair, Share:** students think individually about a question or
  issue — then discuss with partner — then pairs share with another pair or
  the class.
- **Think, Pair, Write:** think individually — discuss with partner — then
  write what they are thinking; writing shared with another pair or class.
- **Group Brainstorming:** groups of four list as many ideas as they can
  about a question or topic; one member records.
- **Whole-Class Discussion:** can benefit from the practices above.

## 1. What a think-aloud is

Think-alouds are central to comprehension instruction. We show students our
thinking as we read aloud — often recorded by coding the text. Students see
our connections, predictions, questions, visualising, inferences.

> "As I read I'm going to tell you what I'm thinking, because good readers
> think as they read."

Think-aloud happens during read-aloud or shared reading. Shared-reading
language may include: *I'm thinking that… / From the title and cover
illustrations, I'm going to predict that… / That made me think about… / And
now I'm thinking… / The illustrations help me to… / I have a picture in my
mind… / I'm going to read that again, I'm a little confused. / In this next
part… / What was going through my mind was… / Let me rethink this. / One of
the good things that readers do is always think… / I'm predicting that… /
Good readers are always thinking about their reading. / I'm connecting this
to when… happened to me. / I think this character is…* (Regie Routman,
*Reading Essentials*).

Book selection is critical — the strategies must be genuine. In shared
reading, teacher and students share strategy use, thinking through the text
together. Model think-aloud in read-aloud/shared reading; picture books suit
(brevity), novels work too.

**Release:** name strategies → students imitate at stops (talk, sketch to
stretch, think, record) → think-pair-share with text evidence → group
strategy practice → post-its on own copies → independent practice time.

## 2. Think-aloud with nonfiction

Read-alouds include nonfiction — readers hear how good nonfiction sounds,
immerse in quality, learn visual information, see how nonfiction writing
differs, build shared content understanding, learn strategies.

> "A think-aloud of reading is creating a record, either through writing or
> talking aloud, of the strategic decision-making and interpretive processes
> of going through a text, reporting everything the reader is aware of
> noticing, doing, seeing, feeling, asking, and understanding as she reads. A
> think-aloud involves talking about the reading strategies you are using and
> the content of the piece you are reading." — T. Wilhelm

We don't always report everything, but we stay aware of using multiple
strategies. With nonfiction, think-alouds help: judge the book's fitness for
purpose; activate prior knowledge; connect; locate features (contents,
index); unlock new words; examine visuals (photos, captions, diagrams, flow
charts, timelines, cross sections); notice genre writing style and literary
devices; infer; question before, during and after; visualise (as a basis for
predicting and more); summarise and synthesise (telling them apart); fix up
lost meaning.

## 3. Fix-up strategies

Something to get unstuck with when confused. All ages need to know meaning
can be repaired — teach children to monitor comprehension.

- **Reread** — very useful; sometimes part of the text is enough.
- **Connect** (text–self, text–text, text–world).
- **Predict** — wrong predictions get rethought and revised.
- **Stop and think** — time to synthesise, question, visualise, weigh
  importance.
- **Ask a question, try to answer it** — clarifying who/what/where/when
  (character, setting, event, process); how/why goes beyond the text via
  inference or another source.
- **Reflect** on what was read.
- **Visualise** — comprehension improves with a mental picture.
- **Use print conventions** — key words, bold, italics, punctuation.
- **Retell** — activates background knowledge; reflection checks
  understanding.
- **Look at text structure** — how it's organised.
- **Write** — summary, response, even a jotted note clarifies thinking.

Background knowledge repairs meaning powerfully — memories, experiences,
subject knowledge, author style, text organisation help readers visualise,
predict, question, infer, focus and remember.

## 4. Classroom proof: questioning and inferring with *The Stranger*

Read to 3A with own copies; questions on post-its for group book discussion.
Samples (verbatim):

**Becky:** Who's the stranger? Maybe the mercury wasn't stuck — maybe he had
no temperature. Why do the rabbits want him to follow? Does he know the
rabbits? How can that be? **Hannah:** I don't think he lost his memory. Who
is HE!? Why did the rabbits expect him to follow him? Why did he turn weird
in his old clothes? Why did the leaf turn orange when he blew on it? Why did
trees and wind suddenly change? **Bridget:** It feels like a ghost around
your body… cold but not nasty. He changes things to autumn. I believe the
stranger is the fall king. **Alice:** Why is the mercury stuck? Why does his
blowing make drafts? Why don't rabbits run? Why doesn't he sweat? Hermit —
or what? Why was the weather peculiar? What was wrong when he was upset? Why
did he leave? **Steffany:** Does the broken thermometer have to do with it?
Why didn't rabbits run? Why didn't he tire? When he left, was it about the
weather? Why was it normal after?

**4B**, choosing one journal question each. **Riley:** *The stranger is the
spirit of all the seasons, forgetting it — rabbits unafraid, no sweat,
north red/south green. He left before goodbyes, disappeared, remembered,
became spirit again.*

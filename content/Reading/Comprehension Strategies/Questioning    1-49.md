## 1. What questioning is

Proficient readers ask questions before, during, and after they read.
Promoting student-generated questions leads to improved comprehension — they
are more interested in reading and more likely to remember information.

> "Questions are the seeds of powerful poetry." — Georgia Heard

Have fun with *"I Wonder..."* poems, using why, where, what's, how, who,
and when. When readers look for answers they focus on the text. We teach the
habit of recording questions before, during, and after reading, then sharing
them in pairs and groups. Code questions B (before), D (during), A (after).

> "Children should 'experience the insight that comes from questions, and to
> learn to love unanswerable questions.'" — Ellin Keene

Students should: question before, during, and after reading/listening · use
questions to clarify meaning and predict · understand that some answers are
in the text, others require inference from background knowledge · know how
questions helped them focus and understand.

**Question/answer codes:** AT (answered in text) · BK (background knowledge)
· inference needed · OS (outside source) · further discussion · still confused.

## 2. Questioning web & lesson structure

**Questioning Web:** a diagram with spokes — "IN OUR...", "TEXT...",
"AUTHORS..." as fill-in spokes (blank master in pack).

**Lesson suggestion:** Teacher reads aloud a story, notes questions on
post-its, discusses and displays, chooses one as focus. Then students:
independent reading, note questions on post-its, choose one for individual
focus, respond in Literature Response Journals.

**Questioning improves comprehension by:** interacting with text (establishes
purpose, focuses reading, forces attention to print) · motivating readers
(propelled to read for answers) · clarifying information (who, what, where,
when, why — helps understand plot, character, setting) · inferring beyond
literal meaning (difficult to infer without wondering).

**Types of questions matrix:** In the Text | In my Head | In Another Source |
Ponderable | Clarifying

## 4. Questioning within the text — example

**Tough Boris** (Mem Fox): Q: *Is Tough Boris a scary old pirate? What kind of
person is he?* Q: *How does Mem Fox create complexity in this character?*
Using illustrations: Q: *What is the little boy doing on the pages? Why is he
there? What is his relationship with Boris?* Q: *Why does Mem Fox use
pictures to tell this part of the story?*

## 5. Questions Game

Scaffolds questioning from literal to inferential:

1. Students read text, write 3 questions they want answered (10 min).
2. Choose partner, exchange questions, answer in writing, share (5 min).
3. Partners discuss answers, form 3 new questions — extensions, unanswered,
   or new from discussion.
4. Two-person team exchanges with another team, discusses and answers (10 min).
5. Two teams combine into four-person team, discuss the six questions (10 min).
6. New groups bring one question to whole-class discussion.

Works with poetry, short stories, novels, nonfiction. With poetry, sometimes
give poems without titles to avoid narrowing context. Students sometimes
lack background knowledge to ask forward-moving questions — after building
background through Questions Game, other texts extend predicting and
questioning. A graphic organiser (Multiple Sources, Multiple Perspectives)
builds on background knowledge gained. New texts provide opportunities for
seeing how other authors deal with the topic, extending inferring and
questioning. New questions lead to higher-level connections (T-S, T-T, T-W).

## 7. Supporting Connections and Questions

> "Many learners need some background knowledge before they know enough to
> want to know more." — Janet Allen

**Writing to Learn** (Allen): reading/writing relationship. Modelled via
read-alouds or students filling organiser individually during read-aloud.
Teacher reads from various sources → students note facts → after read-aloud,
students write responses → students note wonderings → after building
background and making connections, consider unanswered questions → whole
class documents understandings using B-K-W-L-Q.

## 8. QARS (Question-Answer-Relationship-Strategy)

Demonstrates different question types and where to find answers. Helps
students: anticipate questions · find answers · use questions to review
reading. Steps: introduce QARS types with examples → students read short
text → lead through process of answering each type, emphasise going back to
text to verify → practise.

**Group work:** review concepts → each group generates two questions per
category → exchange with another group → work collaboratively to answer.

**QARS categories:**
- **Right There** (explicit, in the text)
- **Think and Search** (implicit, put together several sections)
- **Author and You** (implicit and explicit, mesh thoughts with author's info)
- **On Your Own** (implicit, apply information from text)

## 9. Questioning Before, During, After Reading — Preps and Shared Reading

Children engage with strategies during shared reading before independent
reading. Guided Reading supports this — practises strategies taught during
Shared Reading. Even at this stage, conscious of moving from literal to
inferential questions. Children know good readers ask questions before,
during, after. After asking questions before reading, students read on to
have them answered. Not all questions may be answerable.

**Demonstration lesson:** *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* story told earlier in
year; this lesson uses a Prep-made 'innovation on text' big book — *The Very
Hungry Piglet*. Allows questioning to introduce/revise making connections.

Introduce book by showing cover:
- Does the title remind you of a story you've had before? (T-T)
- What will you expect to read in this story? (predictions)
- Have you ever seen piglets? (T-S) Tell us about them.
- What do you know about piglets? (T-W)
- If not covered: Why is the piglet hungry? What will he eat? Where will he
  get the food from?

During reading, teacher and student questions combined:
- Page 1: What will the piglet do when born? (infer from prior knowledge or
  illustration)
- What food will he find? Predictions possible — days of week, amount of
  food.
- How do we know the apple is rotten?

After reading:
- What happened to the little piglet after he ate all the food?
- Did knowing about *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* help us read *The Very
  Hungry Piglet*? How?
- This book was made by a Prep class. What ideas did they use from *The
  Very Hungry Caterpillar*?
- How does asking questions help us as readers?
- Possible extension: innovation on text.

**Shared Reading/Shared Writing:** often use Shared Reading (part of 5-day
program) as springboard for Shared Writing — retell or innovation on text.
Examples: *Swimmy*, *Goodies*, *A House for Hermit Crab*.

Questioning also used before/during/after nonfiction texts. KWHL organiser
can be used. See: *Dinosaurs* (Preps at end of unit), *Caterpillars*, *Flies*.
At Wembley some Prep poetry books made into big books — *A Big Fat Stomach
Like a Juicy Mango*.

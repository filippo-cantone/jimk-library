## 1. What prior knowledge is

Background knowledge is the information in your head — memories, experiences,
facts. Proficient readers consult their own experience before, during and
after reading. Relating unfamiliar text to prior knowledge — world and
personal — takes three forms: **text-to-self**, **text-to-text**,
**text-to-world**. Readers refine thoughts and knowledge as they read, gain
experience and talk. More relevant connections, better comprehension — and
background knowledge makes readers likelier to question and infer.

Connections help readers: go beyond the literal (growing sophisticated with
practice) · relate to characters (feelings, motivations) · visualise ·
attend (purpose, others' connections, active reading) · remember
(personalised reading sticks) · question (often into inference).

**Text-to-self:** text to myself. Personal experience is firsthand;
personal knowledge can be secondhand (books, TV, stories — anything gained
vicariously). Show children how to bridge books and lives; they can code
connections and write about experiences.

**Text-to-text:** new stories understood — and predicted — through known
ones (other texts, same author). Start with characters or problems; grow to
big ideas and themes. Sophistication ladder: comparing characters and actions
→ events and plots → lessons and themes → one author's themes, style,
perspectives → treatments across authors → versions of familiar stories
(S. Harvey). Jim's Cinderella shelf: Perrault/Bell, Perrault/Ehrlich,
Marshall's (with twist), Cinder-Elly, Korean, Cendrillon, Cinder Edna, Irish
Cinderlad, Egyptian, Yeh-Shen, Rough-Face Girl, Cindy Ellen.

## 2. Bedrock books and relevance

In writing, mentor texts teach craft; in read-alouds, Jim hunts books to
model reading/thinking strategies — the **bedrock** of students thinking,
talking and writing about books. Connections must be *relevant*: serving
meaning or expanding understanding — a way into the text, the character's
mind, the story's feeling. Great stay-with-you books (theme, plot,
characters all working): *Charlotte's Web*, *The Hobbit*, *Holes*,
*Wringer*, *Wilfred Gordon McDonald Partridge*, *Missing May*, etc. Some
serve writing mentorship; strategy modelling wants no craft-teaching agenda —
hence several read-alouds a day (Readers' *and* Writers' Workshop — plus a
poetry book always handy). Knowing books unlocks books: folk tales, fairy
tales, myths teach themes for older readers — one Anansi story lights up the
next trickster; Cinderella and Snow White make new plots instantly
recognisable; good/evil, loyalty/betrayal, sacrifice become familiar.

## 3. Knowing authors, knowing genres

*What predictions follow from same-authorship?* What you know of their
subjects and style · where predictions paid off · surprises vs their other
books · what you'd ask them · whether their life illuminates the books
(Polacco — via internet, *Meet the Authors and Illustrators*). The school's
growing author collections feed text-to-text powerfully.

Genre knowledge at work — two guided-reading cases: *The Big, Bad Cook*
(wolf recognition? same wolf? title page helps; *"Once upon a time…"*
predicted from Three Little Pigs knowledge, story differing anyway);
*Spiderman* through reciprocal teaching (title + cover photo predictions;
book kind?; what nonfiction furniture locates information — remembering we
don't read nonfiction cover to cover; dual font tracks — large text plus
photographed small print — read several ways).

## 4. Text-to-world and the nonfiction toolkit

Teachers build the background that buys independence and new knowledge. With
nonfiction, from the *Using Non-Fiction Texts* curriculum: KWHL (know · want
· how · learnt) · KWL Plus (plus mapping and summarising) · Bundling ·
Possible Sentences · SQ3R (survey, question, read, recite, retell) ·
Brainstorming · DRTA, also for narrative · DLTA · Text Structures · Semantic
Mapping · Anticipation Guides.

## 5. First schema lesson (D. Miller, *Reading with Meaning*)

Think-aloud works — with genuine strategies and critical book choice,
thinking through text together. *"Great readers think and read at the same
time… make connections from what they already know."* Background knowledge
is *"all the stuff already inside your head."*

**Text-to-self:** chart T-S connections during a read-aloud. Books that give
richly: *The Relatives Came*, *Fireflies*, *My Great Aunt Arizona*,
*Koala Lou*, *The Snowy Day*, *Chrysanthemum*. *Which connections serve the
story best?* Small groups: read *Ira Sleeps Over* aloud — *"keep your
connections inside your head"* — talk, record the most important on large
paper (pictures/words); or own copies for read-along/independent with
connection post-its. Assess: real connections? Understood use? Discussion
language? **Text-to-text:** predictions from the other story; compare at many
levels (characters and up; versions of tales — Little Red Riding Hood).
**Text-to-world:** general knowledge accrues yearly; older grades meet
complex social and moral issues.

**Schema, summarised:** activate before, during, after · connect text–life,
text–text, text–world · sort meaningful from irrelevant · rebuild schema on
new information, talk and experience · use schema to understand.

## 6. DRTA (directed reading–thinking activity)

**Purposes:** an active-process comprehension strategy (thinking,
questioning, reflecting) · realising past experience, language and context
knowledge predict stories, accumulating as they read · prediction serving
narrative comprehension · story sense and meaning-monitoring. **Preparation:**
teacher pre-reads and maps section breaks. **Procedure:** predict from title
and cover → read the opening; rethink; predict on new knowledge → read
section by section, verifying, modifying, rejecting. Reinforces text type —
knowing how to read a text eases reading: read as narrative (setting,
characters, main, other, problem, action, solution). **Whole-group
consolidation:** DRTA teaches what students know about language. Efficient
readers construct meaning: predicting from illustrations and text ·
verifying/modifying with evolving text · bringing personal knowledge · using
text-construction knowledge.

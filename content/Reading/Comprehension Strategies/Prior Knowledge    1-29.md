PRIOR KNOWLEDGE
e Background knowledge is the information you have in your head. It
consists of memories, experiences and facts.
© Proficient readers understand better if they think about their own
experiences before, during, and after reading.
e Relating unfamiliar text to their own prior knowledge, both world
and/or personal knowledge takes three forms.
- Text to Self connection
. - Text to Text connection
- Text to World connection
e Readers refine their thoughts and-knowledge as they read further,
have further experiences, and talk to others.

e The more relevant connections a reader makes, the better the

comprehension.

e Readers use background knowledge to enhance understanding.

¢ If background knowledge is used, readers are more likely to
question and infer.

How does Making Connections help the reader?

e With practise the connections made will become more
sophisticated, and take the students beyond literal meaning.

Making connections help the reader:

e Relate to characters, understanding feelings and motivations,

. e Visualise ~ have a clearer picture.

e To pay attention. They are more inclined to pay attention to the
text if they have a purpose, listen to others for their connections,
and read actively,

° To remember. When reading is personalised it is more easily
remembered,

© Question. By encouraging questioning the reader is often led to
inferences,

Text to Self Connections

Connections from the text to myself.

Personal experience is information gained directly from experiences.
Personal knowledge can be from secondhand sources - books, TV, stories -
any information gained vicariously.

DIRECTED READING-THINKING ACTIVITIES
Purposes: .

- To teach a reading comprehension strategy which enables
students to question and think about what they are reading in
order to make it an active process. Reading comprehension is
about thinking, questioning, reflecting.

‘ - Students to realise that their past experiences, their
knowledge of language and context enables them to predict
story. This knowledge accumulates as they read,

~ To use the skill of prediction to aid comprehension in reading
narrative text.
- To develop the students’ sense of story and monitoring for
meaning.
Preparation:
Teacher reads through the story in order to map sections (to tell
Students where to read to.)
Procedure:

- Predict what the story might be about from title and cover.

- Students read introductory section. Rethink predictions on the
basis of this section. Predict what will happen next based on
new knowledge,

- Continue to read to section breaks, verifying, modifying, or
rejecting predictions based on accumulated knowledge.

DRTA strategy can be used to reinforce text type. The more students
know about how to read a text the easier reading will be.
E.g. read as Narrative,
Story Structure:
Setting. Characters.

Action.
Solution/Resolution.

Amos & Boris William Steig.

Amos, a mouse, lived by the ocean. He loved the ocean. He loved the
smell of sea air. He loved to hear the surf sounds ~ the bursting breakers, the
backwashes with rolling pebbles.

He thought a lot about the ocean, and he wondered about the faraway
places on the other side of the water. One day he started building a boat on
the beach. He worked on it in the daytime, while at night he studied
navigation.

When the boat was finished, he loaded it with cheese, biscuits, acorns,
honey, wheat germ, two barrels of fresh water, a compass, a sextant, a
telescope, a saw, a hammer and nails and some wood in case repairs should
be necessary, a needle and thread for the mending of torn sails, and various
other necessities such as bandages and iodine, a yo-yo and playing cards.

On the sixth of September, with a very calm sea, he waited till the high
tide had almost reached his boat; then, using his most savage strength, he just
managed to push the boat into the water, climb on board, and set sail.

The Rodent, for that was the boat’s name, proved to be very well made
and very well suited to the sea. And Amos, after one miserable day of
seasickness, proved to be a natural sailor, very well suited to the ship.

He was enjoying his trip immensely. It was beautiful weather. Day and
night he moved up and down, up and down, on waves as big as mountains,
and he was full of wonder, full of enterprise, and full of love for life.

One night, in a phosphorescent sea, he marvelled at the sight of some
whales spouting luminous water; and later, lying on the deck of his boat gazing
at the immense, starry sky, the tiny mouse Amos, a little speck of a living thing
in the vast living universe, felt thoroughly akin to it all. Overwhelmed by the
beauty and mystery of everything, he rolled over and over and right off the
deck of his boat and into the sea.

“Help!” he squeaked as he grabbed desperately at the Rodent. But it
evaded his grasp and went bowling along under full sail, and he never saw it

missed us. And when they were finally home in Virginia, they crawled into their silent, soft beds

## 1. What determining importance means

Proficient readers always ask *"What is important in this part?"* —
separating meaning-central ideas from lesser ones.

- **Enter through nonfiction**: use text structure to decide importance.
  E.g. *A River Ran Wild!* (Lynne Cherry) runs a time-order sequence — a
  student timeline reveals organisation and the river's history. Dated books
  timeline easily: *My Place* (Nadia Wheatley), *Voices of the Alamo*
  (Sherry Garland).
- **In fiction**, children know the structure (characters, setting, problem,
  resolution) — e.g. *Mrs Katz and Tush* (Polacco; see story maps). In
  narrative, importance usually means inferring bigger ideas and themes:
  *Koala Lou* and *Feathers and Fools* (Fox), *The Lotus Seed* (Garland),
  *Mufaro's Beautiful Daughters* (Steptoe), *El Chino* (Say), *Encounter*
  (Yolen); novels *Sarah, Plain and Tall* (MacLachlan), *Missing May*
  (Rylant), *Sadako* (Coerr), *Matilda* (Dahl), *Hatchet* (Paulsen), *Tuck
  Everlasting* (Babbitt), *Dear Mr Henshaw* (Cleary).
- **In nonfiction**, importance means gaining information, scaffolded by text
  structures (see handout), text features, and reading strategies. Students
  need structure: top level → supporting ideas → supporting details.
  Organising mentally aids comprehension. Cause/effect, for instance, gives
  reasons — *Monarch Butterfly* (Gibbons), used in the Explanation genre
  study, teaches both determining importance and synthesising.

> "The most important ideas in well-written nonfiction are often deeply
> embedded in rich detail… Sometimes the interesting ideas are the most
> important, but not always." — S. Harvey

Read-along example: *Box Jellyfish* (National Geographic, Hamner 1994).

## 2. Tools

**Ideas and Details chart** (Topic / Book / Purpose; Important Ideas vs
Interesting Details) — students must *distinguish* the two in their thinking.

**Nonfiction text features.** Organisers: contents, index, preface, glossary,
appendix. Textual cues: signal words by structure (cause/effect: since,
because, this led to, on account of, due to, may be due to, for this reason,
consequently, then, so, therefore; compare/contrast: like manner, likewise,
similarly, difference, as opposed to, after all, however, and yet, but,
nevertheless; problem/solution: one reason, a solution, a problem; sequence:
until, before, after, next, finally, lastly, first/last, then, on (date), at
(time)). Graphics (*See What You Mean* — Moline): diagrams, charts, cross
sections, graphs, tables, word bubbles, framed text, maps. Illustrations and
photographs. Fonts and effects: titles, headings, italics, bold, captions,
labels, colour print, bullets.

## 3. Worked example 1: *Backyard* (roving assessment)

Roving questions, text-evidence required: *What information is important?
Why?* Build a class chart — Important Ideas, or two-column
Important/Supporting — through discussion. The chart may read: the child at
the window → wondering who stood there → yard as farm a century back →
covered-wagon pioneers → Native Americans → ice over everything → skins-clad
people → dinosaurs and strange beasts → all underwater → algae alone →
everything normal again → the child hunting bones and fossils. (#3)

Students write summaries from the chart. Assessment: *could a reader of your
summary grasp what the book is about?*

## 4. Worked example 2: *Home Place* (Dragonwagon)

Same apparatus. Chart may read: chimney fragment found → stone foundation →
marble, nail, doll's arm and more → imagining the long-ago family (mother,
father, son, daughter, Uncle Ferd) → Mama's daffodils → the burnt house →
yearly daffodil reminders. Summary test as above. Model summary: *A family
walking in the woods find a ruined house and imagine its family from the
clues — chimney, foundation, marble, nail, doll's arm. The mother planted
daffodils before the fire; every year they return as reminders.*

## 6. Early-years lessons

**Questioning.** One important question to answer — a focus. **Anchor
charts.** KWHL opens study: known · wonderings · how to find out · learnt.
**FQR (Fact–Question–Response):** record, question, respond with own
thoughts — serves questioning, importance, opinions, synthesis.
**Mini-experts:** expert charts in the voice of authority (narrow the
topic). **Making a point:** introduction, middle, conclusion.

> "All good writing centres around a controlling point or 'so what?'"
> Gathering nonfiction for a report, ask: *What is the most important thing
> for my audience? What big ideas must they keep? What structure shapes it?*
> (See Text Structure.) **The point is…**: same topic, different points
> (brainstormed, mapped); modelled writing takes one point, independent
> writing another on day 2.

## 7. Anchor charts for nonfiction (Harvey & Miller)

**Harvey's tips:** facts/questions/responses written while reading ·
nonfiction takes time — reread to understand and remember · fiction is a
movie, nonfiction a newscast/slideshow · stop and check sense · abbreviate
notes · think before writing · reading to learn.

**Miller (*Reading with Meaning*):** readers separate fiction from
nonfiction · separate important from unimportant to find key ideas/themes ·
use narrative/expository features to predict organisation and content · use
features to sort importance · use key parts to answer questions and
synthesise.

**FQR in action** (*Humpbacks Comeback*, National Geographic for Kids, Sept
2000): facts (breaching = leaping; 30× more since 1965; near-gone till the
law) → questions (is all jumping breaching?) → responses (WOW, a comeback;
dislike one-part whaling; buffalo-waste reminder). Sharing thinking fixes
important information.

**Conventions** (Miller, one a day, five+ sightings each, then hunt and
share — always *how it helps readers*): labels (identify pictures/parts) ·
photographs (exact looks) · captions (better understanding) · comparisons
(size via the familiar) · cutaways (inside views) · close-ups (small
details)… **Fiction/Both/Nonfiction Venn** (*Guided Reading the Four Blocks
Way*): title/beginning-middle-end/setting/characters/problem/events/
resolution/stories/themes vs index/illustrations/table of contents/help-you-
learn/photos/fun/captions/words/headings/cutaways/information/pictures/
ideas/amazing facts/front-to-back vs any-order.

***Strategies That Work* (pp.134–137):** topic-and-details forms lack
response space — add the third column (topic | detail | personal response)
so kids interact, record feelings and questions. Then **three-column notes**:
Topic | Detail | Personal Response.

# Summarising

> Reconstructed from the Jim K *Summarising* pack (31 pages of lesson
> material). Page 1 was a scanned decorative title; pages 12, 26, 28, 31
> carried graphic organiser / filled-in samples that OCR could not render and
> are flagged for visual re-clipping later.

Summarising sits next to synthesising. You cannot synthesise if you cannot
summarise. Both focus on *what is important*. The difference is that
summarising recalls and reorganises what the text said; synthesising merges
that with prior knowledge or across texts to form something new.

## What summarising asks of students

A summary selects the main idea, focuses on key details, uses key words and
phrases, writes enough to convey the gist, and takes succinct but complete
notes. In the Jim K classroom the language is: *Bare essentials · gist · key
ideas · main points · what's important.*

## Teaching approaches — overview

- **Keyword work** — highlight, underline, or write the key words. When
  stumped, reach for the chosen words.
- **Reduction writing** — start with a paragraph summary, then reduce to a
  sentence, then to a phrase. At each stage the most relevant information
  remains.
- **Reader's questions** — *who · what · where · when · why · how.* Use
  these to identify main points.
- **News Reporter** — answer who / what / where / when / why / how, one
  sentence per question, then combine into a summary.
- **5W1H organiser** — a graphic organiser that works across content areas:
  history (bushrangers), science (hurricanes, dinosaurs), art (famous
  artists), literature (author study).

## Reader's questions — the 5W1H frame

The pack gives a worked model built around a biography. The header row is:

| When and Where | What (Beliefs) | What (Problems) | How (Solutions) | Why (Was the person important?) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Sample questions for a biography:

- Who is the person, and who were the other key people in their life?
- When and where did the person live, travel, or become successful?
- What were the person's beliefs?
- What were their problems, adventures, failures, ideas?
- How did they solve problems, relate with others, achieve recognition?
- Why was the person important?

The teacher note is explicit: this can be used in *any content area* — a
paid endorsement of cross-curricular transfer.

## Paired Summarising

A paired protocol to let two readers articulate their understandings and
confusions through retelling and summarising.

1. Pairs read a selection. Each writes a retelling from memory. They may look
   back at the text to help memory, but they should not write while looking
   back.
2. When the retellings are done, partners trade papers and write a summary of
   the other partner's paper.
3. The pair compares and contrasts the two summaries. Discussion focuses on:
   - what each reader understands;
   - what they *collectively* cannot come to understand;
   - clarification questions for classmates and teacher.
4. Share understandings and questions in a whole-class or large-group
   discussion.

This is a structured way to expose what a reader did and did not take from a
text — useful for teacher conferences and for revealing individual reading
behaviours.

## QuIP — Questions into Paragraphs

A writing strategy. Use a graphic organiser to organise information and
synthesise it into writing.

The QuIP sheet has three columns:

| Question | Source | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| The question you would like to explore | The title of the book or internet site used to research the question | Notes that will help you answer the question |

## Sum It Up — the $2 / 20-word game

A memorable constraint-based summary frame.

> Imagine you are placing an ad or sending a message. Every word costs you
> 10 cents. Limit your spending to $2. Therefore the summary can have 20
> words. (Can be adjusted according to text.)

Procedure:

1. Read the entire selection — chapter, article, handout, primary source, and
   so on.
2. As you read, list the main-idea words on the *Sum It Up* sheet.
3. Write the summary using as many of the main-idea words as possible, one
   word per blank.
4. The final constraint: the summary must fit the $2 / 20-word budget.

The payoff is that students learn, through constraint, what *must* survive a
summary and what can be dropped. It is repeatable across genres and text
lengths.

## Somebody Wanted But So — the four-box frame

A four-part narrative summarising frame:

| Somebody | Wanted | But | So |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Action | Problem | Solution |

Either a folded sheet of paper in fourths, or a ruled page in a response
journal:

- **Somebody** — identify the character.
- **Wanted** — describe the character's goal.
- **But** — describe the conflict that hinders the character.
- **So** — describe the resolution of the conflict.

The teacher reminds students to *focus on the information that is most
significant.* The four boxes are the representation; the completed frame is
the summary.

## Story Maps and Story Summary

The pack works from shared reading of *Stellaluna* by Janell Cannon as a
read-aloud model.

> "Today we are going to learn how to tell someone what a book is all about.
> People will ask you: Who was in the story? (Characters) Where did it take
> place? (Setting) What was the problem? How was the problem solved?"

After reading, the class stops to gather information:

- **Characters** — who is in the story, and who else is important.
- **Setting** — where does the story take place.
- **Problem** — what is the problem.
- **Solution** — how was the problem solved.

The read-aloud is followed by four groups, each making one piece of a display:

- Group 1 — draw the characters, write about them.
- Group 2 — draw the forest, write about it.
- Group 3 — draw Stellaluna without her mother (the problem).
- Group 4 — draw Stellaluna with her mother at the end (the solution).

The display is then used to write a summary:

> This is a story about a bat named Stellaluna, her mother, an owl, and some
> birds. The story takes place in a forest. The problem was that Stellaluna
> lost her mother. With the help of the birds she found her mother and was
> very happy again.

## Tracking story elements

To monitor comprehension, students keep an eye on the key story elements
through a text. The guiding questions are:

- Who are the characters? How does this character develop and change over
  time?
- What do I know about the setting, and how does that help me understand the
  story?
- What major problem does the main character face?
- What contributes to the character resolving this problem?
- Have I identified the theme?

The teacher note points to *Train to Somewhere* by Eve Bunting as another
worked example.

## GIST — Generating Interactions between Schema and Text

A structured summary protocol for nonfiction.

Aim: to effectively summarise a text.

Strategies:

- Determine main ideas.
- Delete unimportant information.
- Use own words to generalise.

Procedure with nonfiction:

1. Use a passage that describes an important concept — displayed on a
   Smartboard.
2. Paragraph 1: students read silently, then write a summary using as many
   of their own words as possible — 20 words or less.
3. Shared writing incorporating the individual summaries.
4. Paragraph 2: students write a summary encompassing paragraphs 1 and 2 —
   20 words.
5. Continue. Final summary: 40–50 words.

The arc is cumulative: each paragraph is first summarised alone, then the
summaries are merged, until the whole passage sits in 40–50 words of the
student's own language.

## Lyric Summaries

A fun, musical alternative for narrative or expository summaries.

Purpose: to provide an alternative, fun format for narrative or expository
text summaries.

Procedure:

1. Review summarising. Ask students to note the types of information that
   comprise narrative or expository summaries.
2. Introduce the musical aspect: summaries can also be written as song
   lyrics to familiar tunes.
3. Choose a melody the class knows. Use it as the background for writing a
   Lyric Summary. (Shared writing first, then sing it with the class.)
4. Small-group work: groups choose a melody they know and a topic or book
   they have recently studied, and create their own Lyric Summary.
5. Share time: groups sing their completed summaries to the class.

The pack includes a worked example — a *Stellaluna* Lyric Summary written to
the tune of *Frère Jacques*. The lyrics follow the story closely: the birth,
the fall, the nest, the owl, the birds, the feeding, the promise, the flying,
the reunion, the return to the birds, the promise to visit. It is a useful
model because it shows how a whole narrative can be compressed into a familiar
song form without losing the plot beats.

## Narrative Pyramid

A shaped summary form for narrative text.

Purpose: to summarise a narrative text.

Procedure:

1. After reading a story, show students the format.
2. Line 1 — character's name.
3. Line 2 — two words describing the character.
4. Line 3 — three words describing the setting.
5. Line 4 — four words stating the problem.
6. Line 5 — five words describing one event.
7. Line 6 — six words describing another event.
8. Line 7 — seven words describing a third event.
9. Line 8 — eight words describing the solution to the problem.

Shared writing first: create a Narrative Pyramid as a class. Then small
groups, pairs, or independent writing. Share time uses the completed pyramids
for discussion.

The worked example is *Thank You, Mr. Falker* by Patricia Polacco:

- Trisha
- Artistic sensitive
- Michigan and California
- Trisha struggled to read
- She learnt to fake reading
- A boy named Eric taunted her
- Her teacher noticed she didn't read well
- With Mr Falker's help she learnt to read

The form forces a shaped summary — short lines, growing word count, a story
arc compressed into eight lines.

## Story Map — the fuller organiser

A fuller story-map organiser for written use:

- **Title**
- **Characters**
- **Setting**
- **Problem**
- **Plot** — events in sequential order
- **Resolution**
- **Theme**

The handout version adds date and author fields and sets the analysis in
three parts:

- **Beginning** — who are the main characters; when and where does the story
  take place; describe.
- **Middle** — what is the problem; how are the characters trying to solve
  the problem.
- **End** — how is the problem finally solved; how did the setting (place,
  time, period, culture) influence this story.

Reading Quest / ReadWriteThink attribution is noted on the handout; the
organiser is explicitly labelled for educational reproduction.

## 3-2-1 — the short-summary frame

A compactly usable summary frame:

- 3 things you found out
- 2 interesting things
- 1 question you still have

(Can be varied.) As a class it can be used to:

- Construct an organised outline.
- Compare and contrast with a Venn diagram.
- Identify sequence.
- Isolate cause and effect.

The discussion is based on the students' own discovered ideas, not the
teacher's.

## Main Idea 1 — a three-level note-taking organiser

A graphic-organiser approach to *locate · select · organise · remember*
important information.

In Main Idea 1, students list three levels of information: the topic, the
main idea, and the details. They record their information, limiting it to
seven to nine main ideas around any concept being learnt. Then they add a
one- or two-sentence summary of the main idea and develop a main-idea question
that ties the whole concept together.

Procedure:

1. Students preview a chapter of text to determine the topic and record it in
   the upper half of the circle on the Main Idea 1 organiser.
2. Students read a section or several sections to determine the main ideas.
   This sets a purpose for reading. No more than seven to nine main ideas
   should be selected.
3. For each main idea selected, students develop a Main Idea 1 organiser.
   They record the main idea in the bottom half of the circle.
4. They reread the passage and record important details that support each main
   idea, in the Details box. This revisits the text with a specific purpose
   and brings clarity to the concepts first read.
5. They write one or two sentences in their own words that summarise the
   information for each main idea.
6. They create a main-idea question. Strategic readers constantly interact
   with the text, asking questions as they read. Different types of questions
   can be formulated at several levels: literal, inferential, evaluative, and
   so on.

The organiser itself has four zones: Topic, Main Ideas, Question, Details,
Summary.

## Structured Notetaking

Structured Notetaking helps students learn organisational patterns, since the
graphic organiser represents the reading selection's major text patterns.

Procedure:

1. Preview the selection to be read, to determine the organisational pattern
   used to convey the information. Examples: Description, Cause and Effect,
   Problem/Solution, Compare and Contrast.
2. Create a graphic organiser that follows this pattern (see the handouts),
   complete with focusing questions.
3. Read the text and take notes by recording the appropriate information in
   the graphic organiser sections.

## Summary Cubes

A physical, six-sided summary/retelling structure.

Purpose: to provide a structure for summarising factual information or
retelling key points of a story. Can be used before, during, and after
reading.

Procedure:

1. Describe the information that goes on each side of the cube.
2. Use Read-Aloud and Think-Aloud to demonstrate the process of determining
   key ideas about either narrative or expository text to write on the cube.
3. Show the students how to assemble the cube.
4. Small groups can be used to guide the students to read a text and create
   Summary Cubes.
5. Share time.
6. Encourage students to create their own cubes as follow-ups to reading
   narrative or expository texts.

The pack gives three options for the six sides:

| Option 1 | Option 2 | Option 3 | Option 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Side 1 Who? | Title | Animal | Topic |
| Side 2 What? | Characters | Habitat | Subtopic 1 / details |
| Side 3 Where? | Setting | Food | Subtopic 2 / details |
| Side 4 When? | Problem | Phys. Descptn. | Subtopic 3 / details |
| Side 5 Why? | Solution | Classification | Summary |
| Side 6 How? | Theme | Illustration | Illustration |

# Symbolism

> Reconstructed from the Jim K *Symbolism* pack (29 pages, Critical Literacy
> folder). The body is fully readable; no OCR-flagged pages.

Symbolism is the use of a word, phrase, description, or object that stands for a
deeper meaning than the words themselves. In the Jim K classroom the entry point
is personal: *talk first about symbols the students already live inside* — sports
teams, badges, brands, colours, logos, gestures. From there the work moves into
literature, especially picture books and poetry, where a concrete image carries
abstract meaning.

## Two ideas held together: symbol and allegory

The pack separates the two but treats them as a ladder.

**Symbolism** — a word, phrase, or description that represents a deeper meaning
than the words themselves.

**Allegory** — a literary work in which characters, events, objects, and ideas
have secondary or symbolic meanings. Allegory takes symbolism a step further. An
allegory is a narrative that serves as an extended metaphor, a comparison that
carries through an entire passage.

The pack notes the distinction for teaching: symbol is local and can be plucked
out of a paragraph; allegory is the whole shape of a narrative and has to be held
across the whole reading.

## Lesson sequence — the feather

The worked theme in the pack is *the feather*. It is framed as a cross-curricular
link — the artwork from the artroom is a lead-in, so the literacy work and the
visual work share an object.

1. **Discussion of the symbol in lived experience.** How has the feather been used
   as a symbol? What does a feather stand for in different settings?
2. **Use prior knowledge and research** to plan a poem using symbolism.
3. **Poetry as the medium** — chosen because of restricted time. The poem can be
   rewritten in a different genre as a follow-up.
4. **Take the text through the writing process to publication.**

The teaching point is explicit: *plan symbolism ahead of writing.* Don't let the
symbol emerge by accident; set it up so the reader can meet it on purpose.

## The feather — the research spine

The pack supplies a reading list organised around feathers, so the research
component has real content rather than a blank prompt.

**The white feather**
General symbolic use — signs, peace, cowardice, messages, flight.

**Egyptian goddess Maat and the Feather of Truth**
The feather as measure of truth and justice — the weighing of the heart against
the feather.

**Fawkes — Dumbledore's pet phoenix from Harry Potter**
The phoenix feather as symbol of renewal, loyalty, and the magic that persists
across the narrative.

**Tawny Owl feathers in Native American culture**
Feathers as objects of honour, use, and meaning in specific cultural traditions.

**The Peacock feather**
The peacock feather as a symbol — plumage, display, pride, eyes, regeneration.

**Native American feathers — the Eagle feather, a Lumbee story**
The eagle feather as a Lumbee story — the specific cultural weight of the eagle
feather in one community's telling.

**The Dream Catcher**
The dream catcher as a symbolic object — protection, filtering, the night.

**Light Horse**
The light horse as a symbolic image — the horse in movement, the mounted figure.

**Black Hawks — Chicago**
Black Hawks as a symbolic name — the hawk as emblem, team identity, birds of
prey.

**Tar and Feather**
Tar and feather as a symbolic act — punishment, humiliation, historical practice.

**Brainstorm other ideas**
An open brainstorm slot so the class extends the list beyond the supplied text.

## Setting up a symbol ahead of writing

The pack gives a worked example of the teaching point — planning a symbol before
the writing begins.

> Man's life ending. Setting — autumn, onset of winter, cold winds, shortening
> days. Symbolises end of man's life.

The configuration is deliberate: the external world is tuned to carry the internal
event. The season is not decorative; it is doing symbolic work. The same logic
applies to any planned symbol — set the conditions so the meaning can be read.

## From symbolism into allegory

The pack closes the first section by extending the conversation:

- Discussion of the lesson and its extensions.
- Extending symbolism into allegory.

This is the move the pack wants teachers to make with older or more confident
readers: start with a symbol the students can point to, then ask whether the whole
story is *about* something else, and whether the parts of the story are standing
for parts of that other thing.

## Allegory — a closer look

Allegory is defined again, now with the teaching purpose attached.

**Allegory** — a literary work in which characters, events, objects, and ideas
have secondary or symbolic meanings. Allegory takes symbolism a step further. An
allegory is a narrative that serves as an extended metaphor, a comparison that
carries through an entire passage.

For teaching, the pack works through discussion of allegorical texts. The model
case is a student who has read *Animal Farm* — *ask Hugo to relate the allegory.*
The point is that the conversation about allegory should be concrete and
student-grounded, not abstract. The allegory is not a label to stick on; it is
something the reader can restate in their own terms.

## Dr. Seuss as allegory texts

The pack names Dr. Seuss as a source of allegory texts for younger readers:

- *The Lorax* — compare to *Truax* by Terri Birkett.
- *The Sneetches*.
- *The Butter Battle Book*.
- *Terrible Things* by Eve Bunting.

For each, the pack supplies a teaching lens.

## *The Lorax*

Teaching point: *The once-ler = people who start factories and pollute the world.*
The book is about what Seuss saw in the world as people caused more damage to the
natural environment when they thought they were making improvements in the world.

The comparison to *Truax* is deliberate: two treatments of the same problem, one
by a children's author and one by a direct response, so students can see how the
same allegory can be told differently and can be argued back.

## *The Butter Battle Book*

Teaching point: students find articles in current misunderstandings and
disagreements that get out of hand. Discuss the need for compromise. Discuss any
historical or current event that mirrors the book's escalation.

The book is read as an allegory of conflict that escalates because neither side
will compromise. The teaching move is to connect the Seuss world to a real world
the students can identify.

## *The Sneetches*

Teaching point: the book parallels real-life racism or political divisions. The two
groups are separated by class, differing by a star on the belly or not. The
star-belly Sneetches control all the best trappings of society; those without are
social outcasts. Sylvester McMonkey-McBean takes advantage of the situation. The
Sneetches come together in unity despite being taken advantage of.

The pack also supplies a real-world endorsement of the book's message: the story
was used by the United Nations to foster racial tolerance in Bosnia.

## *Terrible Things* by Eve Bunting

Named alongside the Seuss titles as an allegory text. The full book is not quoted
in the pack, but it is listed as a Seuss-adjacent allegory text — Bunting writing a
picture book that carries political meaning in the same register as the Seuss
titles.

## Shaun Tan — the visual allegory writers

The pack extends the allegory discussion into Shaun Tan's work:

- *The Red Tree*.
- *The Lost Thing*.
- *The Lorax* (returned to as Tan-like visual storytelling).

These are listed as extensions — visual texts that carry allegory without
depending on a traditional prose narrative. The teaching point is that allegory
does not have to be told in sentences; it can be carried in image and sequence.

Symbolism, in the Jim K framing, is a strategy lesson built around a concrete
object — the feather — that then opens into allegory, and allegory opens into the
picture books and picture-poems that can carry it for young readers. The reading
list is the spine; the writing process is the product.

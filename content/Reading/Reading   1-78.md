READING

Reading is always about meaning. Reading is thinking.

Most of the Reading time in the classroom must be spent by the children
READING.

Framework: Readers’ Workshop

« = Mini-lesson

¢ Reading - Group work/Independent reading

e Share

In the mini-lesson the teacher models through Think Aloud, demonstrating
the use of strategies, and how they make a difference to getting meaning
from print.

In reading time students can practise in small groups, pairs, and
independently.

In share time the class can talk and reflect on the use of strategies. It is a
learning time as well, A great time also for assessment.

This follows the Gradual Release of Responsibility Model (David Pearson).

e Teacher modelling - explaining strategies, thinking aloud about the

mental processes used to construct meaning.

e Guided practice. Children practise strategies in pairs or small
groups.

© Independent practise where the students apply strategies to their
own reading. If they are using the strategies independently then
comprehension will happen.

° Assessment. Handy to take notes during guided reading, reading
conferences, and share time. During these times children share
their thinking - metacognition.

The challenge is for the students to realise how using strategies helped
them as a reader.

Responding

We want the students’ responses to be about what they are thinking and
learning. The purpose of the responses is to enhance understanding. The
discussion that follows the responses furthers the understanding when they
consider what others think.

In order to make the responses relevant we have been using several aids.
Marking texts helps readers pay attention and remember what they read.
¢ Post-its. You can't write on the book, but you can record questions,
inferences, predictions etc. on a post-it at the relevant point. If
this disrupts children’s reading, they can read the text first, and
then use post-its after the reading or during a reread.

We can also use post-its in various ways:

- Use a post-it with an idea about the book. Use it as a seed
and write longer about that idea.

- Use a post-it that has evidence of your thinking. Write
about your idea based on this evidence.

- Record your questions and selected questions of others and
write about possible answers.

- Use a post-it that raises an issue you want to pursue across
other books. Write about that issue and develop that
writing as you read other books.

- Reread post-its and don't just leave them in the reading
notebook. They're there for a purpose - to extend thinking.

- Use post-its in mini-lessons E.g. Rereading post-its and
choosing one for an extended entry in response journal.

At first be prepared to go through a stack of post-its - the kids love
them. But covering the text with post-its is not the aim, so we need
to teach the effective use of post-its where

- the evidence is most meaningful

- they realise the purpose - to use for further thinking, or as
an aid for talking or writing.

e Literature Response Journals. We have been using these for
written and sketch responses,
Children must come to a Literature Discussion Group (Book
Club/Literature Circle) prepared. I ask them to bring their notes
with them. If the work hasn't been done I can't see the sense in
them being there.
Literature Response Journals can support the thinking about books.
It is where students can collect information, take notes, and write
reflections, Reading is thinking - this is where students can record

Teachers need to:
e Understand the cognitive processes used by proficient readers
e Provide explicit and in-depth instruction on these strategies
e Use authentic and challenging texts in the instruction i.e. high
quality literature and well written non-fiction
e Give feedback to the students during conferences, guided reading,
share time
e Demonstrate how one strategy build on another, even if dealing
with them separately.
Implications for our school:
° What books will we use to model the use of the strategy?
e Quality is better than quantity.
We need to carefully choose the books we are using to teach strategies.
Obviously books lend themselves to different or several strategies.
“No book is really worth reading at the age of ten which is not equally worth
reading at the age of fifty.” - C.S.Lewis
Certain genres also lend themselves to teaching different strategies.
Strategies are often taught one at a time. When we read we use many
strategies simultaneously. As strategies are introduced, students will also
use strategies already introduced. Students will come to use multiple
Strategies, integrating them, and knowing what strategy to use when.
If students are reading for understanding they should know at least 95% of
the words.
Strategies are a tool for facilitating and extending comprehension.
There must be time for independent reading so that students can practise
the strategies.
Readers use the most appropriate strategies and distinguish between
meaningful, relevant strategies and those that aren't. Because readers make
connections to better understand their reading, we need to limit connections
to those that deepen understanding.

READING WORKSHOP
What is a reading workshop?
A reading workshop is an organized set of language and literacy experiences (eg.
minilesson, individual-reading, conferring, and sharing) designed to help youngsters
become more effective readers. These experiences can and do occur simultaneously in
the classroom. The reading workshop includes:
% Independent Reading
> Individual students read a text, usually of their own choosing.
> Over time youngsters read and respond to a variety of texts independently.
> You provide minilessons, guide text selection, confer with individual
students, and facilitate a shared discussion to extend their understanding
and enjoyment.
% Guided Reading
> A small group of students work with the teacher on a particular reading
strategy.
> You select a text at a level that presents some challenge to youngsters,
introduce it, and provide supportive teaching that helps the group
understand targeted skills and strategies that will help them become better
readers.
% Literature Study
> A group of readers discuss various aspects of a text or a set of related
texts and sometimes work on projects to extend and share their learning,
> You show youngsters how to analyze and discuss texts with one another in
order to create shared meanings and understandings that are more refined
and deeper than they would discover on their own.
Why cail it a workshop?
A workshop suggests a group of people actively engaged in purposeful tasks. Activity is
at the heart of the workshop. Students learn by doing. They learn to read by reading
rather than simply hearing about reading.
A reading workshop is a laboratory where students are busily engaged in the kind of real
life reading that people do every. day of their lives.

Use the Goldilocks Strategy to Choose Books

Good readers spend time with easy, “just-right", and hard books, but mostly “just-right"

books,

Easy Books

Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, this book is probably an easy book for

you. EASY books help you to read more smoothly and are fun to read aloud and silently.

e Is it a favourite book you have read before?

2 Do you understand the story (text) very well?

e Do you know (can understand and read) just about every word?

» Can you read it easily and smoothly

Just-Right Books
Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, this book is probably a \JUST-
RIGHT" book for you. “Just-right” books help you learn the most because you can
figure out most of the words and you understand what's going on in the text.

e Is this an interesting book that you want to read?

e Are you familiar with the content, author, series, genre?

e Can you tell another person what is happening in the story and/or what
you're learning?

e Do you sometimes need to reread a part to understand it?

» Are there just a few words per page you don't know?

e When you read are most places smooth and some choppy?

Hard Books
Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, this book is probably a HARD book
for you, Spend a little time with it now and learn what you can, Perhaps someone
can read the book to you. give it another try on your own later.

e Are you interested in reading this book?

© Are you confused about what is happening in most of this book?

° Is it hard to understand even when you reread?

e Do you need lots of help to read this book?

CONFERRING

In Reading Conferences the aim is the same as for our Writing Conferences, where
we are teaching the writer, not the piece of writing. Here we are teaching the
reader ~ not the text. From our conferences we want to enhance the student as a
reader, not just read a particular book.

The more we talk with students about their reading, the more we will understand
them as readers. It enables us to evaluate:

- The effectiveness of our mini-lessons.

- Where further mini-lessons are necessary. E.g. particular reading strategies.
- Student abilities and interests for grouping.

- Book selection by student or teacher.

- Reading Logs. Review (e.g. genre/authors)

- Reader's Notebooks/Reading Journals

The evaluation is done through listening. The student should do most of the talking
and problem solving.

Notes to Address:

Topics for shared reading lessons should arise from what you observe children doing - 0

almost doing - in their own reading and writing. It's great to begin a lesson by saying

for example, “I want to share with you something I noticed Sophie and Jamal doing
yesterday.” Shared reading lessons might focus on helping children to:

~ develop clear expectations for reading as a meaningful activity;

— observe the teacher and classmates as models of skillful strategic readers;

— use a variety of strategies when stuck on a word:

— attend to individual letters, strings of letters, and specific words:

— find familiar and unfamiliar words within the text:

— build up a store of sight words;

— attend to specific features of words (i.e. beginning and ending sounds);

— find recognizable parts of words embedded in longer unknown words;

— identify rhyming words or homonyms; -

=- notice patterns in how words are constructed;

— attend to punctuation (i.e, talking marks around spoken words,

stops at periods, etc.);

- notice how punctuation effects the way in which something is read.

LESSON Format:

* During a first reading, the teacher reads the text to the children. The focus should
be on modeling good reading behaviors, getting the meaning, and enjoying what is read,
delighting in the language, rhythm, and story very much as one would in a read aloud.

* In subsequent readings of the same text, the teacher deliberately draws attention
to the print with a ruler, a pointer, or her finger. In doing so she demonstrates such
important early reading behaviors as left to right movement, sweeping back to the
left to begin the next line, the one to one correspondence between printed and spoken
words, and attention to punctuation.

ASSESSMENT:

Teachers will also find that shared reading sessions offer quick and easy opportunities

for assessing what an individual child, or group of children:

* knows about words and letters;

+ how skillfully they use guessing and checking strategies with new words;

+ how their sight word vocabulary is expanding- ~ rs

Following a shared reading lesson teachers will want to determine whether strategies

modeled are carrying over into children’s independent reading and writing.

Shared Reading with Informational Text
An example - generic lesson
Purpose The purpose of this shared reading is to read with all the students and
informational text on overhead transparency (OMT) and to learn about the
features of this genre by looking closely at the structure of the text selected.

about. The source of the text should be stated, for example, Time magazine,
PAL Test, a book on mammals. It is important to listen to several responses to
demonstrate that you value the students’ ideas and suggestions. This is modeling
what good readers do ~ orienting themselves to the book's contents. The mind
begins to think about prior knowledge of this topic and to have a mind-set about
what kind of language might be used.
Reading with The text is read as expressively as possible with commentary provided
about features of the text. This is the first reading.

Discussion After the first reading the teacher begins to focus on the features of the
text and to weave these into a discussion about the text. At the same time
the teacher will be checking for comprehension.

Stalls Direct instruction about the features of the text follows and some
Generalizations are drawn out about this form of text. At the end of the
Segment students will know about the features of an informational text,
The features should be listed on chart paper for future reference.

Activity To reinforce the knowledge learned about the particular text students’
work in pairs to compare and contrast an information text and narrative or
fiction text. They may write one feature that is similar to both texts and two
features that are different. The information is shared with the class and
made into a chart

Follow-up — Shared Writing: the teacher and the students write an informational text

together with the teacher modeling the stages required in collecting the
information and then Presenting it. This may take place over several writing
sessions.
Independent Writing: students follow the established procedure and

: write their own informational text.

What is GUIDED READING?

° It is an instructional and evaluative technique that supports and
encourages the development of strategies for independent
reading.

° It is a small group activity (from 4 to 6 children) and using an
unseen book.

° It's purpose is to help children develop effective reading skills
strategies and to use these strategies effectively to help them

. create meaning as they read.

s Each student's goal is to create meaning independently while
drawing support from other members of the group and the
teacher.

@ The teacher observes, monitors, affirms and responds to the
children needs. The teacher helps the students become aware of
strategies they can bring to the task for making meaning.

¢ The teacher sets purposes, invites predictions, asks questions
and remind students of previously used strategies that have
worked well for them. Risk taking is encouraged and
applauded.

e Teacher support is brief and aimed at returning children to the
task of mostly reading the text by themselves.

e Every text has different challenges and supports as the teacher
responds to the lead of the group of students who have been
grouped together for a particular purpose.

GUIDED SILENT READING involves:
, ° Children reading silently for a purpose.
e Discussion with the teacher and with other children about what has been discovered
during the silent reading.
SOME UNDERLYING PRINCIPLES of this approach:
° It can be used at any level.
° It places strong emphasis, through reader guidance, on developing children's ability to
gain meaning from what they read. -
2 Silent reading, which is the way we usually read, helps children to appreciate and
. interpret the writer's ideas. .
2 The teacher's role involves: ~
- helping children set goals and purposes for reading;
- deepening understanding through guiding children through a text by questioning,
discussing and sharing reactions to ideas, feelings and interpretations,

involve: .
. Preparation

During Reading Observation

It is the reader's job to set the purpose for reading. It is the teacher's job to show

children how to set their own purpose for reading.

PREPARATION . :

Are the children grouped for reading according to similar abilities, needs, interests?

Is the material within the children’s capacity to read independently?
Is it interesting, enjoyable and of manageable length?
Will it provide opportunity for practicing reading skills?

* Will it be suitable for discussion?

Are there opportunities to learn from it?
Does it fit the current classroom programme?
Does it relate to the children's experiences and interests? - Enjoyment, introduction,
extension, development of a theme, development of concepts, skills. .
Will it be likely to promote further interest in reading? ,
Is the rest of the class suitably involved in worthwhile reading tasks?
There should be few difficulties so that reading is successful, not a struggle.

INTRODUCTION - Grab their interesti :
e Relate the story to the children’s own experiences.
« Show a picture, object or illustration which will arouse children’s interests. -
e Draw attention to other books, stories or features of the class programme which
relate to this selection.
e Depending on how the material relates to the children's interests and experiences,
discuss concepts and ideas.
° Give the children oral/aural experience with vocabulary with which you think they may
have difficulty. Don't “teach” these words out of context.
e Alert children to any unusual features in the text such as speech bubbles, print
scattered over pages, print embedded in illustrations.
° Discuss the author, title, illustrations and perhaps read the first paragraph of the
. story. Invite children to predict what the story is about. (Be careful not to overdo
this and put them off reading it!)
SETTING THE PURPOSES FOR READING .
e Make sure that the children know why they are reading this particular story or text.
e When you have introduced the book, have the children ask who- what- where- how- why
questions so that they have their own purpose for reading.
e Make sure that the children are clear about what they are required to read.
e Make sure the children know what they are required to do when they finish silent
reading.

line of inquiry.
2. Ask children to find a section of the story which makes them laugh - feel sad - want to
say something to one of the characters - remember something which happened to them.

3. Find one or more stories which relate to the one read in some way. .

Setting questions before reading is a necessary part of establishing purposes for reading.
Good questioning during the discussion helps deepen the children's understanding, expands :

TYPES OF QUESTIONS |
Closed questions can relate to the children's prior knowledge and help to build background
understanding. They include

- Do you know what a ( ) is?

- Can you... .?

- Have you ever... .?

Open-ended questions are used to stimulate interest and imagination and to set the scene

- What do you think. . .? .

- Where might . . .?

- Who do you think . . .?

- I wonder what . . .? -
USING QUESTIONS |
It is important to allow children to ask their own questions so that they are reading for
their purposes, not to please the teacher.

One way to proceed is to introduce the book (This story is about... The characters are... It's
_ a fairy tale/true story/based on fact.) .

Then prompt the children by asking, "What do you think we may need to know/ find out

about as we read this story?”

Accept children's responses - don't expect ‘right’ answers.

Above all, let delight in reading come through in your introduction and response to books so

that children perceive reading as a pleasurable, informative and non-threatening activity,

Extending reading Strategies - Reciprocal Teaching is a research based
dialogue between group members to jointly construct meaning in text. .
Material at instructional level.
Groups of 6-8. Session approx. 20 min,
Uses 4 strategies which shift the responsibility to the reader.
i. Predicting
ii, Clarifying
ii. Question generating :
iv. Summarising
Modelling - teacher models Strategies, then becomes a group member
monitoring use of strategies.
Short factual articles, relevant to current learning topics are particularly
appropriate.
Predicting
Sets up purpose for the reading.
Provides opportunity to link new ideas to prior learning. The student is
anticipating what will come next in the text, based on appropriate prior
knowledge, and on the Structure and content of the text,
Predicting encourages students to think ahead actively.
As students read, predictions are confirmed or modified. Students
effectively monitor own understandings.
Clarifying
Uses context and word knowledge to overcome difficulties,
° unfamiliar vocabulary
* text which is structured or set out in an unfamiliar way
° new or difficult concepts
* when they lose track of the meaning

® using a dictionary or thesaurus to check words

INDEPENDENT READING ;
How To Begin
% Begin with a whole group meeting that includes a few book talks and a minilesson.
> Book talks allow you to present a new book, inform a group of youngsters
about books of particular interest, or provide opportunities for them to
learn, sharing just enough information to whet their appetites.
> A minilesson is a short focused lesson on something most of your readers
need help learning. Occasionally, the minilesson may need to be longer
because of the particular content you plan to address,
What Happens Next

‘ Students read independently for about forty minutes, usually a text they've
chosen, but sometimes one you've assigned.

% Youngsters read silently, without talking. The room is very quiet. You may wish to
play soft classical music.

% You circulate, talking quietly with individual students and taking notes that will
inform your teaching, enable you to create effective reading groups, and help you
document progress.

And...

% Periodically, you ask youngsters to respond in writing to what they've read. The
response is usually shared with the whole group, occasionally with small groups or a
partner.

> Asa group, youngsters evaluate their individual work and their ability to work
together.

Benefits

% Independent reading, unlike "free reading" or “sustained silent reading", is framed
by instruction; you actively teach during this time, rather than read your own book.

% Teaching happens:

> in the minilesson
> in the instructional conversations you have with individual readers
> in the sharing and feedback that takes place at the end
> Independent reading provides an opportunity for you to assess the reading
progress of individual students.

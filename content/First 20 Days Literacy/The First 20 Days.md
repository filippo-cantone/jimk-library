## READING

Reading is always about meaning. Reading is thinking.

Most of the Reading time in the classroom must be spent by the children
READING.

Framework: Readers’ Workshop

- Mini-lesson

- Reading – Group work/Independent reading

- Share

In the mini-lesson the teacher models through Think Aloud, demonstrating
the use of strategies, and how they make a difference to getting meaning
from print.

In reading time students can practise in small groups, pairs, and
independently.

In share time the class can talk and reflect on the use of strategies.
It is a learning time as well. A great time also for assessment.

This follows the Gradual Release of Responsibility Model (David
Pearson).

- Teacher modelling – explaining strategies, thinking aloud about the
  mental processes used to construct meaning.

- Guided practice. Children practise strategies in pairs or small
  groups.

- Independent practise where the students apply strategies to their own
  reading. If they are using the strategies independently then
  comprehension will happen.

- Assessment. Handy to take notes during guided reading, reading
  conferences, and share time. During these times children share their
  thinking – metacognition.

The challenge is for the students to realise how using strategies helped
them as a reader

## Introducing the Classroom Library

Students will explore and learn how to maintain their organised
classroom library.

o Our classroom library is organised in a specific way to help us select
a variety of books for reading.

o We are all responsible for taking care of our classroom library.

o Readers choose books in many different ways.

o Books can be easy, “just right”, or challenging for a reader.

o Readers should choose “just right” books most of the time.

**Choosing a Just Right Book**

**Easy Books:**

- You can read **all** the words.

- You understand **all** of the ideas.

- You have read it many times before and can read it fluently/smoothly.

- 

## Just Right Books:

- You can read **almost all** of the words.

- You understand **most** of the ideas, but there are some things that
  make you think.

- You’re interested in the book.

- You can read most of it fluently.

- 

## Challenging Books:

- There are many words that you don’t know or understand.

- It doesn’t make sense to you.

- It’s confusing.

You wouldn’t be able to talk to someone about what you’re reading.

## Use the Goldilocks Strategy to Choose Books

Good readers spend time with easy, ‘just right’, and hard books, but
mainly ‘just right’ books.

## Easy Books

*Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, this book is probably
as easy book for you. EASY books help you to read more smoothly and are
fun to read aloud and silently.*

- Is it a favourite book you have read before?

- Do you understand the story (text) very well?

- Do you know (can understand and read) just about every word?

- Can you read it easily and smoothly?

## Just-Right Books

*Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, the book is probably a
‘JUST-RIGHT’ book for you. ‘Just-right’ books help you learn the most
because you can figure out most of the words, and you understand what’s
going on in the text.*

- Is this an interesting book that you want to read?

- Are you familiar with the content, author, series, genre?

- Can you tell another person what is happening in the story and/or what
  you’re learning?

- Do you sometimes need to reread a part to understand it?

- Are there just a few words per page you don’t know?

- When you read are most places smooth and some choppy?

## Hard Books

*Ask yourself these questions. If you answer yes, the book is probably a
HARD book for you. Spend a little time with it now and learn what you
can. Perhaps someone can read the book to you. Give it another on your
own later.*

- Are you interested in reading this book?

- Are you confused about what is happening in most of this book?

- Is it hard to understand even when you reread?

- Do you need lots of help to read this book?

*Ref. Reading Essentials – R.Routman*

**READING RESPONSE JOURNAL**

**Reading Response Possibilities**

**Predictions**

From the front cover, what do you think will happen in the book – use
title and illustration. Include background knowledge you have of the
author.

At the end of each chapter, predict what will happen next. Later, make
an entry as to whether you were right.

How do you think the story will end?

How do you think the character/s will change during the story? Why?

How do you think the conflict will be resolved?

**Connections**

**Text-to-Text**

How is this book similar to another you have read by this author or
another author?

Compare a character in your book to a character in another book you have
read.

## Text-to-Self

Explain how the book reminds you of yourself, people you know, or of
something that happened in your life.

Further character thoughts:

What advice would you give a character in this book? Why?

Which character would you most like to be? Why?

If you were a character in this book, how would it affect the plot?

Describe a character’s personality trait that you’d like to possess. Why
do you like this trait?

## Text-to-World

Explain the connection you have made with your general knowledge.

## Questioning

What you would like to ask the author.  How do you think he or she would
respond?

## Synthesising/ Summarising

Summarise the chapter you just read.

 Do you agree with the point the author is making?  Why?

Have you changed after reading this book? How?

**Story Elements**

**Setting**

Describe in detail the setting of your book and how it fits into the
story.

How would a different setting affect the story?

## Characters

Describe how the main character/s changed during the story.

Select a Character graphic organiser to help.

Describe a character from the book. If you like drawing, sketch the
character as well.

Compare two characters in the book to each other by describing their
similarities and their differences.

Compare a character in your book to a character in another book you have
read.

Write a diary entry in the voice of a character in your book.

## Plot

Write about your favourite part of the story and why it was important.

Did you like the way the story ended? Do you think there was more to
tell? Does it lend itself to you writing a sequel?

## Themes

Themes: What was the theme/s of the story? What are your thoughts and
feelings about the theme?

Select a Theme Chart to assist.

Describe places where the author gives good descriptions of the
characters, setting, problem, or solution.

Did you like the ending of the book? How would you have liked it to end?
Rewrite a new ending for the book.

## Illustrations

Describe what you notice about the illustration. What purpose do they
have? Do they add to the story?

## Reading like a Writer

Describe the author’s craft: What was good about the author’s writing?
 What things might you try to do in your own writing that you learnt
from this author?

Refer to the Writer’s Craft sheet.

Describe how the author makes you feel through their writing.

Can you name the type of lead the author used? Was it effective in
wanting you to read on?

Find examples of figurative language in the text. Write them down.

Is there a chapter that would lend itself to writing a Found Poem?

## Evaluation

Did you enjoy the book? Why or why not?

Was the book hard or easy to read? Why?

What didn’t you understand in the text?

Would you like to read more books by this author? Why or why not?

What did you learn about the time in which the story took place? (If
appropriate)

Write about an important lesson that was learnt in the story.

Describe parts of the book that puzzled you or made you ask questions.

Would you recommend the book to another reader? Explain why or why not.

Describe what you would change about the book if you could rewrite it.

Explain what you want to remember about this book and why.

Make a list of things you don’t understand, find confusing, or have
questions about.

## READING RESPONSE JOURNAL

The purpose of this journal is for you to record your developing
responses to the book. This will help you to understand the novel. It is
YOUR record of YOUR thoughts, and therefore different from anyone
else’s. Suggestions:

- Tell about the story:

> \- Where and when does it take place?
>
> \- Would the story be different in another time or place?

- How do you think the story might develop?

- Reflect on personal reactions.

\- Compare what has happened with your own experiences.

> \- Write about things that have happened to you when you’re reminded
> of by events in the book.

- Describe the characters:

> \- What are your reactions to the characters and what they do?
>
> \- Give your opinion of a character. What kind of person is the
> character? How do you know?
>
> \- Do any of the characters remind you of characters in other stories?
>
> \- Is there a character who changes as the story progresses? How?
>
> \- If you were one of the characters, what would you do and why?

- Comment on how the author is telling the story:

> \- How has the author drawn you into the story?
>
> \- How does the author make you want to keep reading the book?
>
> \- Make notes on words, phrases, lines, passages that you like or
> think are memorable.
>
> \- Does the story convey a particular mood or feeling? What is that
> mood? How does the author create this?

- Think of a different ending to the story. How would other parts of the
  story have to change to fit this ending?

- Is this story like any other you have read? How?

- Is there anything that puzzles you? What questions do you have?

- What is your overall reaction to this book?

> **RESPONDING TO LITERATURE**

Teacher modelling of good questioning techniques is important. Below are
some suggested questions designed to support good conversation and help
structure written responses. Ultimately, the goal is to have children
generate their own questions. These questions are to provide a good
start.

**Before Beginning the Book.**

- How do the blurb, pictures on the cover, comments by book reviewers,
  dedications, connect to the story.

- How do your previous experiences with the author or genre help to
  support early reading?

- How will the way the text is structured help you to plan your reading?

## Questions to use Near the Beginning of the Book

- How does the author get you interested in the beginning chapters of
  the book?

- How does the author introduce you to the setting? (i.e. time and
  place)? Could the story have happened in a different place? Do I have
  enough background information to really understand this place and its
  importance in the story? Do I need to expand my knowledge? – do a
  quick read for more information.

- How does the author introduce you to the characters? What have you
  found out about the characters so far?

- Do you feel like you already know what the main problem is? Tell what
  it is. How did you know?

- What does the author use to get the story started? What do you think
  will happen now?

- Is this author’s style and type of writing familiar? What other books
  have I read that are like this?

## Questions to Think About as the Story Develops

- What are you noticing about the author’s craft? Is there language,
  images that the author seems to keep referring to? Why?

- Were your early predictions, hunches, accurate? Explain why or why
  not.

- What are the most important events that have happened in the story so
  far?

- How is time moving in the story? Quickly, slowly? Are the “times”
  changing? How is the time affecting the place? The characters?

- How do you think the author will resolve the problem? How will the
  author end the story?

- What words would you use to describe the main character? How do you
  learn about him or her? Is it through what he or she does, what he or
  she says (and how it’s said) or does the author just tell you? Give
  examples from the story that made you think this way.

- Have any of the characters changed since the beginning of the book? In
  what ways? What has changed them? Did the change seem believable?

- Look at the secondary characters in the book. What’s their story? What
  are those characters’ roles in the life of the main character?

- Think about the characters in the story. Do they remind you of other
  characters you have met in other stories? Who do they remind you of?
  Why is this character important in the story? Could we tell the story
  without him/her?

- Does this character remind you of yourself in any way? In what way?

- If you could talk to one of the characters, what would you tell him or
  her?

- What is the main problem in the story? Is there a subplot? How do they
  affect each other?

- What questions are lingering in your mind throughout the book?

- Has the meaning of the title become clear?

## Questions to use at the End of the Book

- Did the story end the way you expected it to, or was it a surprise?
  Why?

- What if the story ended a different way? How else could it have ended?
  Would anything else in the story have to change to fit your new
  ending?

- Did this story remind you of any other story you have read or watched?
  What do you think is the “big idea” in this story? What makes you
  think that?

- Why do you think the author wrote this book? What did he or she want
  you to think about? What did you notice about his or her style of
  writing?

- Did you have any strong feelings as you read this story? What were
  they, and why do you think the book made you feel that way?

- Is there anything that’s happened in your own life that reminds you of
  what happened to the character in this book?

- Can you imagine what will happen to the character after the story
  ended?

- If this character went to our school, do you think you could be
  friends with him or her?

- Compare this character to one from another book. What do you think
  would happen if they met?

- Can you imagine how this story would be different if it were told by
  one of the other characters?

- Compare this book to others you’ve read by this author. What’s similar
  about them? What is unique about each?

- Are there “loaded” statements that the author has laid down for your
  personal interpretation? Try to figure out what they really mean in
  the sense of the real world.

- What is the story/piece really saying about the world?

- What is the author saying about fairness, justice? Put yourself on the
  “other side of the fence.” Do you feel a sense of fairness?

- How would other people (races, gender, socio-economic, handicapped)
  feel about the ideas in the story?

- Where were the places you laughed in the story? Was it really funny in
  the life of another person?

## TELL ME

This list of literature questions is intended for the teacher’s own
convenience. ‘It is provided only as an aid to memory.’

Aiden Chambers: Tell Me – Children, Reading and Talk

• Was there anything you liked about this book?

• Was there anything you disliked about this book?

• Was there anything that puzzled you?

• Were there any patterns-any connections- that you noticed?

THE BASIC QUESTIONS

*Was there anything you liked about this book?*

• What especially caught your attention?

• What would you have liked more of?

*Was there anything you disliked about this book?*

• Were there parts that bored you?

• Did you skip parts? Which ones?

• If you gave up, where did you stop and what stopped you?

*Was there anything that puzzled you?*

• Was there anything you thought strange?

• Was there anything that you’d never found in a book before?

• Was there anything that took you completely by surprise?

• Did you notice any apparent inconsistencies?

*Were there any patterns-any connections- that you noticed*?

THE GENERAL QUESTIONS

*When you first saw this book, even before you read it, what kind of
book did you think it was going to be?*

• What made you think this?

• Now you’ve read it, is it as you expected?

*Have you read other books like it?*

• How is this one the same?

• How is it different ?

*\\*

*Have you read this book before? \[If so:\] Was it different this time?*

• Did you notice anything this time you didn’t notice the first time?

• Did you enjoy it more or less?

• Because of what happened to you when reading it again, would you
recommend other people to read it more than once, or isn’t it worth it?

*While you were reading, or now when you think about it, were there
words or*

*phrases or other things to do with the language that you liked? Or
didn’t like?*

• You know how, when people speak, they often use some words or phrases
or talk in away that you recognise as theirs: are some words or phrases
used like that in this book?

• Have you noticed anything special about the way language is used in
this book?

*If the writer asked you what could be improved in the book, what would
you say?*

• \[Alternatively\] If you had written this book, how would you have
made it better?

*Has anything that happens in the book ever happened to you?*

• In what ways was it the same or different for you?

• Which parts in the book seem to you to be most true to life?

• Did the book make you think differently about your own similar
experience?

*When you were reading, did you ‘see’ the story happening in your
imagination?*

• Which details –which passages- helped you ‘see’ it best?

• Which passages stay in your mind most vividly?

*How many different stories \[kinds of story\] can you find in this
story? Was this a book you read quickly, or slowly? In one go, or in
separate sessions*?

• Would you like to read it again?

*What will you tell your friends about this book?*

• What won’t you tell them because it might spoil the book for them? Or
might mislead them about what it is like?

• Do you know people who you think would especially like it?

• What would you suggest I tell other people about it that will help
them decide whether they want to read it or not? Older than you?
Younger?

• How should I give it to them? For example, should I read it aloud or
tell them about it and let them read it for themselves?

• Is it a good thing to talk about it after we’ve all read it?

*We’ve listened to each other’s thoughts and heard all sorts of things
that each of us has noticed. Are you surprised by anything someone else
said?*

• Has anyone said anything that has changed your mind in any way about
this book? Or helped you understand it better?

• Tell me about the things people said that struck you the most.

*When you think about the book now, after all we’ve said, what is the
most*

*important thing about it for you?*

*Does anyone know anything about the writer? Or about how the story came
to be written? Or where? Or when? Would you like to find out?*

THE SPECIAL QUESTIONS

*How long did it take the story to happen?*

• Did we find out about the story in the order in which the events
happened?

When you talk about things that happen to you, do you always tell your
story in the order in which they happened? Or are there sometimes
reasons why you don’t? What are the reasons?

*Are there parts of the story that took a long time to happen but were
told about quickly or in a few words? And are there parts that happened
very quickly but took a lot of space to tell about?*

• Were there parts that took the same time to tell as they would have
taken to happen?

*Where did the story happen?*

• Did it matter where it was set? Could it just as well been set
anywhere? Or could it have been better set somewhere else?

• Did you think about the place as you were reading?

• Are there passages in the book that are especially about the place
that the story is set? What did you like, or dislike, about them?

• Was the setting interesting in itself? Would you like to know more
about it?

*Which character interested you the most?*

• Is that character the most important in the story/ Or is it really
about someone else?

• Which character(s) didn’t you like?

• Did any of the characters remind you of people you know?

• Or remind you of characters in other books?

*Who was telling – narrating – the story? Do we know? And how do we
know?*

*Is the story told in the first person (and if so, who is this person)?
Or the third person? By someone we know about in the story or by someone
we know or don’t know about outside the story?*

• What does the person telling the story – the narrator – think or feel
about the characters? Does s/he like or dislike them? How do you know?

• Does the narrator approve or disapprove of the things that happen and
that the characters do? Do you approve or disapprove of them?

*Think of yourself as a spectator. With whose eyes did you see the
story? Did you only se what one character in the story saw, or did you
see things sometimes as one character saw them, and sometimes as another
and so on?*

• Were you as it were, inside the head of one of the characters, only
knowing what s/he knew, or did the story take you inside a number of
characters?

*Did we ever get to know what the characters were thinking about? Were
we ever told what they were feeling? Or was the story told all the time
from outside the characters, watching what they did and hearing what
they said, but never knowing what they were thinking and feeling?*

• When you were reading the story, did you feel it was happening now? Or
did you feel it was happening in the past and being remembered? Can you
tell me anything in the writing that made you feel like that?

• Did you feel as if everything was happening to you, as if you were one

**Zlata’s Diary Zlata Filipovic**

**A Child’s Life in Sarajevo**

Sarajevo witnessed a savage war between Bosnians and Serbs. Although I
haven’t been to Sarajevo, I know the countryside as I drove down the
Dalmatian coast when the country was Yugoslavia.

I wonder if Zlata lived through this and recorded her diary similar to
Anne Frank’s diary during World War 11?

Zlata’s diary starts on Monday, 2 September 1991. She loves the
countryside, *“enjoying* *and feeling the beauty of nature.”* Living in
the country I can really relate to her feelings. Suddenly this changes
as war starts, and Zlata struggles to understand the reasons. The war is
in Dubrovnik, *“the most beautiful town in the world.”* I would have to
agree that Dubrovnik is/was one of the most beautiful cities I had ever
seen, and my thoughts are of stunning orange-tiled roofs, windows
underlined with geraniums, and steep steps lined by old
deeply-charactered buildings. I also know what happened to Dubrovnik
during the war and feel outraged. I can certainly empathise with Zlata.

Zlata speaks to her diary as Anne Frank spoke to hers – the difference
being that Anne gave her diary a name – Kitty. Zlata writes, “I took you
with me, dear Diary, but I didn’t write anything. You’re not cross with
me, are you?”

I have just read where Zlata refers to Anne Frank’s diary and decides to
give her diary a name – Mimmy.

We learn Zlata’s thoughts and feelings through her diary entries.

*“The people must be the ones to win, not the war, because war has
nothing to do with humanity. War is something inhuman…It destroys,
kills, burns, separates, brings unhappiness.”*

Again I think of Anne Frank. She hid in an attic with her family;
Zlata’s family goes to the cellar. *“The cellar is ugly, dark, smelly.”*

I can see Zlata changing; from an eleven-year-old dreaming of getting
Michael Jackson’s autograph and joining Madonna’s fan club, to a girl
facing war and the death of friends.

*“AND NINA IS DEAD. A piece of shrapnel lodged in her brain and she
died. She was such a sweet, nice little girl. We went to kindergarten
together, and we used to play together in the park. Is it possible I’ll
never see Nina again? Nina, an innocent eleven-year-old little girl –
the victim of a stupid war. I feel sad. I cry and wonder why? She didn’t
do anything. A disgusting war has destroyed a young child’s life. Nina,
I’ll always remember you as a wonderful little girl.”*

Zlata’s self-centredness changes as this innocent girl grows up too
quickly.

*Saturday, 23 May 1992.*

*Dear Mimmy,*

*I’m not writing to you about me anymore. I’m writing to you about war,
death, injuries, shells, sadness and sorrow.*

*“The forces of war don’t know anything about love and the desire to
save something. They just know how to destroy, burn, take things away.”*

*“That’s my life! The life of an innocent eleven-year-old schoolgirl!! A
schoolgirl without a school, without the fun and excitement of school. A
child without games, without friends, without the sun, without birds,
without nature, without fruit, without chocolate or sweets, with just a
little powdered milk. In short, a child without a childhood. A wartime
child.”*

Listen to the student read aloud to determine fluency and accuracy.

Discuss written responses in his/her reader response journal.

## Assessment

**Reading conferences** are used for both assessment and instruction.

They are brief and focused. Systematically cover the class by keeping a
record. E.g. If there are 25 in the class, then five per day during
independent reading time ensures each student has a Reading Conference
once per week. Emergent and early readers will need evaluation
conferences where running records are taken. These will indicate who
needs instruction in self-monitoring and self-correction. The focus for
fluent readers is on comprehension,

## Anecdotal Notes

Take notes, whenever possible, during the time spent with your students.
These notes are shared and discussed with your students to help them
develop a clear understanding of their patterns of learning and growth.

- **Let’s discuss your strengths and what you need to work on.**

## I know I Understand what I am Reading when I can:

- Get the gist of the text even if I can’t read or interpret every word
  and idea.

- Retell the text orally or in writing so that another person
  understands my re-creation.

- Connect or relate what I’m reading to what I already know, have read,
  or experienced.

- Reread a portion of text and clear up confusion.

- Use my knowledge of the subject or author to think and evaluate more
  deeply and critically – for example, to question, agree, or disagree
  with the text.

- Take clear notes that demonstrate my insights and learning.

- Use my notes to help me as I read or reread.

- Summarise or paraphrase what I’ve read.

- Use what I’ve read to think about other contexts and texts.

- Recognise gaps in my understanding and attempt to understand better by
  rereading, listing or highlighting key words or phrases. Figuring out
  the meaning of unfamiliar words, writing notes to myself, asking
  pertinent questions, conversing with others.

- Consider multiple meanings.

- Recognise and use the characteristics of the genre to help me
  comprehend.

- Consider and understand comments of others

- Make new meaning.

## Post-its

- Post-its. You can’t write on the book, but you can record questions,
  inferences, predictions etc. on a post-it at the relevant point. If
  this disrupts children’s reading, they can read the text first, and
  then use post-its after the reading or during a reread.

> We can also use post-its in various ways:

- Use a post-it with an idea about the book. Use it as a seed and write
  longer about that idea.

- Use a post-it that has evidence of your thinking. Write about your
  idea based on this evidence.

- Record your questions and selected questions of others and write about
  possible answers.

- Use a post-it that raises an issue you want to pursue across other
  books. Write about that issue and develop that writing as you read
  other books.

- Reread post-its and don’t just leave them in the reading notebook.
  They’re there for a purpose – to extend thinking.

- Use post-its in mini-lessons E.g. Rereading post-its and choosing one
  for an extended entry is response journal.

At first be prepared to go through a stack of post-its – the kids love
them. But covering the text with post-its is not the aim, so we need to
teach the effective use of post-its where

- the evidence is most meaningful

- they realise the purpose – to use for further thinking, or as an aid
  for talking or writing.

## Learning Structures

Partner Talk:

Turn to partner and discuss issue or question.

Turn and Talk (Partner Talk)

Teacher and students model and practise “Turn and Talk” so they know the
classroom’s routine for speaking and listening about books.

“Thinking and Talking With a Partner”

- Partners use quiet voices

- Partners take turns talking

- Partners listen to one another

- Partners respect each other’s thinking

Think, Pair, Share:

Students think individually about a question or issue – then discuss
thoughts with partner.

Pairs then share their thinking with another pair or class.

Think, Pair, Write:

Students think individually before discussing thoughts with partner.

Students then write what they are thinking. Writing might be shared with
another pair or class.

Group Brainstorming:

Groups of four list as many ideas as they can about a question or topic.
One group member records.

Whole Class Discussion: Can benefit from use of above practices.

## READ ALOUD TO CHILDREN

- Read loud at least every day.

- Try to set aside at least one scheduled time each day for a story.

- Remember the art of listening is an acquired skill. It must be taught
  and cultivated gradually. Learners need many demonstrations of how
  texts are constructed, used and enjoyed.

- Picture can be read to all age groups.

Make sure the children can see the pictures easily.

- Position yourself where both you and the children are comfortable.

- Adjust your pace to fit the story. Reading aloud is a skill, which,
  when done with expression and enjoyment, captures the children’s
  interest.

- Preview the book – so you know what it is about, and can decide if it
  is suitable for the children, or will meet the particular needs you
  have decided to use in your program.

- Don’t continue reading the book if it becomes obvious it is a poor
  choice. Admit the mistake and choose another.

- Vary the genre, subject matter, style of writing and length of the
  material so children can see that all reading is valued, whether it is
  an ‘easy’ read or not.

- If modelling a strategy, tell the children what that strategy is. E.g.
  if questioning, then there will be be questions before, during, and
  after the reading. If visualising, you might tell the students what
  you are picturing in your mind, a or ask them for their pictures.

- Allow time for discussion after reading. Model questions on what you
  would like children to focus on in their own reading.

- Avoid using the Read Aloud as a ‘reward’. It is an important part of
  the normal, daily Balance Literacy program.

## SHARED READING

*“Shared reading is a collaborative learning activity … that emulates
and builds from the child’s experience with bedtime stories. In early
childhood classrooms, it typically involves a teacher and a large group
of children sitting closely together to read and reading in unison
carefully selected enlarged texts. Shared reading ca n also be done
effectively with smaller groups.”* Brenda Parkes

Purposes:

- enjoyable reading experiences

- teaching systematically and explicitly how to be readers and writers

Resources:

- stories

- informational text

- poems

- nursery rhymes

- songs/chants

Strategies:

Through shared reading we teach the reading strategies that the students
will use independently in guided reading and independent reading.

Prior knowledge.

Connections – as children gain more confidence with reading they make
important connections.

- text-to-self

- text-to-text

- text-to-world

Visualising

Inferring

Questioning

Determining important ideas

Synthesising

- summarising

- retelling (See Reading Strategies handouts)

Each classroom has its own permanent collection of big books. The
children need ready access to these books to support the teaching of the
reading strategies. Where possible, small book copies could be available
for groups work.

Shared reading is used to demonstrate how the three language-based
souces of information – semantics, syntax, and graphophonics – work.

Meaning – because shared reading uses a complete text, the children can
use the text to *‘cross-check information from multiple sources and to
monitor that what he or she is reading makes sense.’* (Parkes, B *Read
It Again)*

**Meaning** making occurs when students:

- use the cover of the book to predict what the story might be about,
  using the title and illustrations

- make connections

- use the illustrations to notice details

- use prediction throughout the book

- use visualising to discuss settings, characters, and events.

**Structure** (Syntax) The children’s knowledge of grammar is used when
they predict what words come next.

**Visual** (Graphophonics) Shared reading is important here as the
children are taught letter-sound correspondences in words they already
know. Once the children are familiar with a book, it can be used for
explicit and implicit graphophonic work.

Shared reading exposes the class to high frequency words.

Through shared reading the teacher:

- demonstrates how enjoyable reading is

- models how readers think and what they do

- models fluent, phrased reading

- teaches book and print conventions

- actively involves the children in reading

- teaches reading skills and strategies

- introduces book talk

- teaches the integration of meaning, structure and visual sources

- demonstrates reading for different purposes

## From the Author (Teacher background information)

Joseph's Grandpa can fix anything. He can turn a worn out blanket into a
wonderful jacket, a vest into a Sabbath tie, a ragged handkerchief into
a brand-new button, but when the button is lost, what's to be done? Even
Grandpa can't make something from nothing.

This story is adapted from an old Jewish folksong about a tailor who
makes his old coat into a jacket, vest, tie, and finally a button. When
the button is lost, he makes up a song about it all. I thought, "That's
exactly what I do! Anything that happens can be turned into a story." So
I decided to make the song into a picture book. But a picture book is
for children and there wasn't any child in the song. "I can fix that." I
said. "I'll just make the tailor into a Grandpa. Then he can make all
these things for his grandson, Joseph. But, would the first thing he
made be a coat? No. It would be a blanket."

BEHIND THE SCENES

If you read the words without the pictures, they could take place
anywhere, anytime. It was only when I started doing the drawings, that I
decided to set it back in the olden days, in a Jewish village like the
one the song came from. The book then took on a whole new dimension for
me. It became a way of remembering the world of my Grandparents.

The people in the streets and marketplace are actual portraits of people
who lived in the shtetl.

My Great Grandfather was a poor, Jewish tailor.

While I was drawing, I kept being bothered by the fact that the button
was lost. The kid in me, didn't care if it was all turned into a story.
That kid wanted to know where the button was. This led me to add the
picture story of the mice who live beneath the floorboards, so that they
could find the button.

Joseph's little sister looks like my daughter, Ingrid did when she was a
baby. Grandpa made her a wonderful blanket too. When Joseph loses his
button she offers him her dolly but not her special blanket.

## Obituary/Phoebe Gilman

by Susan Lawrence October 2002

Author and illustrator Phoebe Gilman died Aug. 29 of breast cancer at
the age of 62 in Toronto’s Princess Margaret Hospital.

Phoebe Gilman was best known among Canada’s picture-book set as the
creator of the popular Jillian Jiggs books. Gilman’s rhyming couplets
tripped rhythmically off the tongue: “Jillian, Jillian, Jillian Jiggs!
It looks like your room has been lived in by pigs!” wails the mother of
scatterbrained Jillian in frustration. But Gilman could and did serve up
more serious fare to young readers as well.  
Born in 1940, she grew up in the Bronx, where as a child she used to
love copying comic book illustrations. She studied art in New York City
and in Israel (living there for four years as an artist) before settling
in 1972 in Toronto, where she taught at the Ontario College of Art and
Design for 15 years. She was in her forties when she discovered the work
that became her passion: writing and illustrating picture books. Her
first book, The Balloon Tree, was published in 1984 by Scholastic
Canada. It was followed by Jillian Jiggs, which grew to such popularity
that it spawned a series of books about the messy madcap character. This
fall sees the publication of the last of the series, Jillian Jiggs and
the Great Big Snow.  
One of her more serious books – and possibly one of her best – was the
1992 picture book Something from Nothing, which won the Ruth Schwartz
Children’s Book Award. Here, Gilman reached back to the shtetls (Jewish
ghettos) of her Eastern European ancestors to retell a traditional
folktale. In it, a resourceful tailor takes his grandson Joseph’s
favourite blanket when it becomes too worn, and turns it into a jacket.
When the jacket becomes too worn, the grandfather turns it into a vest.
And so on, until Joseph is left with nothing – except for the insightful
realization that he has “just enough material here to make … a story.”
It’s a masterful book; every page turn creates a suspense-filled pause
at just the right moment.  
With The Gypsy Princess, published in 1995, Gilman took her illustration
work to its height, working in oil and egg tempura on watercolour paper
in a layered technique that made the colours luminous. The book’s theme
is a simple but strong one: self-knowledge is more precious than a crown
of gold.  
Gilman was an energetic and prolific author; her imaginative ideas kept
flowing almost until her untimely death. She had recently written a new
manuscript called The Blue Hippopotamus, but was too weak to do the
illustrations. In bed, with oxygen tubes helping her breathe, she
managed nonetheless to read voraciously and to write a brief family
memoir of her childhood for her grandchildren, whom she adored.   
Gilman leaves her husband, three grown children, and two grandchildren.

## THINK ALOUD

Think Alouds are central to comprehension instruction. We show students
our thinking as we read aloud. This can often be recorded by coding the
text. The students can see our thinking when we read, our connections,
predictions, questions, visualising, and inferences.

“As I read I’m going to tell you what I’m thinking, because good readers
think as they read.”

Think Aloud takes place during Read Aloud or Shared Reading.

During shared reading the language of a think Aloud may include

- I’m thinking that…

- From the title and the cover illustrations, I’m going to predict that…

- That made me think about…

- And now I’m thinking…

- The illustrations help me to …

- I have a picture in my mind…

- I’m going to read that again, I’m a little confused.

- In this next part…

- What was going through my mind was…

- Let me rethink this.

- One of the good things that readers do is always think…

- I’m predicting that…

- Good readers are always thinking about their reading.

- I’m connecting this to when (such and such) happened to me.

- I think this character is…

  - Regie Routman in *Reading Essentials*

The book selection is critical as the strategies used must be genuine.
In shared reading the teacher and students share strategy use to enhance
understanding and construct meaning - thinking through the text
together.

- Model Think Aloud during Read Aloud/Shared Reading.

> Picture books are great to use because they’re shorter, but novels can
> also be used.

- Name strategies for students.

- Students can imitate model during stops – talk, sketch to stretch,
  think, record.

- Think, pair, share – use evidence from text.

- Group work practising strategies.

- Students use a copy of text with post-its.

- Give independent time to allow students to individually practise
  strategies.

Nonfiction

Read Alouds include nonfiction.

This helps the reader

- hear how good nonfiction sounds

- become immersed in high quality nonfiction

- learn about visual information

- see how writing differs in nonfiction genres

- generate common understanding about content

- learn comprehension strategies

Using Think Alouds with nonfiction

“A think-aloud of reading is creating a record, either through writing
or talking aloud, of the strategic decision-making and interpretive
processes of going through a text, reporting everything the reader is
aware of noticing, doing, seeing, feeling, asking, and understanding as
she reads.

**A think-aloud involves talking about the reading strategies you are
using and the content of the piece you are reading.”** - J.Wilhelm

We don’t always report everything, but we are aware of the use of
multiple strategies when reading.

With nonfiction, Think Alouds help

- determine whether the book will be suitable to meet the purpose

- activate prior knowledge

- assist making connections

- locate features needed to find information E..g. table of contents,
  index

- understand new words

- examine visual information and how this helps the reader – photos and
  captions, diagrams, flow charts, timelines, cross sections etc.

- consider writing style – noticing literary devices used with
  particular genres

- inferring

- questioning before, during, and after reading

- visualising, and using this as a basis for using other strategies e.g.
  predicting

- summarising and synthesising information, recognising differences
  between these

- reader to use fix-up strategies when meaning is lost

## The Paperboy Dav Pilkey

The mornings of the paperboy are still dark and they are always cold
even in the summer.

And on these cold mornings the paperboy’s bed is still warm

and it is always hard to get out – even for his dog …

but they do …

And softly they step down the quiet hall past the door where the
paperboy’s father and mother are sleeping.

Past the door where his sister is asleep.

And down to the kitchen where they eat from their bowls.

And out to the garage where they quickly fold their papers, snapping on
green rubber bands and placing them in a large red bag.

It’s hard to ride a bike when you are loaded down with newspapers. But
the paperboy has learnt how to do this, and he is good at it.

The paperboy knows his route by heart, so he doesn’t ever think about
which house to pedal to.

Instead, he is thinking about other things.

Big Things.

And small things.

And sometimes he is thinking about nothing at all.

His dog, too, knows this route by heart.

He knows which trees are for sniffing.

He knows which birdbaths are for drinking, which squirrels are for
chasing, and which cats are for growling at.

All the world is asleep except for the paperboy and his dog. And this is
the time when they are the happiest.

But little by little by little the world around them wakes up.

The stars and the moon fade away and the skies become orange and pink.

And when the paperboy has delivered his last newspaper, he and his dog
race home.

And his empty red bag flaps behind him in the cold morning air.

Soon they are back home. It is still dark inside, but the sounds of
morning are all around. His father and mother are awake and talking
softly in their bed,

and his sister is downstairs watching Saturday morning cartoons.

And back inside his own room the paperboy pulls down his shade and
crawls back into his bed, which is still warm.

And while all of the world is waking up, the paperboy is going back to
sleep and his dog is sleeping, too. Their work is done …

… and now is the time for dreaming.

## GUIDED READING - Discussion

Discussion points:

Cognitive approach. Reinforce that Guided Reading is the students
talking, thinking and reading their way through a text.

As soon as possible Guided Reading should be Guided Silent Reading.

In G/R the students are using the strategies we have taught them in
Shared Reading.

The students read the text independently on the first reading (unseen
text).

The book for Guided Reading should have plenty to support the reading,
with several challenges.

Lesson Format:

Introduction.

Reading

Revisiting

Responding

Introduction:

Tuning in – schema. i.e. background knowledge. Preparation. Could be
prediction/questions. Determine if specific support might be necessary
(best guess) –

MSV – meaning, structure, visual.

Reading:

\- Section at a time – applying a DRTA-like method we use in upper
grades.

With all levels any oral reading must have a purpose. We might ask a
student to read from the text to illustrate a view. At the emergent
level it may be to assess the integration of MSV.

- Independently if managing challenges.

Revisiting:

\- If the reading has been done in sections at the early and emergent
levels, the students can now read the whole text.

- Questioning, discussing, reactions, feelings.

- Teaching if required:

Structure

Vocabulary

Format

Style

Language features

Responding:

Could be more reading.

The book could provide a springboard for writing, drama etc.

## LITERATURE DISCUSSION GROUPS

Book discussion groups can be organised in several ways.

Readers coming together to discuss the same book. Students bring their
Reading Response Journals with them, in which they’ve made notes during
the reading to assist them with their thinking, and discussion within
the group.

Readers coming together to discuss various books they’ve read.

Similar to above, but discussing books around a theme.

Whole class broken into groups to discuss the same book/theme.

Author Study.

Lists of generic book discussion questions are included to assist
discussion.

Handouts are included for the students which can be pasted into their
Reader’s Journal.

Post-its can be available for students to use during their reading.

As the students progress through the school, they will use the reading
strategies taught more automatically and in a more sophisticated way
when involved in Literature Discussion Groups.

**BOOK DISCUSSION QUESTIONS**

**Setting**

What was unique about the setting of the book, and how did it enhance or
take away from the story?

## Theme

What specific themes did the author emphasise throughout the book?

What do you think the author is trying to get across to the reader?

## Characters

Do the characters seem real and believable? Are they convincing?

Can you relate to their predicaments?

To what extent do they remind you of yourself, or someone you know?

How do the characters change or evolve throughout the story?

What events trigger such changes?

Which characters do you particularly admire or dislike?

What are their primary characteristics?

Who in this book would you like to meet? What would you ask- or say?

What motivates a particular character’s actions? Do you think those

actions are justified?

If you could insert yourself as a character in the book, what role would
you play? Would you be a new character or take the place of an existing
one?

## Plot

Is the plot well-developed? Is it believable? Do the plot events unfold
naturally?

Is the story plot or character driven? In other words, do events unfold
quickly? Or is more time spent developing characters’ inner lives?

Story beginning. Were you immediately drawn into the story?

Consider the ending. Did you expect it or were you surprised?

How would you describe the ending? Name it from your knowledge of
narrative endings.

If you could rewrite the ending would you? In other words, did you find
the ending satisfying? Why or why not?

## Talk with the Author

If you could talk to the author, what would you want to know?

Have you read other books by this author? Is their any connection in
terms of theme, writing style, structure etc?

## Reactions to the book

How did you experience the book? Did it intrigue, amuse, disturb,
alienate, irritate, or frighten you?

## The Mystery of Eilean Mor Gary Crew

The shepherds of the Outer Hebrides had feared the isle of Eilean Mor
since the beginning of time. The meadows were haunted, they said.
Spirits rose from the sea, the ghosts of shipwrecked sailors. Ghosts
that longed to go home.

To fly…

Perhaps that was true, or perhaps only shepherds tales.

But the sea still cast its dead upon the stony beach, or dashed them
against the mighty cliffs.

And still the spirits cried out to be free.

To fly…

In the 7<sup>th</sup> century a stone chapel was built, but prayer and
offerings to the gods were of little use. The sea continued to cast up
its dead, and soon the chapel was empty.

Save for wind. And the fog.

And the screaming of birds.

But as the British Empire grew, so did the demand for logic.

‘Ghosts?’ the merchants said.

‘Spirits longing to fly home? Bah! Humbug!’

In 1895 they set about building a lighthouse on Eilean Mor.

‘That will put an end to the rumours,’ they said. ‘To tales of spirits
that want to fly … There will be no more moaning of the dead on Eilean
Mor.’

But the island did not want a lighthouse, nor the machines, nor the
technology, nor the men the merchants brought to build it.

Four years that lighthouse took to build.

The lamp shone for one year only.

On the 15<sup>th</sup> December, 1900, the light went out.

On the 16<sup>th</sup>, as the service vessel *Hesperus* prepared to
investigate the problem, the sea came up, wild as hell and foggy.

The captain was worried.

‘The island is sending us a message,’ he told his crew. ‘Old Eilean Mor
don’t want us to come. Or them spirits have changed their minds and
decided to saty…’

The men aboard the *Hesperus* muttered among themselves. A sailor should
be careful how he spoke about the spirits of Eilean Mor. Three
lighthouse keepers were stationed there: James Ducat, Thomas Mitchell
and Donald McArthur. Yet no one had seen their light for days.

In spite of the fog and the wild sea, the *Hesperus* set out the day
after Christmas. The captain was eager to be there. He carried gifts for
the lighthouse keepers:

rum, fruitcake, shortbread.

As the vessel approached the island, the fog lifted and the sea grew
calm, welcoming almost.

Inviting.

The captain paced the deck as the lighthouse came into view.

Calm as the sea was,

the light was still out.

When the dinghy landed, none of the keepers came to help. Not a rope was
thrown. Not a voice heard. ‘Strange,’ the captain said, turning to his
crew. ‘You stay with the dinghy. I’ll go on alone.’

Three hundred stairs led up to the lighthouse door. The captain scaled
them two by two.

The black iron key was in the lock.

He turned it with a trembling hand.

Beyond lay the kitchen and parlour. Usually an ale, a cheery fire, a
hearty greeting.

This day, nothing.

The captain hesitated.

He cupped his hands and called upward, into the giddy spiral of the
staircase, his voice echoing as it rose.

As if in answer, a black bird swooped and, passing him, quit the tower
by the open door.

Then another.

And another.

‘Three in all,’ the captain told his crew on his return.

‘Three in all.’ And he hung his head, as if to hide his face.

‘One for each man,’ someone said. ‘One for Ducat, for Mitchell and for
McArthur.’

‘Great black birds, they were,’ the captain said, his voice no more than
a steely croak.

‘Never saw their likes…’

‘Spirits,’ someone whispered. ‘Ghosts…’

And the captain shuddered like a wet bird.

‘I went in,’ he told them. ‘Foolishly, I went in…’

There, on the desk in the parlour, the log book lay open.

A pen still rested in the inkwell.

December 12<sup>th</sup>:

*‘Ducat irritable. Weather fine.’*

December 13<sup>th</sup>:

*‘Ducat quiet. McArthur crying.’*

December 14<sup>th</sup>:

*‘Me, Ducat and McArthur prayed…’*

And on the 15<sup>th</sup>, when the light was last seen, the entry
read,

all too simply:

‘God is over all.’

‘Was anything missing?’ the crew wanted to know.

‘Two moleskins.’

‘Moleskins missing? The log said the weather was fine. And only two
moleskins gone?’

‘What about the third?’

‘On its hook,’ the captain croaked, ‘as was the gun behind the door.’

‘Nothing else?’

‘Nothing.’

‘And the light? Did you go to the top?’

‘The wicks were trimmed, but never lit.’

‘So?’

The captain sat hunched over. Uneasy, not himself. He shuddered again.

‘A wave took them, maybe.’ His voice failed him. He cleared his throat.

‘A giant wave,’ he croaked.

‘What?’ the crew wondered, unconvinced. ‘A wave big enough to sweep them
off the cliff?’

‘A wave two hundred feet high?’

‘On a clear fine day?’

The captain shook his head, brooding.

‘You were gone a long time,’ a crewman said. ‘Was there nothing else?’

‘Nothing. I walked across the island to the old chapel. I left the
presents there: the rum, the cake and the shortbread. I left them on the
altar, beneath the crucifix. It seemed right, being Christmas.’

Then he looked to the sea beyond the porthole and got up. ‘I am going
outside,’ he said.

‘But it’s freezing out there. besides, there’s them spirits. Them
spirits flying about.’

‘Yes,’ the captain said, ‘I know…’

The shepherds of the Outer Hebrides had feared the isle of Eilean Mor
since the beginning of time. The meadows were haunted, they said.

Spirits rose from the sea, the ghosts of shipwrecked sailors. Ghosts
that longed to fly home.

But now there is only silence.

## DRTA DIRECTED READING-THINKING ACTIVITIES

Purposes:

- To teach a reading comprehension strategy which enables students to
  question and think about what they are reading in order to make it an
  active process. Reading comprehension is about thinking, questioning,
  reflecting.

- Students to realise that their past experiences, their knowledge of
  language and context enables them to predict story. This knowledge
  accumulates as they read.

- To use the strategy of prediction to aid comprehension in reading
  narrative text.

- To develop the students’ sense of story and monitoring for meaning.

Preparation:

Teacher reads through the story in order to map sections (to tell
students where to read to.)

Procedure:

- Predict what the story might be about from title and cover.

- Students read introductory section. Rethink predictions on the basis
  of this section. Predict what will happen next based on new knowledge.

- Continue to read to section breaks, verifying, modifying, or rejecting
  predictions based on accumulated knowledge.

The DRTA strategy can be used to reinforce text type. The more students
know about how to read a text, the easier reading will be.

## Granddaddy’s Gift Margaree Mitchell

Granddaddy’s name was Joe Morgan. I followed my Granddaddy everywhere,
so people called me Little Joe. But Granddaddy called me daughter.

I grew up on a farm in Mississippi with Grandmama and Granddaddy. Most
people at the time didn’t have much. Granddaddy owned his own land. He
raised cows, pigs, and horses. He grew cotton, corn, and cucumbers.
Grandmamma had a garden, and we always had tomatoes, butter beans, and
black-eyed peas.

Granddaddy had only got as far as eighth grade. He would have had to
leave home to go further, and his daddy didn’t have the money to send
him. So he worked in the fields, saved his money, and bought his land a
little at a time. He ordered books through the mail and read them by the
light of the fireplace.

One particular morning, when I was eight years old, I slowed around
until the big yellow school bus left without me. I didn’t want to go to
school anymore.

It had rained for several days, and this was the first time it was dry
enough for Granddaddy to plough the fields. Well, he must have had X-ray
eyes, because as soon as the bus pulled away, he stopped the tractor and
came into the house.

“Daughter, get your books together,” he said.

We got in the black truck and rode the thirty miles to school. On the
way we rode past a cotton field full of workers, some of them my age.

I asked why I had to go to school. I already knew how to read and write,
and the books we had at home were better than the books at school. Those
books, passed down from the white schools, were raggedy, with pages
missing.

“Daughter,” Granddaddy said sadly, “I know they’re not teaching you
everything they should, but you have to go to school. I want you to
learn as much as you can, so when you grow up, you can choose what you
want to do. I didn’t have that choice.”

That night after supper, Granddaddy said he was taking us to a meeting.
We all got in the truck, me, my brother, and sisters. Granddaddy turned
off into a dirt road and rove for a long time.

He told us that many years ago, segregation laws had been passed in the
South to keep black people from having the same rights as white people,
like the right to vote. He said it was important to vote, that it was a
way for our voices to be heard.

When we finally arrived at a church, the parking lot was full and cars
were lined up on both sides of the road. we found a place to stand at
the back of the church. A lawyer, Mr James Marshall, was asking for
volunteers. He wanted someone to try to register to vote.

At first nobody said anything. Then, from the back of the room,
Granddaddy raised his hand, “I’ll go,” he said.

The meeting ended with a song and a prayer for Granddaddy. In those days
it was dangerous for people to stand up for their rights.

The next day Granddaddy rode into town and parked his truck in front of
the courthouse. He walked like he was going somewhere, the way he always
did. As usual, I was right behind him.

He went into one of the offices and told the lady behind the counter
that he wanted to register to vote.

The lady went into the back room and came back with a man.

“Well, now, Joe,” the man said. “You see, there is this test you have to
take on the Mississippi Constitution. It’s hard, real hard. You’re doing
aa right, Joe. Just be satisfied with what you have.”

Granddaddy left the office. I turned and walked after him, my head
hanging down. I didn’t want to look at Granddaddy’s face, because I knew
he felt bad, too.

He waited for me at the top of the courthouse steps. He took my hand.
“Daughter,” he said, “hold your head up high. We have done nothing
wrong.”

Granddaddy told me there was something important to strive for in
addition to the good things we had. There were some things that made a
person feel good inside, like having the rights we were all entitled to
as citizens of these United States.

I didn’t understand everything then, but I lifted my head. The sun was
shining.

Later that week, Mr James Marshall came by the house. He told Granddaddy
that his name was in the newspaper. In those days the names of black
people who tried to register to vote were printed in the newspaper.

Word had got out that Granddaddy was going to take the test. Not
everybody agreed with what Granddaddy was doing, I discovered. From then
on the co-op store in town wouldn’t let Granddaddy buy feed for his cows
and seeds for planting. People didn’t want their children to play with
me.

At recess I stood alone under a shade tree and watched my used-to-be
friends play jump rope and tag and hopscotch. Now I *really* didn’t want
to go to school.

We started going by Mr Marshall’s house whenever we went to town. Mr
Marshall would go over the constitution with Granddaddy and ask him
questions. I was supposed to be doing my homework, but I was listening,
too. I answered the questions right along with Granddaddy.

On the day that Granddaddy went to the courthouse with Mr Marshall to
take the test, a big crowd gathered outside. People started shouting and
calling Granddaddy bad names. Granddaddy had on his Sunday suit.
Somebody pulled the coat and tore it.

The police met Granddaddy and Mr Marshall at the door of the courthouse.
They asked Granddaddy why he was trying to cause trouble. He told them
he was just trying to register to vote.

In the back room of the courthouse, he answered all twenty-two
questions. Granddaddy passed the test. But he and Mr Marshall had to
leave through the back door of the courthouse.

The news spread quickly that Granddaddy was now a registered voter. That
night we all got in the truck and headed to the church. There was going
to be a big celebration. As we drove up, we could see bright orange
flames shooting up against the dark sky.

After the church burned down, people’s fear turned into determination,
and more men and women came forward to register to vote.

I understood why Granddaddy had wanted me to go to school. I never
skipped school again, even if we did have raggedy books.

On my eighteenth birthday, when I went to register to vote, Granddaddy
came with me. I didn’t have to take a test on the constitution. I just
had to fill out a card with my name, address, and date of birth. Now I
could vote and make my own voice heard.

Granddaddy had taught me to stand up for things, even if I was scared,
and always to be proud. His gift never left me.

At the top of the courthouse steps, Granddaddy took my hand. We had come
a long way. We still had a long way to go.

## RECIPROCAL TEACHING

Extending reading strategies – Reciprocal teaching is a research based
dialogue between group members to jointly construct meaning in text.

Material at instructional level.

Groups of 6-8. Session approximately 20 minutes.

Using 4 strategies which shift the responsibility to the reader.

- Predicting

- Clarifying

- Question generating

- Summarising

Modelling – teacher models strategies, then becomes a group member,
monitoring use of strategies.

Short, factual articles, relevant to current learning concepts are
particularly appropriate.

## Predicting

Sets up purpose for the reading.

Provides opportunity to link new ideas to prior learning. The student is
anticipating what will come next in the text, based on appropriate prior
knowledge, and on the structure and content of the text.

Predicting encourages students to think ahead actively.

As students read, predictions are confirmed or modified. Students
effectively monitor own understandings.

## Clarifying

Uses context and word knowledge to overcome difficulties.

- unfamiliar vocabulary

- text which is structured or set out in an unfamiliar way

- new or difficult concepts

- when they l;ose track of the meaning

Strategies

- rereading

- using the context of the passage

- using their knowledge of written language – e.g. vocabulary,
  structure, grammar

- using a dictionary or thesaurus to check words

- using other reference materials e.g. atlas, encyclopaedia, road map

- it may be necessary for the students to read the whole passage again
  to understand the meaning

## Question Generating

Develops critical thinking skills.

Question generating gives the student an opportunity to:

- identify the kind of information that provides the substance for an
  appropriate question

- frame that question (before, during, and after reading)

When suitable questions have been asked, the student can then:

- offer possible solutions

- find relevant information to answer questions

- monitor their own comprehension

- help others answer questions they have

Students become much more involved in the reading activity when they are
posing and answering the questions themselves, rather than merely
responding to the teacher’s questions or to pre-set questions.

Questioning is a means of self-checking.

## Summarising

Indicates understanding.

Way students can review what they have read themselves.

The student is identifying and integrating important information
presented in the text.

In summarising the student need to:

- initially identify the most important content of the section

- integrate and own the information which indicates understanding

Implementation

Teachers should:

- be familiar with the text

- provide a brief focused introduction

- where appropriate, link the text being read to current content areas

- model the strategies, and support students in using them

- regularly monitor students’ use of strategies

- use information gained through monitoring as a guide to the amount of
  further support and practice needed by students

- reduce their own involvement in the dialogue as soon as possible

During the group lesson, students discuss the text only in small chunks.
When the session is over, it is advantageous for the students to read
the whole text to themselves.

> **FOUND POEMS**

As part of our Reading Curriculum the students have been introduced to
Choral Reading, where they have taken words from a narrative text and
turned it into poetry. This was done at the whole class and group level.
Groups then performed their ‘poems’.

Found poetry is similar at the independent level. A found poem is shaped
after the students collect words, phrases, and sentences from a text.
That is, it is made from ‘found’ words, as well as the students being
able to add their own.

Although the students draw on the language of the mentor text,
rearranging it and adding their own language, they create a unique poem
which is their own work. They may also be influenced by an author’s use
of imagery and observations about life, but they can certainly add their
own. During the teaching of the craft of writing we have done quite a
lot of work on ‘poetic craft’, so the students will look to use this
from the text as well as the author’s style and word choice.

Method:

- Choose a story or a particularly memorable passage from a book.

- Highlight significant words, phrases and sentences.

> In Choral Reading I referred to “the parts where the words are most
> striking.

- beautiful

- interesting beat

- surprising order

- simple, yet so right

- you want to read them again and again

<!-- -->

- Arrange the words into a poem.

Extension:

- Compose a **parallel poem**, using the same structure as the found
  > poem. A parallel poem uses the same line structure as another poem,
  > but focuses on a completely different topic.

Variations to method:

Found Poem Instructions

- Read the chosen text for enjoyment.

- Re-read the text highlighting the striking words, phrases, sentences –
  > words that are powerful, moving, or interesting.

- Make a list of the highlighted words in order. (Double spacing of
  > lines is a good idea so that you can work with the words). Add any
  > others you see in the re-reading that you missed the first time.

- Look over your list. Put a line through anything that is dull or
  > unnecessary, or doesn’t now fit into the theme of your poem.

- You may need to make minor changes so that the words fit together.
  > E.g. tense, plurals, possessives etc.

- When revising – “Does it make sense?” You may need to add a word or
  > two so that the poem makes sense, flows more smoothly or makes the
  > point you want to get across. Re-read.

- Edit for line breaks.

- Proofread and select a title.

- Setting. Words used to describe the setting.

- Characters. Words used to describe character.

## READERS’ THEATRE

- Enjoying and appreciating literature through oral reading.

- Through Readers’ Theatre students also gain an understanding of
  punctuation as they turn a text (novel, poetry, picture book etc) into
  a play script.

- Students can turn indirect speech into direct speech.

- There are many ways of conducting Readers’ Theatre. E.g.

  - A group of students (depending on the number of characters plus
    narrator) meet to prepare a text for an oral performance.

  - Having allocated characters, each student highlights the necessary
    speech for his/her character. Through whole class shared reading and
    discussion, they know not to highlight ‘he said’ etc. (Except where
    some poetry needs it).

  - The group practises, assisting each other with any adjustments.

  - Depending on the experience of the group, they may decide to leave
    out some of the text – e.g. the narrator may have too much to say,
    and they can abbreviate this.

  - The group knows that they only have their voices. In front of the
    class they ‘perform’ their script.

- Students can write their own scripts from stories they know.

- Adaptations of stories can be written and performed.

  - Little Red Riding Hood

  - Cinderella

  - The Three Little Pigs

- Consolidate students’ knowledge of genres

  - Anansi the Spider

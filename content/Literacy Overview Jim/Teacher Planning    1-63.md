## 1. Vision: what we want for students as writers

To plan writing, know your expectations. *What is your vision for your
students as writers?*

- **See themselves as writers.** Every child arrives "writing" — Prep
  teachers take a day-one lesson and a first-week sample from each student
  for a class developmental profile; individual portfolios track growth.
  In Writers' Workshop students learn their strengths, weaknesses and
  development — and understand, as we do, that the aim is writing well over
  time.
- **Keep a Writer's Notebook** as part of their writing life (see handout):
  observations of the world, having-a-go with language, practice, ideas,
  thinking. Choosing what to write about teaches the need to write well.
- **Build stamina and fluency.** Children learn to read by reading; they
  learn to write by writing — writing, writing, writing. Sustained daily
  time builds stamina and fluency exactly as silent sustained reading does,
  developing rigour, purpose-awareness and planned development.
- **Read like writers.** *"What can I learn from this author that will help
  me as a writer?"* ("Reading books and writing them are tied together." —
  Betsy Byars. "I read a lot… My best advice… is: Read." — Mem Fox.) And all
  children are writers.
- **Develop the craft** (see flow chart): growing in focus, organisation,
  style and conventions.
- **Understand genres**, eventually choosing which suits each need.
- **Belong to a literate community** — classroom, school — sharing ideas,
  listening, helping, learning; engaging in writing conversations
  (conferences).
- **Follow the writing process:** planning → drafting → revising → editing →
  publishing.
- **Work independently** in the workshop using gradually-released resources.
- **Know their audience** — including themselves ("I can write with 'me' in
  mind").
- **Observe the world** — writing opportunities everywhere.
- **Publish lots! Love writing!**

## 2. Goals for writers (Davis/Hill, *The NoNonsense Guide to Teaching Writing*)

Students who: live like writers (observing, questioning, wondering,
opinionated, multi-perspectival); find ideas strategically; know themselves
as writers, reflect on process, set goals; write with clear intentions;
belong actively to a writing community (sharing, feedback); write to discover
what they think; know the qualities of good writing; hold a repertoire of
quality-lifting strategies; write towards audience; treat genres as
"containers"; understand revision's purpose; edit with conventions;
internalise mini-lesson and conference teaching; transfer workshop learning
to all writing.

## 3. Growth over time (Katie Wood Ray) + conferring questions

She tracks growth areas, then questions from them — reflection leading to
self-evaluation. Over time we hope to see:

**A sense of self as writers, with personal processes that work** (efficient
when they know things about themselves):

- Out of all your writing, how is this piece similar? Different?
- Tell me about yourself as a writer.
- How did you go about this piece — your process?
- What new things are you trying as a writer?
- What do you do best as a writer right now?
- Which genre feels most comfortable?

**Reading the world like writers** (variety, volume, thoughtfulness —
"writing comes from daily life… the writer's first act is to listen and
observe" — Donald Graves):

- Looking back through your notebook, where has it travelled from your desk?
- How much are you writing? How often?
- What kinds of entries are you gathering?
- What notebook exercises push you?
- Entries that started one place and ended somewhere different?
- What surprises you in your notebook?

**Thoughtful, deliberate purpose** (willing to linger):

- Why this piece? How did you choose it?
- What did you do in and out of the notebook to get ready?
- Plans for the next days (weeks)? Plans as a writer after this piece?

**Responsive community membership** (ideas shared, others heard, questions
asked, strugglers supported, feedback constant):

- What help have you given other writers here?
- Who are you learning from? What?
- Who might want to see this — would you share it with them?
- Any resources that could help another writer?
- What help do you need that you could ask for?

**Reading texts like writers** (craft, genre, form):

- Where did you really try to craft this?
- What decisions did you make while drafting?
- Which published writers are you learning from — what are you trying?
- How will you write this? (form, at planning)
- What do you know about this genre — what examples studied?

**Audience and going public:**

- Who is your audience? What stakes for them?
- Proofreading process — what checked, corrected?
- What resources helped?
- What proofreading help do you need?
- Presentation format for publication?

## 4. Workshop structures

**Minilessons** (focus time): explain why the questions matter; show how
mentor writers answered them. **Independent writing time + conferences**
("Tell me about your writing" — question from what the writer is doing, or
from class lessons). **Share time**: follow up the focus question
(*"What new things are you trying in your notebook?" "What have you learnt
from your mentor author?"*).

| Minilesson | Directed focus, whole class |
|---|---|
| Independent writing | Individual conferences; guided writing, small group |
| Share time | Whole class; groups; pairs — strategies, problems shared |

Students need ready access to: **Writer's Notebook** (see handout — "a place
to play and to study craft" — Isoke Nia; notebook support sheets, e.g. Katie
Wood Ray's, pasted in); **Literature Folder** of mentor-text copies for
year-long reference; **writing book** of drafts.

<!-- p.10–11 -->

### Notebook entries worth trying

Close observations (sights, sounds, textures, moods); overheard talk;
question-chains about a subject; keep-lists; quotations (music, movies,
books); writing from photographs; news-heard plot ideas; memory chains
(*"What does this remind me of?"*); noticed things others walk past;
observed character details; setting ideas from interesting places; personal
obsessions (Antarctica, worms, vacuum cleaners… WHATEVER); researched facts;
great first lines; found poems; reflections; wordplay; oral family stories;
interview notes; name/place lists; long cared-about entries; anything you
invent.

## 5. What we teach: strategies, techniques, questions, conventions

- **Strategies** — ways to do things, e.g. gathering ideas. Imagine a story
  behind a seen object (Jim's *Brown-Paper Bag* poem below); write from
  pictures; let objects take life as persona poetry.
- **Techniques** — ways to fashion things: the craft learnt from mentors
  (see craft lessons) — leads and endings; showing not telling; sharpening
  and tightening; structure, focus, purpose, audience, conventions.
  (Mem Fox.)
- **Questions** — ways to think: the questions authors ask, teaching
  students to ask their own — e.g. *reading my notebook, does a theme
  emerge? What entries am I making?* Notebooks as tools, including choosing
  what to lift out. Plus revision questions.
- **Conventions** — ways to expect things: small-group, conference, or
  whole-class lessons on current majority needs — punctuation, spelling,
  sentence/paragraph structure.

### Student poems (kept verbatim)

**The Brown-Paper BAG** (object story): *As my train pulled into the
station I noticed a bottle in a brown-paper bag… Still wrapped in its
brown-paper bag the empty bottle stands on the platform, tucked in at the
neck where inebriated hands clutched it during the night; its now drained
contents providing the numbness to give sleep within feet of the train.* —
Mr K.

**Scarecrow!** (persona): *Scarecrow I am. Still all day, still all night.
Straw hat and checked top. So scary they think I am. Bored all day, bored
all wight. Not a person in site. Just the grass and me.* — Maddy Johnson,
Gr. Wembley. (Objects with significance; pictures/photographs as starts.)

**Take Me Away:** *Two items; so simple, so fine, that bring harmony and
peace to one — to me. They take me to a place where I am alone, where I am
alive. Two easy, simple items are the portholes to my special place. Pen and
paper — when together take me away, away to may home where I am alive.* —
Bridget Walker, Gr 6 Wembley. (See revising-poem and looking-into-objects
strategies, Poetry 1.)

**Diary** (persona): *Secrets rushing though my pages, making them inky and
— okay, who cares about that? I mean, just because I'm a diary doesn't mean
you have to write in me. I mean, come on! I don't care about the school play
tomorrow, or why your boyfriend dumped you. Gosh, I mean, seriously, get a
life! Not that you should just chuck me in a drawer and forget about me.
Just an occasional poem or something would be nice.* — Alicia Gada-Carolaw,
Gr. Wembley.

**Sense of audience lesson:** a partner reads your writing and retells it —
then you know whether it made sense.

## 6. Units of study: mapping the year

The unit frames minilessons, conferences and share time — balancing set
units with choice-supporting ones. Katie Wood Ray's vision, as possible
studies:

- **Selves as writers/processes:** writers' lives and what they say;
  writing in non-professional lives; our own writer histories.
- **Reading the world:** what writers gather to sustain published life;
  gathering habits of poets, memoirists, fiction writers, essayists…;
  specific notebooks; our own notebooks.
- **Deliberate purpose:** following an idea to publication; "living with" a
  topic; firsthand and secondhand research.
- **Community:** peer conferring and collaboration; writers with writers,
  editors, publishers.
- **Reading texts like writers:** reading for structure and wordcraft;
  specific genres (poetry, memoir, literary nonfiction, fiction, drama,
  journalism, critique, editorials, picture books, description…); "writing"
  without words (punctuation, spacing, layout, fonts); one writer's body of
  work; text-structure possibilities; crafting techniques; sentences and
  paragraphs built for effect.
- **Audience/going public:** publishing opportunities; revision/editing for
  publication; what publishing involves.

Keep in mind: scope and sequence across the school; resources (e.g. the
Mentor Text Library); student/teacher interests; test requirements.

**Possibilities:** how writers work; Writer's Notebook; genre study (poetry,
memoir, biography, autobiography, procedural/how-to, reports, folktales,
picture books, feature article, journalism, realistic fiction, essay,
persuasive text — speeches, ads, commercials, debates, expositions,
arguments, editorials, letters, reviews, cartoons, factual/poetic/protest
forms — poems, protests, letters); explanation; historical fiction;
narrative; recount; diary; literature response journal; text structures
within genre (compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution, description
and listing); author study; process study (writing process, punctuation,
conferring, notebook-as-tool, generating and following through ideas);
students' choice. **Workshop questions** (K. Wood Ray): audiences? passions?
genres to try? stories to tell? causes? never-dones? publication dreams?
occasions this year?

## 7. Reading like writers: craft, genre, framework

**Craft** — focus (topic, clarity, purpose, audience); organisation (format,
detail, structure); style (words, fluency, voice); conventions (sentence
structure; mechanics — grammar, spelling). **Testing genre / author study:**
notice what the author does; check it recurs across books; name its effect;
try it.

**Genre study:** gather material → immersion (read-aloud, shared, guided,
independent — texts like the ones students will write) → mentor texts (*how
did the author write this? What possibilities for me?*) → writing throughout.

**Instructional framework:** gathering texts (striking, craft-rich,
high-interest, readable, real-world, genre-exemplary, accessible to all —
first met as *readers*, then as writers; texts students will emulate; texts
you love) → setting the stage (expectations stated: *we will learn this
craft for our own writing*) → immersion (guided questions — *how does this
writer make it interesting? use punctuation interestingly?* — read-along,
read-aloud, shared, independent, paired, group; notebook entries, post-its,
highlighters on the studied process; "writers have vision from reading" —
K. Ray) → close study (whole-class noticing lists and discussion; groups;
craft/genre/new-teaching lines) → writing (the study demonstrated, minilesson
follow-up in conferences and share). *Study Driven* — Katie Wood Ray.

**Close-study questions.** Across texts: *What kind of writing is this? How
does it differ? What work does it do in the world (topics, audiences,
purposes)? What approaches do writers take? How long, in which containers?*
One text at a time: *How organised — what chunks, what movement (time or
ideas)? Lead and ending — how managed? Striking language — what learnt?
Focus — what's in, what's left out? Modes (description, exposition,
narration…)? Punctuation work? Page/paragraph-break decisions? Graphics,
layout, font — meaning and appeal? Title — relation to meaning, likely
choice?* Then students produce the studied kind of writing.

*"Powerful communicators understand intimately that the choosing of words
and the placement of words has an affective impact on meaning."* — Mem Fox.

## 8. Two worked units

### Punctuation as craft

Gathering: *Click, Clack, Moo* (D. Cronin), *Diary of a Wombat* (J. French),
*Eaglet's World* (E. Minshull), *Puddles* (J. London), *Roller Coaster*
(M. Frazee), *The Great Gracie Chase* and *The Wonderful Happens* (C. Rylant),
*Yo! Yes?* (C. Raschka). Stage it (*the noticed punctuation goes into your
own writing*), introduce the collection (*how is this writer using
punctuation interestingly?*), independent reading with highlighting. Close
study (*any interesting punctuation work?*): whole-class craft lists and
discussion, naming over time what each mark does. Writing shows the study;
conferences differentiate.

<!-- p.28 -->

### Newspaper commentary (single text)

*Ready-Made Sandwich Just Too Convenient* — Leonard Pitts, *Miami Herald*,
17 Nov 2003. Stage it, read independently with highlighters/post-its
(*how is this author crafting it to be interesting?*), close-study the
striking language. (The pack reprints the syndicated column in full for this
study; it is not retyped here — third-party copyright. The craft analysis
below is Jim's.) Craft: main idea lands at the end; organisation (lead,
structure, ending); detail (dialogue, sensory detail, anecdotes,
thoughtshots; setting/character/object description; questioning); short
single-sentence paragraphs; style (words, voice — *"I'm sorry. Did I say
lazy?"*, *"Heaven help us"*, humour, creative phrasing); repetition and
lists; punctuation used interestingly.

## 10. Genre reference

### Subgenre lists

**Procedural** — instruct (build, game, experiment, directions) vs explain
(food, care, making, operating). **Informational** — announcements, blurbs,
book reports, brochures, Q&A books, weather reports, descriptions, diagrams,
explanations, interviews, reports, questionnaires. **Journalistic** — advice
columns, entertainment/home/garden/human-interest/sports articles, magazine
articles, local/national news, feature articles, weather, travel, comics.
**Persuasive** — campaign speeches, book/movie/music reviews, apologies,
classifieds, debates, editorials, posters, real-estate copy, job
applications, letters (editor, complaint), essays, debates, expositions,
arguments, cartoons. **Literary** — realistic fiction, mysteries, traditional
folktales, pourquoi stories, fables, modern folktales, fantasy.

<!-- p.34–35 -->

### Narrative

Orientation (who/when/where) → complication (middle, expanded) →
resolution (satisfactory, not necessarily happy). **Planning:** plot (what
happens first/next/last, why, in what words); setting (where, what like,
pictured? when; describing words); characters (who, what like, look like,
personalities, speech, relations, thoughts, actions); story structure (lead
sentence, problem, resolution, ending). **Strategies:** story maps, written
outlines, character profiles, cartoon strips, speech charts, partner
retelling, plot/setting/character questions (*Write Ways* — Lesley Wing Jan).
**Activities:** language experience (teacher scribes); rewrite known stories
with changed elements; alternative endings; new perspectives (pigs' wolf);
character profiles (*Hobbit*'s Bilbo; *Danny*'s father); story maps.
**Purpose:** entertain. **Structure:** orientation (who/what/where/when) →
complication (event series) → resolution.

<!-- p.36–38 -->

### Recount

Features: past tense, verbs, personal comment/pronouns, time-word links
(chart "words" with students, not "then"). Types: personal (publish theirs);
factual (find in newspapers; write articles as recounts); imaginative (as a
character — e.g. Alexander's terrible day); biography; historical recount;
autobiography. Subgenres: all-about-me books, eyewitness accounts, personal
letters, postcards, journals, autobiographies, diaries, memoirs, obituaries,
reflections, personal narratives, resumes. Mentor mini-lessons: striking
verbs (*Pioneer Girl* — Anderson's westward covered-wagon passage); time
words; striking adjectives (*Back Home* — B. Pinkey's "sparkling eyes and
apple-dumpling cheeks"). **Purpose:** retell experience/event.
**Structure:** introduction (who/where/when/why) → sequenced events →
personal comment.

<!-- p.39–41 -->

### Memoir and biography organisers

*The Relatives Came* (Rylant): intro (who the relatives, what came, where
from Virginia, when summer, why to visit) → sequence (left Virginia, drove
all day, pop and crackers, pulled in, hugging, big supper, quiet talk, slept
breathing together, tended garden, headed back) → optional close (*"…dreamed
about the next summer"*). *Pioneer Girl* (Anderson): intro (b.1867, Midwest,
Pa/Ma/Mary/Carrie) → events (prairie house, Wisconsin returns, age-7 move,
one-room house, Iowa hotel, Dakota, teaching at 15, 1885 marriage to
Almanzo, 1894 Missouri, 1915 San Francisco, first book past 60, 1949 his
death, 1957 hers) → conclusion (Laura's pioneer-tales quote).

<!-- p.42–43 -->

### Explanation

Features: reasons, cause/effect, signal words (because, so, consequently),
diagrams, illustrations, labels, present tense. **Purpose:** how/why things
happen. **Structure:** what's explained → sequenced events → conclusion.

<!-- p.44–45 -->

### Report

Planning: topic → known → want-to-know → questions (teaches paragraphing;
Echidna chart) → notetaking → draft. Features: present tense, concise,
technical terms, accuracy, bibliography. **Purpose:** classify and describe
factually. **Structure:** general statement/classification → feature
description → conclusion.

<!-- p.46–47 -->

### Procedure

Features: leading action verbs, timeless present, headings, diagrams,
concision, labels, photos, illustrations. Contexts: recipes, maths games,
classroom procedures, experiments, phys. ed., art/craft. **Purpose:** how to
do something. **Structure:** title → aim → needs → ordered steps.

<!-- p.48–49 -->

### Argument / Discussion / Description

**Argument** — persuade with reasons: issue (what + opinion) → supporting
reasons → conclusion with recommendation. **Discussion** — both sides: issue
→ for → against → considered conclusion. **Description** — what something is
like: opening statement → characteristic features.

<!-- p.50–52 -->

### Purposes and forms (teacher reference)

Record feelings/observations/thoughts → letters, science reports, poems,
diaries, journals, sensory jottings. Describe → portraits, event sequences,
labels, captions, ads. Inform/advise → posters, news scripts, minutes,
invitations, programs. Persuade → ads, editorials, debate notes, cartoons.
Clarify thinking → research notes, graph explanations, diagrams, jottings.
Relate to others → letters, requests, cards, questionnaires. Predict →
speculations, story endings, research questions. Compare → charts,
note-making, diagrams, graphs, descriptions. Direct → recipes, instructions,
stage directions, rules. Amuse → jokes, riddles, drama scripts, stories,
poems, anecdotes.

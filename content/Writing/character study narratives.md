**CREATING CHARACTER**

Going beyond physical description.

- Actions. Describe the character’s actions.

- Internal monologue. (Inner life). Getting inside the character’s mind.

- Point of view. What other people think of the character.

- Setting. Time and place surrounding the character.

- Dialogue. What the character says.

Students make notes in the following chart.

Combine descriptions to create a story or a poem.

**Voice**

Students can decide in which point of view they wish to write.

First person. Using “I” as narrator. The tone is very personal, speaking
directly to the reader.

Second person. Using “you”. This can be explained as a kind of letter
spoken to someone who isn’t in the room - a way of trying to understand
a person who isn’t directly there by speaking directly to him/her.

Third person. Describes everyone as “he” or “she”.

**Why Character Study?**

 When readers attend to the characters in their books, their level of
comprehension and engagement increases.

 Characters in a story offer concrete- and often visual- footholds to
steer students through a story.

 Learning strategies for getting to know characters, purposes for
understanding characters, and ways to use what they know to better
comprehension creates readers who pay close attention as they read.

 Characters carry much of the meaning of a story. Inferences related to
characters are a major key in understanding the nuances of the entire
story.

Inferring Character Traits

**Inferences about Characters**

We can infer things about characters from…

 The things they say

 The things they do

 Their interactions with other characters

 What other characters say or think about them

We can infer certain things about characters, such as…

 Their traits

 Their thoughts

 Their feelings

 Their motives and goals

 How they have changed

 Lessons they have learned

**Inferring Character Traits**

**Readers get to know a character by…**

 Paying attention to what the character does and asking themselves,
“What kind of character would do that?” They can use what they know
about people in real life to help them by asking, “Who do I know who
would do something like that? What kind of person is he/she?”

 Paying attention to what the character thinks and says as well as what
he does. Then the readers ask, “Do they match?” If they don’t match,
readers push themselves to think more by asking, “What does that tell me
about the character?”

 Paying attention to what other characters in the story say or think
about them.

**Inferring Character Thoughts and Feelings**

**Readers get to know a character by…**

 Figuring out his/her feelings. Readers pay attention to the
character’s actions and ask, “Why did the character do that? How might
he be feeling? How would I be feeling if this happened to me or if I
said/did this?”

 Using what they know about people in real life to help them understand
the character. They might ask, “What would make a person say/do/think
that?”

**Using pictures to infer about characters**

 Readers are aware that sometimes there is information provided through
pictures that is not given by the words in the text. They pay attention
to the pictures and notice things about how the character looks. They
combine that with what the words are saying to infer something that the
author did not come right out and say.

**Inferring Character Motivation**

Readers get to know a character by…

 Paying attention to what a character says, thinks, and does and
asking, “What does the character want?”

 Using what they know about people in real life to help them understand
the character. They might ask, “What would make a person say/do/think
that?” or “What does the character have to gain from doing this?”

**Inferring relationships between characters**

 Readers pay attention to how characters act towards one another and to
the things they say about each other. They think about people they know
who act that way and think about how they would describe their
relationship. This helps them decide what kind of relationship the
characters they are studying might have.

**Growing theories about characters**

 Readers know that, like real people, characters can be multifaceted
(are not just one way). They

 They are not too quick to make up their minds about a character. They
pay attention to EVERYTHING the character says and does and develop a
theory that includes all of it.

 Readers develop theories about characters and, as they read on, they
pay attention to things that either support their theory or require them
to revise it.

 Readers are not too quick to judge their characters. They consider
what might have made someone the kind of person they are or why else
they might act a certain way.

**Inferring from things that surprise us**

 Readers notice when characters do something unexpected or surprising
and ask themselves why the character did it. This can help a reader
understand a character’s personality, feelings, or goals.

**Inferring about character change**

 Readers notice when a character’s behaviour has changed. They think
about what made the character change. One thing they can do is to look
closely at what happened right before the character began to act
differently.

 Readers notice when a character’s behaviour has changed. They ask
themselves what the character may know or understand now that he didn’t
before. This helps the reader figure out what lesson the character
learnt.

**Inferring implied but unstated information**

 Readers use what they know about the world, how stories go, and the
characters in a story to figure out information that the author did not
directly state. For example, if a father loses his job and the family
stops taking special trips, we can use what we know about the world to
figure out that they have less money to spend than they used to.

**Making predictions**

 Readers study characters’ actions and reactions to situations and use
what they have learnt about the character as a person to make
predictions about how he might act or react in another situation. They
ask, “How would a person like this character act when something like
this happens?”

 As readers read on and learn new information they think about how it
fits with their prediction. They use the new information to change their
prediction or make a new one if it doesn’t fit with their original
prediction*.*

**Consider varying points of view**

 Readers ask themselves, “Does this character/narrator know everything
that is going on in the story?” “Does he have a certain opinion that
might make him see things differently than other characters would?” They
use that to determine how well the reader can trust what the
narrator/character is saying to be the full truth.

 Readers consider what a character may not know that the reader does
and ask themselves what the character is probably thinking, given his
limited knowledge

**Using other strategies to support inferences**

 Readers use what they know about a character and about life to
visualise- “become” the character. The reader can actually dramatise the
character’s facial expressions, body language, and tone of voice.

 Readers ask themselves questions about characters as they read.
Sometimes the answers are not given in the text. Then readers use what
they know from the text and what they know from life to figure out the
answers for themselves. They say, “The answer MIGHT be…”

 Readers think about themselves and people they know to help them
understand characters.

**Using Text Evidence to Support Inferences about Characters**

 Readers must be able to support their ideas using information from the
text.

 They note places where the character’s thoughts, actions, etc. provide
support for the reader’s ideas about him/her.

 Readers often use a plan for keeping track of their ideas. They could
use post-it notes or a T-chart.

**Use Prompts to help students infer as you confer with them:**

What are you thinking about the character so far?

What kind of person is this character?

What do you think the character is thinking?

How do you think the character is feeling?

What does the character have to gain from his actions?

What do you think made the character act differently here?

What do you think the character learnt?

Is there anything that makes you think this narrator/character may not
be telling you the whole story/accurate information?

Based on the relationships between the characters in this story, what do
you think the character will do?

Based on what you know about the character so far, what do you think he
will do next?

**Inferring about CHARACTERS**

*A strategy that good readers use while reading fiction is to gather
information about the characters in the story. Good readers think*

*about the characters in the story and determine some of the*

*characters’ traits. Good readers also look for the ways that characters
change or grow or what they learn throughout the story. Doing these
things is a way of making an inference*.

*Authors don’t’ usually tell us a character’s traits. They rarely say,*

*“\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ is brave.” However, the author writes the story so
that*

*the reader can figure out if the character is brave.*

When making inferences about characters we need to examine the
character’s physical traits, inner traits, dialogue, and actions.
Students list examples from the text that fit each of these areas, then
make inferences about the character based on what they have listed.

**Physical Traits**

Words used to describe facial expression, body, clothing, hair, etc.

**Inner Traits**

Words that tell what the character is thinking (words not spoken) that
reflect mood, feelings, reactions and personality.

**Dialogue**

Consider dialogue between two characters. What does it reveal about a
character’s thoughts? What do you think the character is thinking when
he/she says those words?

**Action**

Review the character’s actions and think about why the character acts as
he does, predict what the character might do next, think about words or
phrases that describe the character’s behavior.

**Character’s Feelings**

The author shows the reader how the character is feeling by describing
the character’s behaviour. It’s then up to the reader, using their own
experience to figure out or infer how a character is feeling.

**BOOK DISCUSSION QUESTIONS**

**Setting**

What was unique about the setting of the book, and how did it enhance or
take away from the story?

**Theme**

What specific themes did the author emphasise throughout the book?

What do you think the author is trying to get across to the reader?

**Characters**

Do the characters seem real and believable? Are they convincing?

Can you relate to their predicaments?

To what extent do they remind you of yourself, or someone you know?

How do the characters change or evolve throughout the story?

What events trigger such changes?

Which characters do you particularly admire or dislike?

What are their primary characteristics?

Who in this book would you like to meet? What would you ask- or say?

What motivates a particular character’s actions? Do you think those

actions are justified?

If you could insert yourself as a character in the book, what role would
you play? Would you be a new character or take the place of an existing
one?

**Plot**

Is the plot well-developed? Is it believable? Do the plot events unfold
naturally?

Is the story plot or character driven? In other words, do events unfold
quickly? Or is more time spent developing characters’ inner lives?

Story beginning. Were you immediately drawn into the story?

Consider the ending. Did you expect it or were you surprised?

How would you describe the ending? Name it from your knowledge of
narrative endings.

If you could rewrite the ending would you? In other words, did you find
the ending satisfying? Why or why not?

**Talk with the Author**

If you could talk to the author, what would you want to know?

Have you read other books by this author? Is their any connection in
terms of theme, writing style, structure etc?

**Reactions to the book**

How did you experience the book? Did it intrigue, amuse, disturb,
alienate, irritate, or frighten you?

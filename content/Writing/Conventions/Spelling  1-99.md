Literacy Experience Relation to Spelling

Read-aloud Children may listen for words related to a spelling focus, For example, children
may listen for words that thyme or words that are plurals,

Shared reading Children may listen and look for words related to a spelling focus. For example,
children may listen for words with a specific sound, or may find words with a
particular spelling pattern or suffix.

Guided reading Children may return to the text to find examples of words relating to a class
spelling exploration, or teachers may focus on the Specific spelling needs of the
Particular group. For example, beginning readers may be asked to locate a high-
frequency word and learn it, and more experienced writers may be asked to find
examples of homophones from a particular set.

independent reading Children may notice examples of words related to the class exploration or par-
ticular words they are personally interested in.

Independent writing Children use all the strategies they have learned in other reading and writing ex-
periences. Teachers assess children’s spelling strengths and needs and plan suit-

+ able spelling work for the other literacy experiences.

Shared writing Spelling strategies can be demonstrated by the teacher and suggested by the
children,

Interactive writing Spelling strategies can be demonstrated by the teacher and attempted by the
children.

Guided writing Teacher writes with groups of children with common spelling needs,

Eight Principles of Literacy Learning

1. Children need to understand the Purposes of literacy
so they can fully appreciate and enjoy literacy in their
lives.

2. Children need to hear written language so they can
learn its structure and take in new information and
ideas.

3. Children need to become aware of the sounds of
language, to enjoy those sounds, and to use this
knowledge as a tool in becoming literate.

4. Children need to have many experiences working
with written symbols so they can learn how to look
at letters and use this information to read and write.

5. Children need to explore words and learn how words
work so they can use this information effectively and
efficiently in reading and writing.

6. Children need to learn the conventions of print and how
books work so they can use this knowledge as readers

. and writers.

7. Children need to read and write continuous text so they
can use and expand their knowledge about letters,
sounds, words, and language.

8. Children need to develop flexibility and fluency to
enhance comprehension and enjoyment of reading and
writing.

Our challenge as educators is to Provide a rich and
balanced program that offers a full range of learning
opportunities for all children.

Principles and Practices of Spelling K-8
Overa!l Goals
* understand that the primary Purpose for learning about spelling
is so that others can read their writing
* know that their writing is valued regardless of the stage of
development of their spelling
* develop an interest in words and spelling and want to do their
best
* learn how to apply spelling strategies that will help them to write
or learn any word
* learn specific words that they use frequently and so become able
to correctly spell these words automatically
* know how to use a variety of resources to help with spelling
Principles and Practices
Frequent Purposeful Writing
Teachers should help children to think, “What kind of writing will I do
today?” or “Who will I write something for?”
Opportunities for Children to Read Each Other's Writing
Teachers should allow time for children to read each other’s writing in
all curriculum areas. Children also need to frequently select writing to
publish so that there is a purpose for learning how to proofread. The
classroom library should have examples of children’s published work.
Many Opportunities to See and Read Print
Growth in children’s spelling is greatly influenced by the amount and
range of reading and writing they do.
Assessing Children’s Writing to Inform Teaching
Observation of the kinds of strategies which children are using on
unfamiliar words guides teachers to the focus for spelling and word
study.

Modeling Spelling Strategies
In modeled, shared and interactive writing there should be
demonstrations of ways to work out how to spell words, how to use
various resources to help with spelling, and how to proofread to check
spelling.
Encouraging Risk Taking
No one knows how to spell all words in the language they use to write.
Invented spelling is temporary spelling. One of the misconceptions
about invented spelling is that it means that teachers don’t teach
Spelling. That is not so. We encourage children to try interesting and
colorful words in their writing and then we teach strategies which help
- them gain a command of conventional spelling, as they are writing.
A Process of Inquiry
Problem solving with words is a powerful way of learning how
to spell,
1. Talk about the purpose of the inquiry e.g. spelling focus.
2. Use class reading materials to find and list examples of words
containing the spelling focus.
3. Have the children find further examples from material they can
read.
4, Guide children to categorize the examples to see what can be
learned from them.
5. Guide children to form hypotheses based on their examples.
6. Demonstrate how to use the new knowledge or strategy during
shared or interactive writing.
Use Children’s Writing to Demonstrate Strategies
Every child can learn something from every other child in the class,
and you can develop a community of learners where each child’s
strengths are valued and children know what the others are working
on.
Focus on Strategies
Teach children strategies which they can try rather than lists of words.
To develop competent readers we teach children to use many different
strategies to help them determine whether a word looks right, sounds

Every Teacher Teaches Spelling
Writing and reading occur in most curriculum areas, so spelling needs
to be dealt with by most teachers.
Informing Parents
Parents need to understand the purpose of teaching strategies rather

charts, signs, posters, directions, lists, messages, labels
* class-made and commercial big books, fiction and non-fiction

Spelling in Perspective
Society values and expects good spelling, but the teaching of spelling
should not be treated in such a way that students’ reluctance to make
spelling mistakes turns into a fear of writing. Children need to be
taught a variety of strategies to tackle unfamiliar words so that there
is growth in their spelling towards the conventional form.

Writing- Students will be involved in a meaningful and
stimulating writing program.
They will be writing every day for different audiences
and purposes across the curriculum.
They will have time to plan, draft, revise, edit, publish
and share their writing.
Editing- — Proof-reading. Students underline spelling.
Have-a-go strategy.
An important strategy to encourage students to be
risk-takers, Have-a-go demonstrates that it is okay
to attempt a word, and look closer at it later. At the
appropriate time (proof-reading), the student can
consciously think about correct spelling - a thinking
process that is best done at a time when it doesn't
interfere with composing - and also doesn't force the
student to use safe words.
Spelling Journal
A spelling journal ensures that we are linking spelling
to writing, It includes:
- words from own writing
- words negotiated from have-a-go
- high frequency words
- class focus words
- interesting words
- topic words
A spelling journal develops a bank of known words.
Learning words.
The ‘look, say, spell, cover, write, check’ method is one
strategy for learning words using memory.

- visual patterns — - meaning

A Shared Reading Approach to Teaching Spelling -
1 What skill, strategy or feature of the language do the students
need to learn about now? You should use the scope and
sequence chart and your understanding of the writing which
the students are doing now, Identify their needs.
2. Know your big books, now your stories/articles on transparencies,
Choose a text which best covers the word study you want to teach,
. This is the time for the whole clase to focus on one piece of text,
either from a big book or from a transparency.
3. Identify, with the students, the words you want them to learn
about e.g. compound words, words with an ‘ed’ ending, plurals,
words which begin with ‘b’, words with the ‘e’ sound.
Make a list of these words as you read the familiar big book or
article or story,
4. Explore the words, Thig segment of the lesson provides a time
for the students to talk and learn. Explore any connections or
links which the students may make. It is appropriate, at this
Point, to reorganize the list you have made into a different,
but perhaps, more logical order, This helps students to attend
to the features of these worde in detail, The order can be suggested
by the teacher or the students. There can be suggestions for more
than one way of listing. Students love this segment of word study.

5. It may be appropriate for a rule to be established at this
point, especially if the studente discover a rule. It should be
written with the students, ag they work to make the language
clear,

6 Encourage the students to add to the list. Words will be found
around the room, at home, in library book, in newspapers and
magazines. Make this a rewarding, exciting experience.

2. The words may be written on cardg and a game could be played
with therm, during the literacy center/activity time.

&. The chart, listing the words, should be displayed in the classroom, for
use during writing time. Students may wish to put the words in

GAMES & ACTIVITIES

Though it is clearly our basic classroom Structures for reading and writing that have the greatest impact
on the children we teach, and no game or activity can begin to substitute for knowing our children well as
readers and writers, games can play a useful role in a balanced program. Thoughtfully used games can
spark children's interest, provide variety, and reinforce important skills. Their use can also provide
valuable practice in thinking, following directions, problem-solving, and other social and cognitive skills,
There are many commercially made games that can be very useful in developing these skills: Split Words,
Boggle, Spill ‘N Spell, ete. Designing and making your own has the advantage of addressing the Specific
*ognitive and developmental needs and interests of your students,
These games and activities are very simple to make and use, requiring little more than index cards and a
good set of markers. Any playing boards that made sense to include are here, though many are just models
for you to use and customize for your own purposes in meeting the needs of your kids. They shouldn't
require too much time to prepare, as we know that your precious time is better spent with your children
and their work, puzzling out where they need to go next. ECLAS results, coupled with other kinds of
observation and assessment can help you decide which of your children might benefit from each of the
activities described here. Virtually all of them are of such a flexible design that you can vary the
specifics depending on your purposes,
Many teachers enlist the support of parent volunteers in making games. It's a great way for parents who
‘ant to help out, but can't be in the classroom during the day, to pitch in and feel like a part of classroom
life. What's more, they'll often take the time to make them especially attractive,
Some things to consider when choosing or designing a game include:

1. Is the game clear, uncluttered, and easily understood?

2. Does the game truly instruct a child or reinforce a skill that needs to be mastered?

3. What is the student mastering by playing this game?

4. Do all the players have enough knowledge to play the game, and is the level appropriate?

5. Does play require a task useful to reading ability?

6. Is the learning gained relevant to classroom instruction?

7. Is play clearly more useful than actually reading at this time?
It's important to remember, that no game or activity will take the place of those precious stretches of time
to allow for our children to delight in the pleasures of actual reading and writing of their own choosing.

Source: Reading: The Name of the Game, by Pearl Zeitz in The Reading Teacher

SORTING WORDS
Sorting words is a simple activity that helps children really focus on very specific features of words,
Current sight word lists, or cumulative ‘word wall’ words are a good source of words to use,
To begin, write about a dozen words on large index cards and ask children to write them on separate
pieces of paper at their tables, Then ask them to sort the words into different groups depending on some
characteristic those words have in common. Children might make groups of words with a certain number
of letters, for example, or all words with a particular letter anywhere in them. They might make groups
where all words begin with the same letter or blend, or those that end with a particular letter or blend.
Where appropriate, children might sort longer words by the number of syllables they contain. Later, sorting
words by what they mean or semantic features can be a challenging and vocabulary-expanding activity,
Descriptive words, action words, even things found in the park or at the beach might make good
categories.
The teacher may choose to tell children the *rule® by which to sort (eg. “Group all the words that begin
with ‘th’") Other times, the teacher may list words for children (e.g. hat, eat, that, sit) and challenge them
to “Guess my rule!” In this case, the words all end in the letter 't’, though children might have had a hunch
they were all going to be ‘_at’ words,
Once you've used this activity with your children a few times, they enjoy taking over as the activity
leader, choosing the words to sort, or the “rule” for sorting.
Source: Phonics They Use, by Patricia Cunningham
and Word Matters by Gay Su Pinnell and Irene Fountas

Learning about spelling Diane Snowball and Faye Bolton
The following article provides a brief sumunary of the processes for learning the main aspects
about spelling, It is intended to as a guide for teachers who wish to try to approach the teaching of
spelling in a way that involves the students in an active participation in their own learning.
(For more details refer to Spelling K-8: Plannirig and Teaching by Diane Snowball and Faye
Bolton, Stenhouse Publishing)
Learning High-Frequency Words
The 100 most frequently used words make up about half of the writing that is done by all authors,
. including children. Here are some ways to help students learn to spell these words, but do not
focus on this until children are at the stage of writing where some words are being spelled the
conventional way.
Involve students in locating the words
To underscore the idea of high-frequency words, have students keep a list of the words they
encounter most often in their own reading and writing. Also carefully observe students' writing,
and if see they are consistently misspelling the same high-frequency word, say, "I notice you are
using that word often in your writing, so let's find out and learn the correct spelling.” It may be
very difficult for students to break the habit ofa misspelling if they do it for a few weeks. Make
learning high-frequency words an early priority, along with trying unknown words, as it’s also
important for children to bé risk-takers.
Later supply them with a chart like of the 100 Most Common High-Frequency Words which
students can keep in their writing folder or notebook or display it on their desk. They can refer to
the list when writing and check off each word as they learn it. You could have older studénts
* check early in the year if they know how to spell all high-frequency words. If not, they need to set
personal goals to learn them.
Learning the words and developing a word wall
Words are selected one at a time for children to learn. The word is written on a chart and thildren
talk about anything that surprises them about the spelling of the word. They may notice smaller
words within the word or think of another known word with a similar spelling pattern. Use the
following strategy to help children learn the word:
LOOK at the word carefully so that you can picture it when your eyes are closed,
SAY the word.
SPELL the word, almost chanting the letters.
COVER the word and picture it in your mind.
WRITE the word.
CHECK the spelling, letter by letter.
This is done several times over until the children can write the word quickly and automatically. It
is helpful if they have individual erasable boards for this so they can write and erase the word
, Many times. Then add the word to 4 word wall that is preferably displayed in an area where you
can easily refer to the words for many future word activities, When suitable ask children to try to
make new words just by changing the beginning of the known word, such as play — may, May,
stay, day, way, bay, stray ot by adding endings such as Play — plays, playing, played.

Learning New Words for Writing
A seven-year-old recently told me that instead of the whole class learning the same spelling
words, students would be better off learning the words they wanted to use for their own writing.
Smart kid! So how do.we help children select and learn relevant words? They need to learn how
to proof-read, how to choose appropriate words, and how to best learn those words. These tasks
can all be demonstrated by using overheads of samples of children’s writing, Inyolve the child
writer in the process and tell the class that they can all Jearn about how to do the same things with
their own writing.
. Proofreading
As soon as children are spelling some words conventionally, encourage them to proofread,
underline words they think are wrong, and then try to get them right, Children may try each word
two or three ways and then use a resource to check the spellings. Help them to use a variety of
resources, including books and people, because a dictionary or a wordbook is not useful unless
they are sure about how the word begins. They also need to know how much of this correcting to
do because this task may be so daunting that children will never want to write again. There has to
be a sensible balance between caring or not caring about getting it tight.
Choosing Words
From their proofreading (and class word lists, if appropriate), children select a group of words
they think will be useful for future writing. Also indicate one or two other words a child may
want to lear because you have noticed these misspelled in the students’ writing. Help children to
jearn about making wise choices of words to learn.

**Author Studies**

An author study allows students to take the opportunity to explore the
author’s themes, characters, and writing style through reading
selections of the author’s books.

An author study involves making a reading and writing connection between
a selection of books by a particular author and the writer's life. The
goal is to improve writing skills by studying the author's writing style
and techniques. The author becomes a writing mentor for the reader.
Texts from the author studied are kept in the students’ Mentor Text
Folder for use during the study and beyond.

**Understandings:**

Knowing about the lives of published authors often serves to motivate
students to read and write.

Learning about the work of a published author improves skills,
knowledge, and pleasure in both reading and writing.

The craft of writing can be improved by studying the writing of
published authors.

Spoken language develops by listening to and talking about experiences,
events, and books.

**Essential Questions:**

1\. What things in the author’s life influenced his/her writing? (How do
the life experiences influence an author’s writing?)

2\. Where do writers get their ideas for writing?

3\. How does knowing about an author’s background help me understand her
writing?

4\. How do the illustrations in a book help me understand the story?

**Information about the author’s life** enhances the study. For example:

- Author's background-life & interests.

- Interview transcripts.

- How did the author get ideas for the stories?

- Why did the author use certain writing craft?

- What themes emerge from the texts?

- Is the author working on any new stories?

- Which genres does the author write in?

- What advice does the author have for writers?

Students will be able to use the internet to research:

- Lists of published books.

- Biographical sketches.

- Interview transcripts.

- Book reviews.

- Art.

Writing Craft studied will be apparent in the students’ independent
writing.

- Author’s writing style: use of imagery, figurative language etc

**Author Study**

Information

You should take notes on what you find. You should try to learn as much
as you can about your author. Become an expert.

**Professional:**

- What are the names of all the books your author has completed?

- Which was the first book written?

- Have any of the books won awards?

- What other kinds of jobs has the author had?

- Does the author have a favourite book they have written? Why is it
  their favourite?

- Which part of creating books comes easily to your author? Which parts
  are more difficult?

- Is there anything that is a special inspiration to your artist?

**Personal:**

- When was your author born? How old is the author? If dead, when did
  your author die? How old were they?

- Where does your author live?

- Does your author have a family? children?

- Does your author have any pets?

- Does the author have any special interests besides writing books?

- Can you give examples of the artist’s family, pets, personal
  experience, or interests appearing in or affecting his or her work?

- In a few sentences describe what your author does in books that makes
  them different from any other author.

> (In other words, be able to describe the artist’s style and favourite
> themes or genres.)

**Assignment**:

Read and analyse 3 - 4 books by your author.

Learn about your author and share your information. Think about how you
will share it – I.T./Book/Poster.

Compare/Contrast the story grammars completed for each story. Look for
common elements/threads in each of the stories read. List these elements
and discuss an author's style.

Write an original story using what you have learnt about this author's
writing style.

**Author Studies**

**How to choose authors for author studies:**

\* Author has several books available: in print and/or in the library

\*Author has several books in the same genre

\*Books that are age appropriate and engage readers emotionally and/or
intellectually (Books the students will read!)

\*Books that evoke a range of responses

\*Books that connect to students’ lives

\*Books with memorable language

\* Author has distinctive identifiable features to his or her writing
like Chris Van Allsburg’s surprise endings, Kevin Henkes’ characters,
Katherine Paterson’s language

\*Autobiographical and/or biographical information available about the
author

\*Select quality literature: avoid series books or books with formulaic
plots and shallow characters. What do you want writers to reach
towards**?**

Themes must be inferred.

Themes are revealed by the way characters change in the story, conflicts
in the story, and statements made by narrator or characters. They are
still open to interpretation. Understanding the themes therefore comes
through understanding plot, characters, and setting.

> **QARS - Question-Answer-Relationship-Strategy**
>
> Demonstrates to students that there are different types of questions
> and possible places for finding answers to those questions.
>
> The strategy help students:

- To anticipate questions

- To find answers to questions

- To use questions to review reading

Steps:

- Introduce concept of QARS by explaining each type. Provide examples;
  discuss differences.

- Students read short text.

- Lead students through the process of answering each type of
  questioning. Emphasise going back into the text to verify answers.

- Practise.

Group work

- Review important concepts and information from reading.

- Each group generates two questions for each category.

- Exchange questions with another group.

- Work collaboratively to answer questions.

|                                                    |                                    |                                                                                                      |
|----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **The Question-Answer-Relationship-Strategy QARS** |                                    |                                                                                                      |
| **Kind of Question**                               | **Textually Explicit or Implicit** | **Characteristics**                                                                                  |
| Right there                                        | Explicit                           | Answer stated directly in the text                                                                   |
| Think and Search                                   | Implicit                           | Answer requires students to put together several sections of the text                                |
| Author and You                                     | Implicit and Explicit              | Answer requires that students mesh their thoughts about a topic with information the author presents |
| On Your own                                        | Implicit                           | Answer comes from application of the information from the text                                       |

Craft:

**Show not Tell.**

Although we tell the students to ‘show not tell’, in all writing,
whether narrative or poetry, writers use a combination of showing and
telling. When a writer ‘shows’, he/she tends to slow down and develop
the moment. This engages the reader, enabling any connections to be
made.

Writing Small.

The students at Wembley all use a Writer’s Notebook. Part of using this
has been to write important little details they observe or hear about.
E.g.

Read *The Summer My Father Was Ten (Read Along or Independent Reading)*
to discover the important little details.

Students write using all their senses. This was done when using *Home
Place*. By doing this, noticing details about the things around them,
the students will write small.

In other work we have used Georgia Heard’s notion of ‘cracking open’
words.

…‘*writers “crack open” their first words and drafts to get to the
“crystal part” of their writing… Words like fun, nice, pretty,
wonderful, and scary are all generic words that don’t describe anything
specific for the reader or give the reader any particular picture in her
mind.’*

When they have these words, the students close their eyes and re- vision
by describing what they see in their minds using words. Once again they
use all their senses, and the end product is writing small.

These words might be:

Vague, abstract words.

Words with no images.

Clichés.

Words that the student uses too many times.

Obvious words.

Doing this will breathe life into the students’ writing. It will also
help to produce Voice.

**Further thoughts:**

**PRIOR KNOWLEDGE**

*Students use what they already know to help understand something new.*

Prior knowledge means combining current understandings and past
experiences to help anticipate and comprehend the deeper meaning of
text.

Prior knowledge is used before, during and after reading.

Prior knowledge increases readers' ability to assimilate new information
from text into what they already know.

Prior knowledge clarifies misunderstandings and develops deeper
understanding of the text.

The more students know about a topic, the better they comprehend and
learn from the text.

**How to help children to activate their prior knowledge.**

- Prompt students to think about what they know about the topic prior to
  reading.

- Students predict what the text may be about based on the title.

- Students predict what the text may be about based on the
  illustrations.

Students think about everything they know related to the topic/text.

Students quickly write in note form as many ideas as they can.

Students discuss their ideas in small groups before they read.

**Making Connections**

Readers relate what they read to personal experiences (text-to-self), to
information from other text (text-to-text), and to information about the
world (text-to-world) in order to enhance understanding.

Students make connections before, during and after reading.

Connections are made by reacting to the text and combining it with
previous knowledge from text, personal experience or world knowledge to
build, change or revise understanding.

**QUESTIONING**

Readers ask questions about the text and the author's purpose and seek
information to clarify and extend their thinking before, during and
after reading. They ask questions to understand what is being read.

Generating questions about the text provides a purpose for reading and
enhances understanding.

Questioning is done for a variety of purposes.

i\) clarify meaning

ii\) speculate about text

iii\) determine author's style, purpose, content or format

iv\) focus attention on specific components of the text

v\) locate a specific answer

vi\) reflect on ideas inspired by the text

The text and prior knowledge are used to generate questions that provide
a purpose for reading and enhance interaction with the text.

Students generate a variety of questions for a variety of purposes.

> Including answers that are in the text, inferred from the text, and
> beyond the text

**VISUALISING**

*Students create a movie in their minds while reading**.***

*The mental imagery that we experience while reading, either
spontaneously or induced by instruction, is now known to have powerful
effects on comprehension, memory, and appreciation for text."*

*—Mark Sadoski (Sadoski, 1998)*

Visualisation is the creation of images that may include any of the five
senses in the student’s mind that reflect/represent the text read to
enhance understanding.

Visualising is used before, during and after reading.

Visualising helps the reader go deeper into the text and makes it come
alive.

When visualising, the reader uses sensory and emotional clues from the
text and from prior knowledge to create a picture of the story in the
mind.

During the author studies we will be using a large number of picture
books. Written text and illustrations work together to convey meaning.

**Artistic Response – Visual Art**

**To create artistic representations based on a text.**

Students read the text and create an artistic representation of the
text. Students use artwork in any medium (drawing, collage, sculpting,
painting etc), music, drama (including choral reading, readers’ theatre,
puppets etc.) or dance.

Artist shares what the representation means, where it came

from, or what it represents to him.

**Guided Imagery - To create mental images**

Read a descriptive passage to students, pausing occasionally to share
mental images and emotions. Select a rich text that helps create images
and emotions using all five senses.

Students close their eyes, breathe deeply and relax as they listen to a
descriptive passage.

Ask students what they see, hear, taste, smell and feel.

What words helped them create the mental image and emotions.

**INFERRING** See recently revised Inferring course.

Readers think about and search the text, and sometimes use personal
knowledge to construct meaning beyond what is literally stated. That is
they use clues to figure out what the text really means.

Inferring is used while reading the text and uses all the other
strategies.

Inferring expands understanding by helping the reader discover what is
implied but not explicitly stated by the author and/or illustrator.

Keywording

When summarising the reader selects the important ideas. Summarising is
also part of Synthesising.

Summarising:

Readers identify key elements and condense important information into
their own words during and after reading to gain meaning. I.e. Tell
what’s important.

Summarising is done during and after reading.

Reading Response Journal

As a story is being read (individually or as a read-aloud), have
students discuss and write down important issues, themes and ideas in
their Reading Response Journals. Suggested teacher prompts:

• What was the focus of the reading selection?

• Which details are most and least significant?

• How can you use key ideas to condense the information in this story?

• Which words helped you get the gist of the story?

• What is important and essential to the text?

• Tell me about some of the important ideas that struck you.

Story Elements

Somebody/Wanted/But/So

• Somebody (identify the character)

• Wanted (describe the character’s goal)

• But (describe a conflict that hinders the character)

• So (describe the resolution of the conflict)

**SYNTHESISING**

Readers create original insights, perspectives and understandings by
reflecting on text(s) and putting together elements from text and
background knowledge. That is they put the pieces together to see them
in a new way.

Synthesising is done while responding to a text.

Synthesising helps the reader stimulate original thought to create new
meaning beyond the text.

When synthesising, the reader merges new information from the text with
current understandings to create original ideas.

**Use strategies** **together** to understand what is read.

We introduce these strategies one at a time by modelling our own use of
each one. Then we give students time to practice each strategy. We are
aware, however, that these strategies do not occur in isolation.
Strategic readers are connecting, inferring, questioning, visualizing,
and synthesising continually as they read.

—Stephanie Harvey and Ann Goudvis, from Strategies That Work: Teaching
Comprehension to Enhance Understanding

The Poetry Environment
Read poetry to the class throughout the week - not just in poetry lessons.
It only takes a few minutes to read a poem.
Have an abundance of poetry books in the class library.
Have copies of poetry students can choose for their own anthologies.
Help students to find poems to use as models.
Allow students to memorise their favorite poems and to recite them.
Make Choral Reading and Readers’ Theatre a core part of the program.
During Shared Reading use group and whole class choral reading as a way of
enjoying poetry.
Show your enjoyment of poetry - both reading and writing.
Perform poetry with the class:
- Readers’ Theatre
- Choral Reading
- Poetry for two vaices
- Combine music and poetry
Have students keep a Writer's Notebook
- Collect thoughts
- Sketch
- Collect words/phrases
Talk about the poetry in song lyrics.

Georgia Heard in Awakening the Heart says
A classroom environment can send out messages: that all of our

Choral Reading
Choral reading using a familiar and well-crafted text: reconstructing
favorite lines into a performance poem. Any genre can be used.
Each student has access to the text.
“Highlight the parts where you think the writing is best. Find the part that
is most striking to you (no more than fifteen words). Practice reading this
part in your best read-aloud voice.
Come to the reading circle ready to read your selection.
I hope several people have chosen the same part so we have a repeating
refrain in our performance. (This dispels the worry about choosing the same
as someone else.)
Each student reads in turn.
This gets the students to think about the writing in the text. They have to
really listen to the text as they practice and others read. It develops good
writing in their heads, which flows into their writing.
Extension: Small-group work. Each group is to craft q choral reading from
the chosen lines of their group. They can only work with those lines, but can
manipulate them E.g. repeat them, break them up. This forces them to draft
and craft.
Ref: Wondrous Words - Katie Wood Ray

Reading Poetry

Possible weekly format.

Monday:

Read poem twice to class.

What does the poem make you feel?
Listen to the sounds and meanings of the words - what stands
out for you?
Does the poem remind you of anything in your own life?
What pictures do you see in your mind?

Tuesday:

Read poem again.

Students sketch, draw, or paint a picture of images the poem paints in
their minds.

Wednesday:

Read poem together as a class.
Thursday:

Ask students to bring in a personal response or connection to the
poem - a letter, a photo, an object, another poem, a book, a song, or anything
that will help them connect to the poem.

Friday:

Group reading. (Some may want to recite it.)
Possible interaction with poem:

Read other poems by the same author.

Read excerpts from the biography of the poet. Discuss how
circumstances, beliefs, and events of his/her life and the world around
him/her are reflected in the poetry.

What does the poet write about?

How would you describe the voice in the poetry?

What is the feeling or the mood of the poems?

How does the poet use line-breaks, rhyme, image, pattern?
Students underline part that feels especially true or memorable to them.
Explain why.

Write a real or imaginary letter to the poet telling or asking him/her
anything they'd like to know.
Write a call-and-response poem - like a poem for two voices.

Alliteration
Repeating consonant sounds in several words placed close together.
Assonance
Echoing of vowel sounds.
Onomatopoeia
Matching sounds to meaning.
- hiss, crash, thud imitate the sounds they describe.
Imagery
Vivid description
Simile
Comparison of two things. Use of ‘like’ or ‘as’.
Metaphor
States one thing is another.
‘The sea is a hungry dog,
Giant and grey.’
Rhyme
Repetition of the final sound of a word.
‘Used skillfully, rhyme can add to the music of poetry and help
the reader enjoy a stanza as a rhythmic whole, Used merely from a sense of
duty - because it's expected - rhyme can seern to trivialize the meaning of a
poem and weaken its impact’
Mandy Tunica

Metaphor
Metaphor is more subtle than simile. One thing is presented as
another, sometimes without the poet telling you what the original object is.
In this case we have to infer to work out the meaning.
Night gathers itself inte a ball of yarn.
Night loosens the ball and it spreads...
from Night by Carl Sandburg
Metaphor comes from a Greek word meaning to ‘transfer’ or ‘carry across’.
A metaphor carries one image across to another: seemingly unrelated.
Some poems are one single metaphor, other use a variety of different
metaphors to describe one thing.

Fact versus Opinion
A fact is information that can be documented or verified. It is true.
Opinions are personal beliefs, views or judgments, It is what a person feels.
New articles always use factual information.

Concept Definitions
Fact:

* A testable proposition.

* — Represents things which actually exist or which have happened in the
past. They can be verified as true or false. E.g. Wanita is wearing a
hat.

Opinion:
*  Astatement about a fact that alters it.
* A personal judgment about a fact.
Inference:

* The act or process of concluding from premises or evidence and
observable facts or statements.

* Derived from reasoning. E.g. That man is staggering because he is
drunk, The man is staggering, but the reason for his behavior is a
judgment.

Bias:

* A tendency or inclination which may be for or against.

* There may be support for a bias in terms of facts, logic etc. e.g. I
prefer milk to cola,

Prejudice:

* — Literally to judge in advance. Prejudice is a preconceived attitude
which is formed without consideration of the facts.

* Tt is an attitude of the mind.

Discrimination:

* — Action which results from prejudice. E.g. I did not promote a woman

to that job because women cannot handle stress.
Impartiality:

* Being unbiased. The subject displays no partiality. E.g. We will
promote the candidate who is qualified regardless of gender, color, or
religion.

(Adapted from Frankel. Teaching Students to Think and Value)

News Articles: Qualities of Writing
Accuracy: As the news article purports to be fact, the facts ~ statements,
names, quotes, dates, etc. must be verifiable. The overall impression must
also be accurate - the way the details are put together and the emphasis
given to these details. The event or situation should be reported accurately,
with carefully selected details and proper emphasis.
Balance: = Closely related to accuracy, facts are given proper emphasis
and are placed in proper relation to other facts. The reporter writes a
complete story, presenting both sides of an issue.
Objectivity: the news article is a factual report. Bias should be evident,
with the reporter being honest and impartial.
Concise and Clear: This is aided by the inverted pyramid approach to
writing mentioned above:

Headline.

Lead. This paragraph tells the most important elements of the story
in a nutshell, in a manner everyone can understand.
Concise writing cuts out unnecessary words which are not essential to the
meaning of the article.
Timing: A news article is about recent events.
Active Voice:
Active voice sentence structure is subject-verb-object.
In most cases it is simpler and easier to understand.
The exception is when the object of the sentence is more
important/newsworthy than the subject.
Tn active voice, the subject is doing the action. Mostly this means that the
person or thing doing the action appears first in the sentence. Doer (the
subject) first, then the verb.

Tn passive voice, the target of the action is the subject.
Not all sentences containing the verb fo be are in the passive voice. E.g. I
am holding the bat. In the passive voice this would be The bat is being held
by me. The sentence is passive if the subject isn't taking a direct action.
Passive voice isn't incorrect - it's just not the best way to write thoughts.
Emphasis needs to be on the subject, which sometimes can be left out in the
passive voice.
Crime reporting sometimes uses the passive voice because nobody knows who
the criminal was. The store was robbed.
Other reporting using passive voice might be reporting injuries, deaths and
natural disasters. Passive voice is often used here because the subject has
been the victim of the action. Three children were trapped in the burning
house.
Important also not to confuse passive voice with past tense just because of
the verb used. E.g. Wembley was the winner of the swimming sports.
Leads:
As with other writing, the lead is the most important part, in order to gain
the readers’ attention. It contains the most important elements of the
story, but not the whole story. Details can be told later in the article.
‘What happened’ should be the basis of the introduction. This will come
from our 5 W's and H; but not all these are in the introduction. Otherwise
the sentences will be averloaded and confuse the readers. One or two key
points per sentence. We should remember:
5 W's + H- not all in introduction.
Active voice.
Facts first. Ask “How does this affect my readers’ lives?” The
answer is the heart of the article.
No quotes. Do not begin the article with a quote. It is important to
know who is speaking.

How to Detect Blas in the News :

At one time or other we all complain about “bias in the news.” The NEWS:

fact is, despite the journalistic ideal of "objectivity," every news ante

story is influenced by the attitudes and background of its & .

interviewers, writers, photographers and editors. \F

Not all bias is deliberate. But you can become a more aware news W

reader or viewer by watching for the following journalistic NOT JUST

techniques that allow bias to "creep in” to the news: BLACK & WHITE

Bias through selection and omission

An editor can express a bias by choosing to use or not to use a specific news item.

Within a given story, some details can be ignored, and others included, to give

readers or viewers a different opinion about the events reported. If, during a speech,

a few people boo, the reaction can be described as "remarks greeted by jeers” or they

can be ignored as "a handful of dissidents."

Bias through omission is difficult to detect. Only by comparing news reports from a

wide variety of outlets can the form of bias be observed.

Bias through placement

Readers of papers judge first page stories to be more significant than those buried in

the back. Television and radio newscasts run the most important stories first and

leave the less significant for later. Where a story is placed, therefore, influences what

a reader or viewer thinks about its importance.

Bias by headline

Many people read only the headfines of a news item. Most people scan nearly all the

headlines in a newspaper. Headlines are the most-read part of a paper. They can

summarize as well as present carefully hidden bias and prejudices. They can convey

excitement where little exists. They can express approval or condemnation.

Bias by photos, captions and camera angles

Some pictures flatter a person, others make the person look unpleasant. A paper can

choose photos to influence opinion about, for example, a candidate for election. On

television, the choice of which visual images to display is extremely important. The

captions newspapers run below photos are also potential sources of bias.

Bias through use of names and titles

News media often use labels and titles to describe people, places, and events. A

person can be called an “ex-con" or be referred to as someone who "served time

twenty years ago for a minor offense." Whether a person is described as a “terrorist”

or a “freedom fighter" is a clear indication of editorial bias.

Bias through statistics and crowd counts

To make a disaster seem more spectacular (and therefore worthy of reading about),

numbers can be inflated. "A hundred injured in aircrash" can be the same as "only

minor injuries in air crash," reflecting the opinion of the person doing the counting.

Bias by source control

To detect bias, always consider where the news item "comes from." Is the

information supplied by a reporter, an eyewitness, police or fire officials, executives, v

or elected or appointed government officials? Each may have a particular bias that is ~y

introduced into the story. Companies and public relations directors supply news 8

outlets with puffpieces through news releases, photos or videos. Often news outlets t

depend on pseudo-events (demonstrations, sit-ins, ribbon cuttings, speeches and v

ceremonies) that take place mainly to gain news coverage. r

legitimate news. Some of the ploys used are:
Facts. Newspapers should report both sides, but not when it comes to
technical or scientific matter which requires someone with training and
expertise to speak knowledgably.

If you're going to write about a scientific matter, you need to bring
data, gathered by people who know what they are talking about.
Watch for articles where opinions are given by people not qualified.
Catchy nickname. gate.

Passing advertisements off as news.

Guessing the motives instead of reporting the facts. The reporter

Text Features

Students need to know that they can read nonfiction in different ways.

What features do you see on the page?

What information can we get from that feature?

What is this article about? or What is the main idea of this article?

FEATURE ARTICLE
What is a Feature Article?
> Found in newspapers and magazines. It is different froma
newspaper report or letter to the editor in that it is not topical
- that is, not related to current news.
> Provides detailed information on one aspect of a narrowed topic
- it has an angle. The article must be about something - not all
about something, but something about something, It should
contain a main message, a central idea or major theme. It is not
a fact sheet or an encyclopaedia entry, It should make one big,
overriding point - but not more than one.
>» Wellresearched. This is reflected in the details used.
> May have a persuasive tone, depending on the author's bias,
> Plays on the interests and emotions of the reader.
Purpose
> To explore or discuss a particular topic of interest.
> Clear angle. It demonstrates the opinions of the writer.
Structure
> Lead that grabs the reader's attention. Leads may be:
- Anecdotes (brief story)
- Vignettes (vivid description of the setting, sensory
details)
- Quotes ~ from authorities
- Familiar sayings or play on words that connects to the
reader
- Surprising statistics or facts
- Question or questions for the reader
- Anunusual statement
- Necessary background information
> Justifier
This paragraph at the end of the lead establishes the angle or
focus. It hints at what the reader will find out by reading the
article.

---

<!-- page 48 -->

> Body
- Contains the main ideas related to the article's angle.
- The main ideas are described, explained and developed
with details.
> Conclusion
- Makes a point
- Could make use of a strategy similar to the lead.
- May leave the reader something to think about or
connect back to the lead.
- May offer advice or a warning based on the opinions
expressed in the article.
The Language of Feature Articles
> Personal tone - first person narrative. The tone will vary from
article to article, depending on the topic and the writer. It can
be humorous and informal to appeal to the audience.
> Anecdotes (short narratives) ~ maintain the reader's interest.
Anecdotes must add ‘human interest’. Ask “What is going on
here that is interesting or entertaining?"
> Facts - validate the writer's viewpoint. Facts add content.
They should be evenly spread through the article. Facts answer
the questions who, what, where, when, why and how.
> Humor if appropriate.
> Rhetorical questions - help involve the reader.
> Emotive words can be used to evoke a personal response in the
reader.
> Effective use of imagery and description engages the reader's
imagination.
> Quotes.
- The use of direct quotes personalises the topic.
- They reveal something about a person or issue.
- Sometimes you need to paraphrase. That is, restating or
interpreting the source's words.
- Show not tell.
> Catchy headline. E.g. use a pun.

Idea Development and Support
» Main ideas are explained and described through:
- Descriptions
- Explanations
- Comparisons or analogies
- Quotations
- Statistics
- Bulleted information
- Use of specific facts.
» Demonstrates evidence of research - correct information that
is clearly explained and described.
> “Shows” rather than “tells”. The article should let the reader
see - and hear and smeil and fell - for himself. It should always
be showing the reader something, making pictures form in the
reader's mind. This will more likely hold the reader's attention.
Hints for Writing Feature Articles
> Choose a topic that you know a lot about.
>» Remember it is your opinion.
> Decide upon the tone before you start. It is your decision as to
how your article is presented. E.g. will it be humorous or
serious. Think about the topic. Which is the best approach?
> Your voice should come through.
» Feature articles often have images or photographs. A
photograph needs to capture the attention of the reader.
One Planning Model
Write an anecdote that captures your reader's attention, and makes a point
that illustrates your theme. (Lead)
State your theme implicitly or explicitly. (Justifier)
Illustrate your theme with facts, quotes and anecdotes. (Body)
Make sure you are writing using your voice - passion and tone.
End in a way that will leave your reader with a lasting impression.
(Conclusion)

Further Tips for Writing Feature Articles

A feature article differs from a straight news story in one respect - its
intent. A news story provides information about an event, idea or situation.
The feature article does a bit more - it may interpret news, instruct or
entertain.

Structure:

The introduction (lead) is the most important part, as this hooks the reader
in. Use drama, emotion, quotations, questions, descriptions.

The body of the article.

The conclusion should be written to help the reader remember the story ~
use a strong punchline.

Keep in Mind:

Focus on human interest. Even if the article is science based, don't think
about writing a ‘science’ story - think about writing a ‘human interest’ story.
Be clear about why you are writing the article, Is it to inform, persuade,
entertain or a combination of several?

Accuracy is important.

Keep your audience in mind. What matters to them? Remember when
writing that the audience might be YOU.

Use anecdotes and direct quotes.

When using people as resources, use more than one person.

Decide on the tense of your story and stick to it. The present tense usually
works best.

Ideas come from everywhere.

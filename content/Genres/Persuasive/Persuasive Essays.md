Persuasive Essay
Introduction:
Remember all the work we've done on leads - the need to grab your
reader. This is just as important in persuasive writing.
Think of some possibilities. Which works best for your topic?
e Unusual detail.

Manitoba, because of its cold climate, is not thought of
as a great place to be a reptile. Actually, it has the largest
seasonal congregation of garter snakes in the world.

e Strong statement.

Of all the problems facing the environment today, the

one that bothers me the most is global warming.

© Quotation. Direct quotes from leading experts. The following
quotes are from nelson Mandela. (Note the use of these algo in
our Poetry course).

Education is the most powerful weapon which you can use
to change the world.

How people treat you is their karma, how you react is
yours,

There is no passion to be found playing stnall - in settling
for a life that is less than the one you are capable of living.

e Anecdote. Needs to be short and to the point.

e Statistic or fact - to add interest or emphasis. It may also add
emphasis to include the authoritative source.

® Question.

Have you considered how many books wed read if it were
not for television?

e Exaggeration or outrageous statement.
The whole world watched as the comet flew overhead.

The introduction should include a focus statement.
This:
- tells the reader the specific topic
- imposes manageable limits
- suggests organisation
This is actually telling the reader - I've thought about this topic, I
know what I believe about it, and I know how to organize it.
Middle:
Evidence to support opinion. At least three paragraphs. Another
paragraph could be used to discuss opposing view and a counter
argument. If you understand the opposite viewpoint of your position,
you can counter it by providing contrasting evidence,
Possible techniques to explore:

e Facts - from reading, observations or personal experience.

e Using hypotheticals. Used when you're creating an argument
and you want to see a different point of view. When the reader
reads ‘what if..or ‘suppose that...', these provide a clue that a
hypothetical is on the way.

e Clarify a position,

e Draw comparisons. Use something similar to what is being
explained.

e Make an analysis. Analyse a problem by looking at the parts.
This can help the reader's understanding.

e Quotes.

e Statistics. Excellent support. Cite sources.

e Draw an analogy. This identifies significant likenesses between
two objects or ideas when otherwise they would be quite
different.

e Examples, These enhance the meaning, making ideas concrete.
They are the proof.

Conclusion:
Summarises most important details of the argument and
restates belief.
e Restate focus statement
e Summarise main points (paraphrase).
e Write personal comment or call for action. Again remember the
work we've done in Writers’ Workshop with endings.
Techniques to try:
End with:

° Aprediction. Conclusion may suggest or predict what the results
may or may not be in the situation discussed or in similar
situations.

e Aquestion. This lets the reader make their own prediction, draw
their own conclusions.

e Recommendation. This stresses the actions or remedies that
should be taken.

© Quotation. Quotations can summarise, predict, question, or call
for action; therefore can be used in any kind of text.

REMEMBER
e Have a firm opinion that you want to convince your reader to
accept.
© Write a lead that will grab your readers’ immediate attention.
e Use evidence to support your opinion.
e Conclude with a restatement of what you want the reader to do
or believe.

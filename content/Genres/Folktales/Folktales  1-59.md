FOLKTALES
Background.
Folktales - Stories told by the people. They began with an oral tradition,
written down many years later.
The primary reasons for folktales were to:

> Entertain

> Teach

> Recognise cultural and social mores

> Explain the creation of the world and its inhabitants.
There is such a wide range of subgenres within Folktales that it would be
possible to spread them across the school, not only to prevent a lot of
repetition, but to build on what has gone before and do more in-depth
studies, This allows for an increasingly sophisticated use of reading
strategies and writing craft.

> Cumulative Tales

> Animal Tales

> Wonder Tales

> Tall Tales

> Myths & Legends

>» Trickster Tales

>» Realistic Tales

> Pourquoi Tales

>» Fables

>» Droli/Humorous Tales

> Charles Perrault - 1691

We are not just studying Folktales as an end in themselves. Folktales are

read, analyse, and create them. Beyond this even is the fact that by
studying Folktales, students understand and use the power of story.

Through Folktales, the students can use analytical skills to understand any
story. They can then apply this to a wide range of narrative texts.

>» Characters who rarely change.

> Straightforward plots.

>» Direct language.

> Clear symbols.

Writing
Planning and writing folktales expands the students’ ability to think
abstractly about narrative text. Innovation on text enables students to
manipulate characters and conflicts. If a student changes a character, e.g.
- the bad witch becomes good
- the princess doesn't want to marry the prince

the students discover that by changing one element in a story, other
elements also need to change. By questioning and proposing answers,
students get a valuable insight into how elements of a story interact.
e.g. Hansel and Gretel.
Tf the witch is good then she won't want to eat Hansel and Gretel. Why then
does she want them to visit her house?

Literary Elements of Folktales.
Wonder stories and animals stories follow the narrative structure.
Patterned Beginning
This alerts the listener that a story was about to begin (from the oral
tradition of the storyteller). E.g. Once upon a time...
Children should know that if they use this, we, the readers, know what kind
of story is coming.
Setting
Usually the setting belonged to the audience listening to the story. It was
their forest, village or country. It is referred to as a backdrop setting (as
ina play). Although it is important, it does not affect the characters or
plot. Therefore the storyteller doesn't spend a lot of time developing the
setting.
Characters
Usually stereotypical - flat and not well-developed. Often very good or very
evil - beautiful and kind or ugly and bad. The main character is usually the
underdog.
Plot
The plot develops when a problem is established. Events follow with the
character's actions in response to these events. Suspense and action are far
more important than character development. The tale ends abruptly with a
quick resolution and a patterned ending. E.g. They lived happily ever after.
Themes
Themes in folktales reflect what the culture values - good overcomes evil.
Humility, kindness, patience, sympathy, hard work, and courage are
rewarded, Virtues of compassion, generosity, and humility triumph over
vices of greed, selfishness, and excessive pride.
Language
The language used was meant to hold the audience. Therefore it contains
rhyme, rhythm, personification, metaphors and similes.
In folktales, magic is considered a natural part of everyday life. Magic is
realised through the use of motifs. E.g.

> Magical powers

> Transformations

> Magical objects

> Wishes

> Trickery

Legends
A legend is a folk narrative set in present or historical past. Legends are
about people and their deeds, a piace or thing. The people are often
historical. Stories told for a purpose, but not completely true. Purpose
could have been to teach a lesson.

King Arthur

Robin Hood
Pourquoi Tales
Pourquoi is French for “why”. Pourquoi stories are short narratives that
have been passed orally in all cultures to provide an explanation for creation,
nature's elements, people's actions and animal characteristics.

A Different Ending
Students
> Problem solve, thinking creatively within the story framework.
> Share ideas orally.
> Give opinions and justify them.
> Think of alternative solutions.
Choose one of the folktales to use for this activity. Stop the story when the
characters and problem have been established.
Prediction: How do you think the story will finish?
Students can think and make notes or think and write an appropriate ending.
Sharing can be Think, Pair, Share/Small Group/Whole Class.
A variation could be to come up with another ending for a well-known folktale.
‘What if.’ questions might help. E.g.
What if Cinderella didn't want to marry the prince?
What if Jack couldn't chop down the beanstalk?
Discussion
Which ending do you like best? Justify your opinion.

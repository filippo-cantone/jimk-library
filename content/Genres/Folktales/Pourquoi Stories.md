# Pourquoi Stories

<!-- page 1 -->

POURQUOI STORIES
‘Pourquoi’ means ‘why’ in French. They are short narratives that have been passed on
orally in ail cultures to provide an explanation for creation, nature's elements, people's
actions and animal characteristics.
Elements
> Beginning - patterned
>» Setting - backdrop
> Characters - flat, stereotypical
> Problem - one dimensional
> Plot - actions to answer stated question
> End - short, patterned, states why things are the way they are today.
The pourquoi story structure follows the narrative one.
Patterned Beginning
Emphasises the time of the story rather than setting. Alerts listener that a
story is about to be told. E.g.
When the earth was young...
A long time ago...
Many, many years ago...
In the beginning of time...
In the beginning...
In the early days of the world...
One cold dark winter..
Collect these on a class chart as stories are read.
Setting
Tf we are told the setting it is usually the country or culture in which the story
takes place. The storyteller assumes the audience knows the setting.
Characters
Either animals, people, or nature. Again, because the storyteller assumes that
the audience knows about the animal or person, they are often presented
stereotypically. There is usually one main character with supporting characters.
The main character will change by the end of the story.
Conflict
One-dimensional, centreing on why or how something changed from the way it
used to be to how it is today.

Plot Development
Linear, revalving around the character's actions to solve the question of how or
why. The main character often receives help from supporting cast. The
resolution answers for us the burning question. End is short and sometimes
patterned.
Variety
> People as main character.
> Nature as main character.
> Animal as main character.
Teaching
Discuss questions the students might have about their environment, beginning each with
‘Why?' or ‘How?’ Explain that early cultures also asked questions about their
environment. Storytellers attempted to answer these questions through a story
because they did not possess scientific information.
Read-Aloud
Immerse the class in the genre through Read-Alouds. The students will come to
understand the structure of pourquoi stories by using the Pourquoi Map with the whole
class. Read one story and use questioning to help fill out the map.
A class chart can be kept - What We Discovered About Pourquoi Tales.
Beginning - How does the story begin? Add this to class ‘Pourquoi Leads List.’
Setting- How do you know where the story took place?
Do the illustrations give a clue to the culture or country of origin?
Characters - Who or what is the main character?
What do we know about this character?
Who are the supporting characters and why do they appear in the tale?
Conflict- What was the problem?
How was the earth different long ago?
Plot development.
What actions of the characters end in the change between the world as it
was, and the world that we know?
What is the resolution?
How does the tale end?
What do we learn at the end of the story?

How Echidna got his Quills L.& G. Adams

In the very beginning the world was empty and silent. There were no animals
in the bush. No birds to sing their songs. No pretty butterflies to flutter along. Even
the trees stood quietly, for there was no gentle breeze to whisper through their leaves.

When the Great one saw that the world was empty, he made creatures to fill it.
As he created each one, he gave it something to keep it from harm.

To Dingo he gave strong teeth. To Eagle he gave swift wings. To Native Cat he
gave sharp claws. To Crocodile he gave great jaws. To Owl he gave eyes that could see
in the night. To Kangaroo he gave long legs to run swiftly in flight.

But when at last he came to Echidna, the Great One had nothing left to give.
Nothing at all.

With nothing to keep himself safe, Echidna knew that soon the other animals
would try to eat him. So he quietly crept away. But as he was crawling into a bush, he
overheard the other animals talking.

“Pm feeling hungry,” said Dingo, snapping his teeth.

“1 want to eat,” growled Native Cat, stretching her claws.

“There’s no sense in hunting each other,” hissed Python, flicking his tongue in
and out. “We’re all too strong. But I know someone who can’t fight or bite.”

“What?” said Dingo in surprise. “Can’t fight or bite? Who can it be?”

“Echidna,” answered Python.

“Anyone who can’t fight or bite must have been made to be eaten,” purred
Native Cat. “Let’s eat Echidna.”

“Yes, let’s eat Echidna,” said the other hungrily.

Poor little Echidna shivered with fright. “I must find the Great One,” he
thought. “I must ask him to find me some long legs or sharp teeth or wings that can
fly. I must find him, or I shall die.”

Not daring to make a sound, Echidna quickly hurried off.

All through the hot, dry desert Echidna searched. But he wasn’t really sure what
the Great One looked like. So it was very hard to find him.

“T don’t suppose the Great One lives here at all,” said Echidna, resting his weary
paws. “The sun is much too hot.”

He looked towards the distant horizon and saw a long, green line that marked
the end of the desert and the beginning of the bush.

“Perhaps he lives over there, where everything is nice and green,” he thought.

Just as Echidna was setting off, a cloud of dust came swirling and whirling
across the desert. Closer and closer it came until it turned into a fearsome shape. It
was Dingo following Echidna’s trail!

Off to the distant bush Echidna shuffled as fast as his little legs could run.

“What a lovely meal I’m going to have,” growled Dingo as he drew closer. But
just as he pounced, Echidna reached the edge of the scrub and dived into a big clump of
thorny bushes.

“T must find the Great One,” whimpered Echidna, emerging from the bushes.
He had left Dingo sniffing around on the other side.

All through the bush Echidna searched. He peeked under the rocks and peered
high into the trees. He crept into dark caves and crawled through the thickets. But the
Great One was nowhere to be found.

“| know,” said Echidna as he spied something in the distance. “He must live by
that billabong. Everything there looks nice and cool.”

At last he reached up with a tiny paw. He felt about for something to hold, but
nothing was there.

“Nothing?” he thought. His heart skipped a beat. Surely this was the top of the
mountain. Carefully he pulled himself up and over the edge. He stood on the
mountain top in the ghostly mist.

“Is...is anyone here?” he whispered, hardly daring to speak. But no answer
came. ‘There’s no one here,” he thought. “My searching was all in vain.”

But as he stood there, sad and alone, the breeze came gently blowing. Just for a
moment the mist parted, and there...Echidna gasped! There, through the mist, was the
Great One.

“Why have you come to my mountain?” a great voice boomed.

“Please...I have come for something to keep me safe,” said Echidna hardly
daring to look.

“There is nothing left to givel” echoed the voice through the mist.

“But anything will do,” pleaded Echidna. “Some leftover wings or teeth or ...”

“There is nothing,” said the great voice. “Nothing but a few old bones!”

“But please,” cried Echidna. “You must give me something...”

“Must?” roared the voice angrily. “Must?” Then the Great One picked up the
old bones that lay scattered around. Suddenly there was a crash of thunder. The whole
mountain trembled and shook!

By the time the fearsome noise had faded and the mountain had become still
once again, the Great One had vanished.

But the bones! Oh how Echidna wished he had never come to the mountain!
All the bones the Great One had thrown were stuck in his skin. And try as he would,
Echidna could not pull them out. The bones had become part of him.

“So,” laughed Dingo when poor, sorry Echidna made his way to the bottom of
the mountain. “What bony old teeth the Great One has given you.”

“What bony old legs,” laughed Native Cat.

“What bony old wings,” mocked Eagle.

And as all his hungry enemies rushed in, little Echidna rolled himself up into a
tiny ball and squeezed his eyes shut with fright. But something very strange happened
as he waited for the end.

Dingo was the first to take a bite. As his teeth snapped shut on the bones, he
gave a painful cry.

Next, Native Cat pounced on Echidna. She screamed as the bones stuck into her
paws.

Crocodile took a bite and raced off howling horribly, with bones trailing from
his jaws.

Python tried to wrap his coils around the bony little bail and slithered away
hissing with pain.

Eagle squawked loudly as she landed on Echidna and flew off screeching, never
to be seen again.

When at last everything was quiet, the spiky little ball slowly opened its eyes and
looked about in surprise.

It was amazing. Echidna could hardly believe his eyes, His enemies had run
away in fear of his old bones.

“Bones?” thought Echidna. “They’re not just old bones. They’re...they’re
quills!” he laughed. “That’s what they are. Nice, sharp quills. Oh, thank you, Great
One. Thank you!”

And giving his nice new quills a proud litile shake, Echidna waddled off into the
bush, never to be bothered by the other animals ever again.

Reading Strategy - MAKING INFERENCES

Revise work already done with making inferences ~ (See Reading Strategies Course)
Although the focus is inferring, we realise that multiple strategies are being used as
they are all interconnected. Inferences are logical guesses, with the prediction
strategy very much in play. Inferences are predictions where the students will use
their prior knowledge and information gained from the text.

Game - What Are the Eyes Telling Us?

Tt is very easy to find photographs of front on expressive faces in magazines. With
frames, cover the faces apart from the eyes ~ one could be done for each table. As the students
pass these around they can write down inferences about what the person is feeling. The eyes are
the clue that is used to make inferences. The students realize that they are using their prior
knowledge of what eyes look like when a person is angry, happy etc. Reading can be like this when
we don’t have all the information but infer from the clues given. We create our own meaning of
the text from the experiences we bring to the text. That’s why people can interpret the text in
different ways, However we want the students to justify their inferences with evidence from the
text ~ What made you think this? What was it in the text that made you think this?

Using a Text the students will make inferences based on what they already know
combined with clues from behavior. E.g. Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People’s Ears by Verna
Aardema. (An African Pourquoi Story).

Prediction/Prior Knowledge: This is an African legend (pourquoi story) which gives a
possible explanation of why mosquitoes buzz in people's ears. Why do you think they do
this? What personal experience have you had? What did you do?

In Why Mosquitoes Buzz in people's Ears the characters make inferences based
on what other characters do. The problem in the story is that the inferences they
make about each other's behavior is wrong. These misunderstandings cause a great
problem. Our aim today is to identify the inferences that the animals make and the
problems these misunderstandings cause. As we read, you will notice that a red bird is
a part of every illustration. He witnessed all of the misunderstandings but never said
anything. We are going to pretend to be the red bird and talk back to the characters.
We will explain to each character how the inference that they made was incorrect.
Think Aloud: Read until python goes into rabbit's hole.

Python made an inference about the way iguana treated him. Iguana did not talk
to him, so python thinks that iguana is mad at him and planning mischief.

How Turtle Got His Tail Mourning Dove
No one could run faster than Rabbit. He had won many races and prizes. He had won
Frog's tail from Frog and Bear's tail from Bear. Rabbit's own tail was very long.

One sun, Turtle, who had no tail at all, went to Rabbit and said, “Spe-pa-lee’-na, my
friend, | would like to race with you. | think | can beat you. | would like to win those tails,
yours and the others.”

Rabbit laughed, for Ar-sikh’ was such a slow traveller. Rabbit made fun of him before
the people. But Turtle insisted, and Rabbit finally agreed to race with him. “Beating you will
be easy,” said rabbit. “When do you want to start?”

“Let us race tomorrow while the morning is young,” said Turtle.

The people all gathered the next morning early to watch the strange race between
Rabbit, the swift-jumper, and Turtle, the slow-walker. They started, and rabbit quickly left
turtle far behind.

“No use for me to run all the way now,” Rabbit remarked to himself. “I will sit down
awhile and wait for that silly Ar-sikh’. That will make him feel foolish.”

So Rabbit stopped to rest. He went to sleep. When he awoke, he was surprised to see
Turtle moving slowly along some distance ahead.

“| must have slept long,” said Rabbit, and he hopped swiftly after the slow-walker. He
passed Turtle and kept on going until, when he looked back, he could not see the other.
Then he sat down to wait, and again he fell asleep. While Rabbit slept, Turtle crawled by,
and when Rabbit opened his eyes there was turtle far ahead. But that did not worry Rabbit.
He easily overtook the slow one.

That is the way they raced, Rabbit running and resting and going to sleep, and Turtle
plodding, plodding without a stop. The race trail was long. !t went to a half-way stake and
returned to the point of starting. On the home stretch, Rabbit decided to take one last rest.
He intended to stay awake, but, in spite of himself, he fell asleep. When he finally awoke he
could not see Turtle anywhere.

“He ought to be in sight by now,” said rabbit. “Perhaps he has given up.” Then
Rabbit rubbed his eyes and looked again. Away off, near the finish-line, he saw a speck. It
was Turtle. Rabbit was startled. He jumped up and ran. He ran his fastest, but he had slept
too long. Turtle crawled over the finish-line first. Rabbit was a few leaps behind. The
people laughed and laughed at Rabbit, and he was very much ashamed.

Cutting off his own tail, so that there was only a little stump left, Rabbit gave it to
Turtle. He also gave Turtle the other two tails. Turtle first tried on Bear's tail. It was too long
and bushy. He threw it aside and tried on Rabbit's own tail. That did not suit him, either, as
the fur was thick and fine - the water and mud would make it too heavy. Then Turtle put on
Frog's tail.

“That is the tail | need,” said turtle. “Frog's tail matches my color, it hasn't any fur or
hair on it, and it is just the right size.”

All turtles have tails like that today, while rabbits have scarcely any tail at all.

DROLL or HUMOROUS STORIES
These stories are known by various names such as Numskull Stories or Noodlehead
Stories. They are told in the spirit of laughing at the silliness in all of us, not to make
fun of others. The same stories appear in many anthologies under different names.
Immersion ~ read Droll stories as Read Alouds and have the children read them if you
have copies.
Brainstorm a list of Key Elements that the students have discovered. With these
stories they may find exceptions to the general framework.
The main character in these stories always has a problem; i.e. something is not going
the way the character wishes it were going. Students listen or read to discover:

- What is the problem?

- How does the problem get solved?
Students can use the Patterns in Noodlehead Stories sheet to help them identify the
structure of most noodlehead stories.
Developing Characters
A key element in droll stories is the characters. When writing, the students will need
to develop their characters for the story to be effective, The Writing Game can help
here.

Students make up their own. It helps if they think of something foolish in ordinary

Z. Sutherland - Children and Books

Fables and Trickster Tales Around the World
Fables and trickster tales are short narratives that use animal characters with human
features. Taking on human wisdom we see human nature and human behavior exposed
through the animals.
As witha lot of folktales, they were handed down through oral tradition, being written
down many years later.
Anansi trickster tales derive from the Asante people of Ghana, and were brought by
African slaves to the Caribbean and parts of America. These stories developed into
Brer Rabbit stories, and were written down in the 19"" Century.

Illustrators

The Bibliography acknowledges the authors of the stories. However, if the school is
going to build up a collection of books to appreciate, then books with beautiful
illustrations should be considered. As with all picture books there is the balance
between text and picture - both are important elements. For instance, if looking to buy
a copy of Hanse/ and Gretel, (where most of the versions will be the same story), look
for the one illustrated by Lisbeth Zwerger, She is one of the world’s preeminent
children’s book illustrators, and has also illustrated Little Red Cap, The Bremen Town
Musicians, and The Little Mermaid. In 1990 she was awarded the Hans Christian
Andersen Medal for her lifetime contribution to children’s literature.

When studying Snow White in New York by Fiona French with older readers, we studied
also the illustrations which reflect the art deco period of the text. Similarly, in The
Smallest Samurai, Fiona French reflects the Japanese legend in her illustrations. She
has also illustrated Jamil's Clever Cat ~ a Folktale from Bengal and Lord of the Animals
- a Native American Creation Myth.

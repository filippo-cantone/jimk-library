# Teaching Choral Reading and Found Poetry

> The teaching method behind the Choral Reading packs, lifted into one place.
> Jim's wording is kept; headings and section order are mine. The book
> extracts and student poems stay in the two Texts files.

## 1. Why choral reading?

- It makes a great first move towards getting students to think about the
  writing in a text.
- It develops the sound of good writing in their heads — and so helps their
  writing.

## 2. The Draft & Craft lesson: reading aloud meaningfully

Use familiar, well-crafted text — this could be read during class read-aloud.

1. **Reconstruct favourite lines**, making them into a performance poem.
2. Students read the text, highlighting where they think the writing is best.
   *"Find the parts where the words are most striking"* — beautiful;
   interesting beat; surprising order; simple, yet so right; you want to read
   them again and again.
3. Find the most striking part. **Get it down to fifteen words or less.**
4. Practise reading them in best voices (quietly). Check with struggling
   readers.
5. Gather in a circle. Choose a starting point. *"I hope several people have
   chosen the same part, so we have a repeating refrain in our performance."*

**Extension — small groups (4–8, 10 minutes).** Each group crafts a choral
reading from their lines. They can repeat them, break parts of them up.
Perform — students can add movement, sound effects, reading in 2s, 3s,
softly, loudly, etc.

Ref: *Wondrous Words* by Katie Wood Ray.

## 3. Found poems: the independent version

As part of the reading curriculum, students have been introduced to choral
reading — taking words from a narrative text and turning them into poetry at
whole-class and group level, then performing. Found poetry is similar at the
**independent** level: a found poem is shaped after the student collects
words, phrases and sentences from a text — made from "found" words, though
students may add their own.

Although students draw on the mentor text's language, rearranging it and
adding their own, they create a unique poem of their own work. They may be
influenced by an author's imagery and observations about life, and by the
poetic craft already studied — they will look to use the text's craft as well
as the author's style and word choice.

**Method:**

- Choose a story or a particularly memorable passage.
- Highlight significant words, phrases and sentences — *"the parts where the
  words are most striking"* (beautiful; interesting beat; surprising order;
  simple, yet so right; you want to read them again and again).
- Make a list of the highlighted words in order. Double-spacing the lines is
  a good idea so the words can be worked with. Add anything spotted in
  re-reading that was missed the first time.
- Look over the list. Cross out anything dull, unnecessary, or off-theme.
- Minor changes may be needed so words fit together — tense, plurals,
  possessives.
- When revising: *"Does it make sense?"* Add a word or two so the poem makes
  sense, flows, or lands its point. Re-read.
- Edit for line breaks. Proofread and choose a title.

**Extension — parallel poem:** same line structure as the found poem, but a
completely different topic.

**Shorter instruction version** (for the wall or notebooks): read for
enjoyment → re-read highlighting striking words (powerful, moving,
interesting) → list in order → cross the dull → adjust to fit → check sense →
line breaks → proofread → title.

## 4. Mini-lessons across a series

Read-aloud: text with striking language. Over a series of lessons, look for
specific information:

- **Setting** — words describing it.
- **Characters** — words describing them.
- **Plot** — sequence of events; look for verbs, especially *unusual* verbs.
- **Vocabulary / word choice** — revise powerful words.
- **Poetic elements** — revise simile, metaphor, repetition.

Worked example — *Miz Berlin Walks* by Jane Yolen, a book used in choral
reading: *"Her hair was white and fine like a dandelion. Her skin was the
colour of milk agate, like some marbles. Oftimes she talked to herself.
Talking or singing or in quiet contemplation. Still nodding and talking up a
storm. One rained right into my hand, and it was all over gold. I would run
home stuffed full of tales. It was a hard tale full of hardscrabble dirt, a
two-room cabin, and a lot of hunger."*

## 5. Choosing texts

Any picture book or novel extract enjoyed for its language is suitable —
including the class novel (several classes have read *Holes* by Louis
Sachar). Grade 3/4 turned *My Great Aunt Arizona* by Gloria Houston into a
poem. If students are "reading like writers", their silent reading supplies
plenty of striking prose.

Mentor authors loved for striking language: Jerry Spinelli, Gary Paulsen,
Sharon Creech, Ralph Fletcher, Cynthia Rylant, Lois Lowry, Jane Yolen, Jean
Craighead George, Patricia MacLachlan, Eve Bunting.

As with other literary lessons, students are: using effective reading
strategies; reading self-selected literature; analysing plot, theme,
characterisation, style, mood and tone; discussing literary devices
(figurative language, dialogue); evaluating their impact on meaning; and
extending vocabulary.

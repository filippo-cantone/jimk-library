PRIMARY SOURCES
A primary source is first hand evidence. It was there at the time of the
event. It is contemporary to the period being studied.
Examples of primary sources:
Speeches letters songs
Journals/diaries interviews artifacts
Autobiographies photographs memoirs
Art literature news articles
Political cartoons charts/graphs maps
Documents
- official papers
- birth certificates
- public records
- legislation
‘Primary source: any material created by a person, including firsthand
accounts of particular events.
Secondary source: any interpretation of a primary source.’
M.Edinger
Through the use of Primary Sources students will:
- enjoy a memorable learning experience
- see history as real
- think about the past
- construct their own view of history
- learn through genuine inquiry
- appreciate that history has different points of view and
multiple interpretations
- learn writing craft
- continue an interest in words
- use research skills
- reinforce reading strategies
- take pride in creative presentations
- connect with others, learning tolerance and acceptance
- compare and contrast different stories in order to gain
an understanding of the past
- use various sources
- understand that people in different times and places view
the world differently

THE NAME PROJECT
We care about our names. Given to us by our parents, they soon become our
own. Sometimes they change, become shortened, or altered into nicknames.
Sometimes we like them and sometimes we don’t. However we feel about them,
they are still ours alone.

For this writing project you are going to explore your name. You may
already know a lot about it, or you may be surprised by what you discover.
Either way, it is bound to be a lot of fun, and a great way to introduce yourself.
Researching Your Name.

Primary Source Information.

A primary source is getting as close to the original as possible. In the case of
your name, your best primary source informants are your parents. After all, they
gave you your name. Find out as much as possible about why they named you
as they did. Are you named after a relative? If so, why? Were you names for
some other special reason? Try to find out as much as possible and write down
your source (your parent or anyone else you ask about your name) at the end.
Secondary Source Information.

A secondary source is further away from the original. In the case of your name it
might be some background about your name. Use ‘Baby Name Books’ to see
what you can find out about your name. Write down the source (book) from
which you get the information.

Thinking About and Describing Your Very Own Name.

Listen to or read “My Name” from Sandra Cisnero’s novel The House on Mango
Street and discuss the different ways she describes her name. Then write down
your own creative descriptions of your name.

Writing About Your Name.

Now use all the information you collected about your name to write an
interesting paragraph about your name. Be sure to provide an interesting lead to
get your readers interested, and a way to end the paragraph as well. Once
finished, read it over and revise it. Conference. Do a final edit.

My Name Sandra Cisneros

In English, my name means hope. In Spanish it means too many letters. It
means sadness, it means waiting. It is like the number nine. A muddy color. It
is the Mexican records my father plays on Sunday mornings when he is shaving,
songs like sobbing.

Tt was my great-grandmother’s name and now it is mine. She was a horse
woman too, born like me in the Chinese year of the horse ~ which is supposed to
be bad luck if you’re born female - but I think this is a Chinese lie because the
Chinese, like the Mexicans, don’t like their women strong.

My great-grandmother. I would've liked to have known her, a wild horse
of a woman, so wild she wouldn’t marry. Until my great-grandfather threw a
sack over her head and carried her off. Just like that, as if she were a fancy
chandelier. That’s the way he did it.

And the story goes she never forgave him. She looked out the window
her whole life, the way so many women sit their sadness on an elbow. I wonder
if she made the best with what she got or was she sorry because she couldn’t be
all the things she wanted to be. Esperanza. | have inherited her name, but I
don’t want to inherit her place by the window.

At school they say my name funny as if the syllables were made out of tin
and hurt the roof of your mouth. But in Spanish my name is made out of a softer
something, like silver, not quite as thick as sister’s name - Magdalena - which is
uglier than mine. Magdalena who at least can come home and become Nenny.
But J am always Esperanza.

I would like to baptise myself under a new name, a name more like the
real me, the one nobody sees. Esperanza as Lisandra or Maritza or Zeze the X.
Yes. Something like Zeze the X will do.

Zlata’s Diary Zlata Filipovic

A Child’s Life in Sarajevo
Sarajevo witnessed a savage war between Bosnians and Serbs. Although I haven’t been
to Sarajevo, I know the countryside as I drove down the Dalmatian coast when the
country was Yugoslavia.

I wonder if Zlata lived through this and recorded her diary similar to Anne Frank’s diary
during World War 11?

Zlata’s diary starts on Monday, 2 September 1991. She loves the countryside, “enjoying
and feeling the beauty of nature.” Living in the country I can really relate to her feelings.
Suddenly this changes as war starts, and Zlata struggles to understand the reasons. The
war is in Dubrovnik, “the most beautiful town in the world,” 1 would have to agree that
Dubrovnik is/was one of the most beautiful cities I had ever seen, and my thoughts are of
stunning orange-tiled roofs, windows underlined with geraniums, and steep steps lined by
old deeply-charactered buildings. I also know what happened to Dubrovnik during the
war and feel outraged. I can certainly empathise with Zlata.

Zlata speaks to her diary as Anne Frank spoke to hers — the difference being that Anne
gave her diary a name — Kitty. Zlata writes, “I took you with me, dear Diary, but I didn’t
write anything. You’re not cross with me, are you?”

I have just read where Zlata refers to Anne Frank’s diary and decides to give her diary a
name — Mimmy.

We learn Zlata’s thoughts and feelings through her diary entries.

“The people must be the ones to win, not the war, because war has nothing to do
with humanity. War is something inhuman...It destroys, kills, burns, separates, brings
unhappiness.”

Again I think of Anne Frank. She hid in an attic with her family; Zlata’s family goes to
the cellar. “The cellar is ugly, dark, smelly.”
I can see Zlata changing; from an eleven-year-old dreaming of getting Michael Jackson’s
autograph and joining Madonna’s fan club, to a girl facing war and the death of friends.
“AND NINA IS DEAD. A piece of shrapnel lodged in her brain and she died. She was
such a sweet, nice little girl. We went to kindergarten together, and we used to play
together in the park. Is it possible I'll never see Nina again? Nina, an innocent eleven-
year-old little girl — the victim of a stupid war. I feel sad. I cry and wonder why? She
didn’t do anything. A disgusting war has destroyed a young child’s life. Nina, PH
always remember you as a wonderful little girl.”
Zlata’s self-centredness changes as this innocent girl grows up too quickly.

Saturday, 23 May 1992.
Dear Mimmy,
I'm not writing to you about me anymore. I’m writing to you about war, death, injuries,
shells, sadness and sorrow.

Analysing Primary Sources
Photographs:

Look carefully at each section of the photograph and list what you
see,

What clues in this photograph tell you when it was taken?

What things can you find out about this child or these children by
looking at this photograph? Why?

If you could step into this photograph and talk to this child or these
children, what would you say? What questions would you ask?

How do you think this child or these children feel? Why?

How do you feel about the way this child or these children have to .
live? Why?

If appropriate ~

What do you think this child or these children might be thinking about
while working in this place? Why?

What dangers might this child or these children face by working in
this place? Why?

How would you feel if you were working with this child or these
children in this place? Why?

Students can expand this list by thinking about the needs, wants, families,
friends, feelings, and other aspects of their lives.

Some of these questions help students to make connections with children
from the past.

Students “can find common bonds with people far of f in time and
space, recognizing in them our common humanity, while understanding the
differences that may separate us.” M.Meltzer (The Story of
Ourselves: Teaching History Through Children’s Literature)

“Being able to take the perspective of people in the past is a
requirement for meaningful historical understanding." L.Levstik

This increases their observation skills and notetaking as they record
reactions.

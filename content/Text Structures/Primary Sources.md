## 1. What primary sources are

A primary source is first-hand evidence — it was there at the time of the
event, contemporary to the period being studied.

Examples: speeches, letters, songs, journals/diaries, interviews, artifacts,
autobiographies, photographs, memoirs, art, literature, news articles,
political cartoons, charts/graphs, maps, and documents (official papers,
birth certificates, public records, legislation).

> "Primary source: any material created by a person, including firsthand
> accounts of particular events. Secondary source: any interpretation of a
> primary source." — M. Edinger

Through primary sources students will: enjoy a memorable learning experience;
see history as real; think about the past; construct their own view of
history; learn through genuine inquiry; appreciate different points of view
and multiple interpretations; learn writing craft; keep an interest in words;
use research skills; reinforce reading strategies; take pride in creative
presentations; connect with others (tolerance and acceptance); compare and
contrast stories to understand the past; use various sources; and understand
that people in different times and places view the world differently.

## 2. Using primary sources as read-alouds

**Model with an object (artifact).** E.g. when Grade 2 studied Autobiography,
Jim modelled planning and writing with a penny-moneybox he received at age
four. Planning prompts: *Who gave it to you? How does it make you feel? How
did it make you feel when you first got it?* Then: brainstorm objects →
partner talk → independent writing (see Autobiography course). He also made a
"newspaper footy" to show the kinds of things he played with as a kid.

**Primary sources in literature.** E.g. E. B. White's early drafts of
*Charlotte's Web*; Lewis Carroll's original photographs of the real Alice;
C. Bjork, *The Other Alice* (letters); L. Erdich, *The Birchbark House*;
D. W. Myers, *At Her Majesty's Request*, and J. Hansen, *Breaking Ground,
Breaking Silence* — both biographies built from primary sources, and great
mentor texts for showing students how to write history themselves.

## 3. The Name Project (classroom handout)

*We care about our names. Given to us by our parents, they soon become our
own. Sometimes they change, shorten, or become nicknames… they are still ours
alone. For this project you will explore your name.*

**Researching your name — primary sources.** Get as close to the original as
possible: your parents. Are you named after a relative? For some other
special reason? Write down your source at the end.

**Secondary sources.** Use baby-name books for background on your name; write
down the book as your source.

**Describing your name.** Listen to or read "My Name" from Sandra Cisneros's
*The House on Mango Street*; discuss how she describes her name; write your
own creative descriptions.

**Writing about your name.** Combine everything into one interesting
paragraph — with a lead that hooks readers and a way to end. Revise,
conference, final edit. Ref: *Seeking History*.

Jim's own hook for the project: as a kid he played a street game where more
letters in your name meant a better chance of winning — the only kid with one
given name, he invented a middle name (Sebastian). Starter: *"Write down
your feelings about your name" (5 minutes).*

## 4. Found poems from primary sources

**Historical fiction — when to use it.** E.g. the Sovereign Hill visit with
Jackie French's *The Night They Stormed Eureka* (goldfields, 1854): strong
historical background, strong characters. Key questions: beginning of a unit
or after immersion? Does starting with fiction stop children constructing
their own version of the past?

> "Our students need to read and hear the stories and perspectives of all
> groups… and to use both secondary and primary sources if they are going to
> construct a more complete picture of a time period or event." — E. Fresch

Students construct knowledge through inquiry, discovery and authentic
activities — e.g. writing a journal in the voice of a historical figure.
Sovereign Hill prompts: *What did goldfields children do for fun? How did
pioneer children help their families? What hardships did they face, and how
did they overcome them?*

**Questioning for inquiry:** decide on a question → best guess (hypothesise)
→ examine primary sources → collect data → ask more questions → summarise →
conclusions → record. Keep asking: *Is there more than one answer? What
resources do I need? Have I asked why or how? Must I combine sources to
conclude?*

## 5. Units: children living through history

At different times the school took units that benefited from looking at
children in those times — Anne Frank's diary, the Holocaust through *Let the
Celebrations Begin*, ANZAC Day work, the Sovereign Hill camp, Grade 6
democracy. Candidate studies: Children Living Through War, Children in
Slavery, Child Labor, Immigration, Civil Rights.

> "Our students can make connections with history when it is approached as
> narratives — as true stories about real people… they can make a leap from
> the past to their lives today." — E. Fresch

Compare and contrast different stories — events, people, places, situations.
*We Were There, Too!* (P. Hoose) collects young people's stories in their own
words.

### Children living through war

Accounts of young people in wartime show war's impact on lives. Talk/write
about a time you did something you thought you couldn't do. Compare Anne
Frank's diary with *The Hidden Children* (H. Greenfeld) — Jewish children
hidden from the Nazis. Write poems, journals, letters, editorials from the
historical figures' perspectives. Compare primary sources with secondary
sources, and with each other.

**As mentor texts:** immerse in the period through research, then pretend to
live then — create journals, diaries, letters like the source being studied.

**Making connections to today:** 20th-century children in war-torn areas
wrote too — compare with the historical period. E.g. Zlata Filipovic,
influenced by Anne Frank; both wrote to imaginary friends: *"In a way we
were in the same position… writing a diary, lonely, can't go outside, losing
our childhood. The difference is she was in an attic and I was in a
cellar."* — Zlata.

Close the study with: *What did the children who survived have in common?
What can we learn from them? Is anything in my life somewhat similar? What
helped them through — and what helps us today?*

### Jim's response journal: Zlata's Diary (modelled for students)

*Shared with grades 5/6 in a lesson on conducting reading conferences — this
is the modelling working as intended.*

Sarajevo's war between Bosnians and Serbs; Jim connects it to his own drive
down the Dalmatian coast when the country was Yugoslavia, and to Dubrovnik —
"the most beautiful town in the world" — whose fate outrages him.

Zlata's diary opens Monday, 2 September 1991, loving the countryside — Jim,
living in the country, relates — then war scrambles everything. Like Anne,
Zlata addresses her diary; reading Anne, she names hers Mimmy (Anne's was
Kitty).

*"The people must be the ones to win, not the war, because war has nothing
to do with humanity. War is something inhuman… It destroys, kills, burns,
separates, brings unhappiness."*

Attic vs cellar: *"The cellar is ugly, dark, smelly."* Jim traces Zlata
changing from an eleven-year-old dreaming of Michael Jackson's autograph to a
girl facing war and friends' deaths:

*"AND NINA IS DEAD. A piece of shrapnel lodged in her brain and she died…
Nina, an innocent eleven-year-old little girl — the victim of a stupid war…
A disgusting war has destroyed a young child's life. Nina, I'll always
remember you as a wonderful little girl."*

*Saturday, 23 May 1992. Dear Mimmy, I'm not writing to you about me anymore.
I'm writing to you about war, death, injuries, shells, sadness and sorrow.*

### Child labor

Primary source: Lewis Hine photographs. *If you could step into the
photograph, what questions would you ask the children?* Coal mines — *Growing
Up in Coal Country* (S. Bartoletti), e.g. the boy with the mule: *What
dangers did they face? What was it like underground all day?*

Today: 10–20 million children work as bonded laborers — "like being a
slave." S. Newman, *Child Slavery in Modern Times*; D. Parker, *Stolen
Dreams*; S. Kuklin, *Iqbal Masih and the Crusaders Against Child Slavery*;
J. Springer, *Listen to Us*. Use photographs and stories together.

## 6. Analysing photographs (classroom protocol)

- Look carefully at each section; list what you see.
- What clues tell you when it was taken?
- What can you find out about this child / these children? Why?
- Step inside: what would you say? What would you ask?
- How do you think they feel? Why? How do you feel about how they live? Why?
- If appropriate: what might they be thinking while working there? What
  dangers do they face? How would you feel working alongside them?
- Expand the list via needs, wants, families, friends, feelings.

> "Students can find common bonds with people far off in time and space,
> recognizing in them our common humanity, while understanding the
> differences that may separate us." — M. Meltzer
> "Being able to take the perspective of people in the past is a requirement
> for meaningful historical understanding." — L. Levstik

**Display idea:** photographs with cartoon bubbles recording what students
would say to the subject; other students answer in role as the figure — and
write poetry in role. Revisit responses on thinking/feeling against the
primary accounts.

**Diversity:** lives in any period differ — ethnicity, economic background,
sex, location. The goldfields prints showed it plainly: different conditions
for the Chinese, for women, rich/poor, Aboriginal people, miners, police.

**Civil Rights:** guide photo study with feeling questions (*What do you
imagine she is feeling? The crowd? If you were in the scene, what would you
say — to her? To the crowd? Why?*). **Guided imagery:** *Close your eyes and
take a trip back in time. Imagine… Open your eyes — draw or write what you
felt.* Inquiry questions: *What motivated these children to participate? How
did they find the courage? How were they trained? What was each one's most
challenging situation?* Students develop their own. Compare *The Story of
Ruby Bridges* (R. Coles) with *Through My Eyes* (R. Bridges); *Children of
the Dust Bowl* (J. Stanley) with *Out of the Dust* (K. Hesse); photographs
with illustrations — what is similar, what is different? A graphic organiser
helps order attributes so information sticks.

**Making connections:** through a diary or letter, students infer a figure's
values and beliefs and compare with their own. Discuss: *How does the time we
live in affect our values, attitudes, beliefs and ideas?* *"When you believe
in something, even if you're afraid, you'll find a way to accomplish your
goals."* — Elizabeth Eckford, Little Rock Nine.

## 7. Multigenre research

A multigenre paper is a collage with one theme — each piece a different
genre revealing one facet: mini-research report (analysis and synthesis of
all collected research), diary entry, poetry, letters, interview, newspaper
article, fiction.

Most classes keep **nonfiction notebooks and resource folders** (models,
drafts, research notes, printouts) and **response journals** reflecting on
literature read.

### Six-Room Poetry (G. Heard, *Awakening the Heart*)

1. **Image.** Something seen outside — amazing, beautiful, interesting. See
   it like a photograph; describe (write) it accurately.
2. **Light.** Same image: bright? Shadows? Colors?
3. **Sounds.** Voices? If silent — what kind of silence? Empty? Lonely?
   Peaceful?
4. **Questions.** Anything you want to know or wonder about.
5. **Feelings.** Any feelings about the image.
6. **Room 6.** Look back over the five rooms; choose a word, phrase or line
   that feels important and repeat it three times. Arrange rooms in any
   order, cut freely — make a poem.

Additional rooms: three similes or metaphors; smells; touch; taste; *if your
image could speak, what would it say — what would its voice be like?*

**Double-voice poem:** two sides of an issue — conflicting opinions, or
saying vs thinking.

## 1. Why teach top-level structures?

Awareness of the organisational patterns in texts helps reading comprehension.
The patterns used to organise experience:

- **Compare and contrast**
- **Cause and effect**
- **Problem / solution**
- **Description and listing**

Work on top-level structures must be done **in the context of the genre being
studied, using real texts** — never as isolated exercises.

Why it matters:

- Understanding of the central message improves.
- Writing becomes more logical and clear.
- Recall of information is appreciably enhanced.

These patterns help students interpret and construct meaning at three levels:

| Level | What the reader does |
|---|---|
| Whole text | Finds the organisation of the most important information — "the structural theme running through the content of the text" (Bartlett and Briese, 1979). The main idea is content *plus* its organisation. |
| Paragraph | After recognising the overall structure, identifies organisation in the supporting details — often *different* from the overall pattern. |
| Sentence | Spots structures within and between ideas in a sentence (see §4). |

Opportunities to immerse students in the language of top-level structures arise
continually during the school day.

<!-- p.2–3 -->

## 2. How to teach them

Gradual release of responsibility, in order:

**Read-alouds / Modelled writing → Shared reading / Shared writing →
Guided reading / Guided writing → Group and independent work.**

Suggested mini-lessons:

1. **How to recognise the main top-level structures and their organisation.**
   Key question: *"How has the text been organised?"*
2. **Which words signal each structure?** These are *not* given as a prepared
   list — they are discovered within the texts being used (student-devised
   charts work better than teacher-given ones).
3. **Using graphic organisers as planning tools when writing** — to impose a
   structure, and to select important information when researching and
   note-taking.

## 3. Worked example: "Loss of Body Water"

This demonstration text runs through the pack. Overall structure is
**compare / contrast** (coaches vs doctors), with different structures inside
the supporting paragraphs.

- **Paragraph 1 — cause / effect** (or read as problem / solution): athletes
  must lose body water because they are overweight. Problem — coaches have
  athletes above maximum weight. Solution — dehydration.
- **Paragraph 2 — cause / effect**: dehydration is condemned because of
  harmful, dangerous side effects. Note the **effect is placed before the
  cause** — very common.
- Paragraph 2 signals cause / effect with **verbs**: *hurts, results in,
  causes* — "loss of body water *hurts* physical performance… a loss of five
  percent *results in* heat exhaustion… seven percent *causes*
  hallucinations…"
- The text's intention is **not** signalled in the title or first sentence.
  Paragraph 2 opens *"In contrast…"* (compare / contrast) — so the reader must
  identify the two views being contrasted to find the main idea:

| Loss required | Loss condemned |
|---|---|
| by coaches | by doctors |

- The pattern would have been more apparent if the text had opened:
  *"Coaches and doctors have opposing views on the deliberate loss of body
  water by athletes."*

### Sentence-level signals

**Conjunctions:**

- *The clouds are low and black.* (List)
- *Fred enjoyed playing tennis, but liked basketball best.* (Compare / contrast)
- *The whistle blew so the players stopped.* (Cause and effect)
- *The shark was hungry so it attacked a seal.* (Problem / solution)

**Adjectives, nouns and verbs:**

- *Jan is taller than Wendy.* (Adjective)
- *The athlete took the following steps to prepare for the race.* (Adjective and noun)
- *There were many reasons for the disaster.* (Noun signalling cause / effect)

## 4. Top-level structure and genre — how they differ

Similarities: both aim to enhance learners' ability to use language
effectively, and both give a system for making patterns of organisation
explicit.

| Top-level structure | Genre |
|---|---|
| Concentrates on developing underlying **thinking skills**. | Aims to improve learners' ability to use language **appropriately and effectively**. |
| Thinking patterns which help in **making sense**. | Means of **describing language use** and explaining variations. |
| Develops students' ability to **identify the main idea**. | Expects ideas to **follow a particular pattern**. |
| Concerned with only two textual features — overall structure and cohesion. | Makes explicit a **range** of textual features. |
| Focuses on enhancing ability to **recall information and ideas**. | *(—)* |

> "Genre and top-level structure, in combination, are extremely powerful tools
> for promoting and fostering independence and self-managed learning."
> — Ann Turner, *Patterns of Thinking*

## 5. The structures in detail

### Compare / contrast

Two parts of an idea are compared — similarity and difference. Often signalled
by words such as:

*however, on the other hand, while, in contrast, although, same, whereas,
instead, like, similar to, conversely, just as, even so, resemble, despite,
-est words, different from, only, neither, meanwhile, both, have in common,
compared with/to, share.*

But not always — sometimes the reader must **infer** compare / contrast to
make meaning when no signalling words are used:

> *The blue whale is big. Bigger than a giraffe. Bigger than an elephant.
> Bigger than a dinosaur. The blue whale is the biggest creature that has ever
> lived on Earth. Female blue whales are a little bigger than the males. Blue
> whales can grow to 100 feet long and weigh 150 tons — that's heavier than 25
> elephants or 115 giraffes.*
> — *Introduction to Big Blue Whale* by Nicola Davies

<!-- p.7 -->

### Cause / effect

What comes before, and what happens as a result. Signal words include:

*because, as a result of, so, consequently, therefore, effect, if…then, cause,
when, whenever, following, since, in order to, this led to, hence, thus, may
be due to, why.*

> *The arrival of settlers in Tasmania caused a great deal of disruption to
> the Tasmanian tiger. The tigers' hunting grounds were taken over by the
> farmers. As a result the tigers began eating the farmers' lambs and
> chickens. The farmers began shooting and poisoning the tigers. The effect of
> this was that the Tasmanian tigers soon disappeared. They are probably now
> extinct.*
> — *Patterns of Thinking*

<!-- p.8 -->

### Problem / solution

Helps students identify and clarify a difficulty. Signal words:

*to prevent, problem, question, solution, answer, difficulty, concern,
resolution, solved, trouble, resolve, decision.*

In this pattern there is always something that **intervenes**. It is evident
in the narrative story map, and helps students recognise a story's underlying
theme: as the complication progresses, conflicts and challenges appear, which
the characters solve.

> "Events or episodes in stories are built around problems and solutions,
> causes and effects. The writer organises ideas and information to show links
> between: what the characters did, thought and felt; how one action led to
> another; how conflicts were resolved; how a character's personality
> influenced the course of events."
> — Ann Turner, *Patterns of Thinking*

<!-- p.9 -->

### Description and listing

Description draws on:

- **lists**
- **unordered collections of details**
- **sequences** — ordered logically or chronologically

Signal words: *for example, for instance, first, such as, characteristics,
then, many, parts, after, finally, as time passed, also, next, some, features,
types, later, to begin with.*

- **Lists** sometimes support larger structures (compare / contrast,
  cause / effect, problem / solution). E.g. a TV guide is a compare /
  contrast structure containing three lists: titles, channels, programs.
- **Collections of details**: a series of statements of fact or feeling about
  a topic.
- **Logical and chronological sequences**:

> *The pack studies the herd of musk oxen to find a weak animal, one that may
> not be able to defend itself. Now the wolf pack chases the herd. The herd
> starts to run. Soon, the pack will strike. The pack surrounds the selected
> animal and will bring it down together. After the leaders eat, the others
> get their turn. Each wolf can eat as much as 20 pounds of meat.*
> — M. Berger, *Call of the Wolves*

Description in narrative:

> *The barn was very large. It was very old. It smelled of hay and it smelled
> of manure. It smelled of the perspiration of tired horses and the wonderful
> sweet breath of patient cows. It often had a sort of peaceful smell — as
> though nothing bad could happen ever again in the world.*
> — E. B. White, *Charlotte's Web*

Listing often supplies the supporting details of a larger structure:

> *In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit. Not a nasty, dirty, wet
> hole, filled with the ends of worms and an oozy smell, nor yet a dry, bare,
> sandy hole with nothing in it to sit down on or to eat: it was a
> hobbit-hole, and that means comfort.*
> — J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Hobbit*

This also shows the structures **overlap**: *"…a great deal of effective
writing derives its power from their interconnection and interdependence."*
— Ann Turner.

## 6. Putting structures to work in writing

Particular genres favour particular structures:

| Structure | Suits |
|---|---|
| Cause / effect | Explanations, discussions, arguments |
| Description | Procedures, recounts, information reports |

E.g. **information reports**: students use the top-level structures of
description and listing, use appropriate signalling words, and use graphic
organisers.

## Appendix A — Demonstration text: LOSS OF BODY WATER

> *The loss of body water is frequently required by athletic coaches of
> wrestlers, boxers, judo contestants, karate contestants, and 150-pound
> football team members so that they will reach specified body weights.*
>
> *In contrast to action taken by coaches, loss of body water for athletes is
> strongly condemned by the doctors of America. They condemn loss of body
> water due to the fact that loss of body water hurts physical performance
> and loss of five percent results in heat exhaustion. Moreover, a loss of
> seven percent of body water causes hallucinations. Losses of ten percent or
> more of body water result in heat stroke, deep coma, and convulsions; if not
> treated, death will result.*

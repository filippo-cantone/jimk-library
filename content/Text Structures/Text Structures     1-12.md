Text Structures
Awareness of the organisational patterns in texts is helpful in reading
comprehension.
Patterns used to organise experiences -
Top Level Structures:

Compare and Contrast

Cause and Effect

Problem/Solution

Description and Listing
The work on top-level structures needs to be done in the context of the genre
being studied. Real texts must be used. As in other areas, the gradual release of
responsibility model is employed. Teachers are able to use:

Read Alouds Modelled Writing

Shared Reading Shared Writing

Guided Reading Guided Writing

Group and Independent work
Possible Mini-lessons:

- How to recognise the main top-level structures and their
organisation.

“How has the text been organised?"

- Which words signal the various top-level structures? These are not
given as a prepared list, but discovered within the texts being used. We have
spoken before about the advantages of 'student-devised' charts.

- Use of appropriate graphic organisers as planning tools when writing:
as well as imposing a structure on a text and helping to select important
information when researching/notetaking.

Why Teach Top-Level Structures?

- Understanding of central message improves.

- Writing is more logical and clear.

- Recall of information appreciably enhanced.
The pattern characteristics of top-level structures help students to interpret
and construct meaning at different levels of the text.

- Whole text level
- Across sentences at the paragraph level (the sentence
pattern may be different from the whole text pattern).
- Between ideas within a sentence.
Opportunities continually arise during the school day to immerse the
students in the language of top-level structures.
At the whole text level:
Top-level structure is ..."(the) organization of the most important
information in the text. It reveals the structural theme running through the
content of the text." (Bartlett and Briese 1979)
The mail idea or critical information in a text involves content and its
organisation.
At the paragraph level:
After recognising the overall structure of the text and the main idea, the
redder can then identify patterns of organisation in the supporting details.
Often different from the overall pattern. E.g. Loss of body water.
Overall structure - compare/contrast.
Supporting details
- Cause/effect, paragraph 1. Athletes are required to lose
body water because they are overweight: or interpreted
as problem/solution. Problem - coaches have a problem
that athletes are above maximum weight. Solution -
dehydration.
- Cause/effect, paragraph 2. Dehydration condemned
because it has harmful and dangerous side effects.
Note: Effect placed before cause. This is common.
At the sentence level:
The structures can be seen at the sentence level -
- Using conjunctions/
The clouds are low and black. (List)
Fred enjoyed playing tennis, but liked basketball best.
(Compare/contrast)
The whistle blew so the players stopped. (Cause and effect)
The shark was hungry so it attacked a seal. (Problem/solution)
- Using adjectives, nouns and verbs,
Jan is taller than Wendy. (Adjective)

LOSS OF BODY WATER

The loss of body water is frequently required by athletic coaches of
wrestlers, boxers, judo contestants, karate contestants, and 150-pound
football team members so that they will reach specified body weights.

In contrast to action taken by coaches, loss of body water for
athletes.is strongly condemned by the doctors of America. They
condemn loss of body water due to the fact that loss of body water hurts
physical performance and loss of five percent results in heat exhaustion.
Moreover, a loss of seven percent of body water causes hallucinations.
Losses of ten percent or more of body water result in heat stroke, deep
coma, and convulsions; if not treated, death will result.

Cause/effect.
What comes before and what happens as a result.
Signalling words:
because asa result of so
consequently therefore effect
Hf..then cause when
whenever following since
tn order to this led to hence
thus and may be due to why
The arrival of settlers in Tasmania caused a great deal of disruption to the
Tasmanian tiger. The tigers’ hunting grounds were taken over by the
farmers. As a result the tigers began eating the farmers' lambs and
chickens. The farmers began shooting and poisoning the tigers. The effect
of this was that the Tasmanian tigers soon disappeared. They are probably
now extinct.

Problem/solution
The pattern helps students to identify and clarify a difficulty.
Signal words:
to prevent problem question
solution answer difficulty
concern resolution solved
trouble resolve decision
In a problem/solution pattern there is always something that intervenes.
Problem/solution is evident in the narrative story map. It assists students
in recognising the underlying theme of the story. As the complication in the
story progresses, conflicts and challenges appear, to which the characters
solve the problems.
“Events or episodes in stories are built around problems and solutions,
causes and effects,
The writer organises ideas and information to show links between

- what the characters did, thought and felt

- how one action led to another

- how conflicts were resolved

- how a character's personality influenced the course of

events”
Ann Turner in Patterns of Thinking

Description
Description draws on

- lists

- unordered collection of details

- sequences - ordered logically or chronologically
Signal words:
for example for instance first
such as characteristics then
many parts after finally
as time passed also next
some features types later
to begin with
Lists. Sometimes lists are used as supporting detail with compare/contrast,
cause/effect and problem/solution structures.
The TV guide is a compare/contrast structure. Tt includes three lists of
titties, channels, and programs.
Collections of details. Usually a series of statements containing facts or
feelings about a topic.
Logical and chronological sequences.
The pack studies the herd of musk oxen to find a weak animal, one that may
tot be able to defend itself.
Now the wolf pack chases the herd. The herd starts to run. Soon, the pack
will strike.
The pack surrounds the selected animal and will bring it down together.
After the leaders eat, the others get their turn. Each wolf can eat as much
ds 20 pounds of meat.

M.Berger  Call of the Wolves
Description in narrative.
The barn was very large. It was very old. It smelled of hay and it smelled
of manure. It smelled of the perspiration of tired horses and the wonderful
sweet breath of patient cows. It often had a sort of peaceful smell - as
though nothing bad could happen ever again in the world.
E.B.White Charlotte's Web

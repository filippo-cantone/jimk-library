INQUIRY BASED APPROACH

“A skilled magazine writer can make any subject fascinating, yet in
school we teach children to write research papers that often end up
being lists of facts nailed together instead of a legitimate inquiry into
a topic.”

B.Lane  After the End: Teaching and Learning Creative Revision
Our studies become ‘issue-driven.’
“Students investigate an issue or pose a question that eventually leads
them to a thesis statement. They then analyse and shape ideas
relevant to the thesis into something that is meaningful to them. It is
this transformation of ideas - how they manipulate and synthesise
information - that causes students to think critically, to draw
conclusions, and to assimilate new information into their existing
schema.”

M.McMackin Knowing How
We begin with an issue, thesis statement, or question students want
to investigate. What is the big question that will bind their work
together? This means putting a lot of effort into shaping our topic -
as much as researching it.
Writing non-fiction requires a great amount of high-level thinking as
the students have to use data from multiple sources, analyse it,
reorganize it into organizational structures, and make connections to
integrate this new data. All this with passion!
“Passion is at the heart of inquiry. Interest and curiosity breed
engagement. We must honour kids’ passion and celebrate their
individuality. Exploring passions, interests, and questions brings the
world into focus and opens the door for the broadest interpretation
of ideas. Solutions to big questions rarely emerge from cloned
thoughts ... Passion is contagious.”

S.Harvey Nonfiction Matters

Formulating a Research Question
This can be modelled by demonstrating how some questions from the
What I Want to Know column are better research questions than
others. By doing this the students can see the difference between
literal and open-ended questions. This shows them that they will be
doing research in order to find answers to the important questions
they have. E.g. If a question could be answered in a word or two, it is
a little question.
The children will also be able to see here that some of their questions
may not be able to be answered. Nonfiction picture books are useful
in mini-lessons on big and little questions. E.g. Grade 4 has been
studying Biography. Picture book - Eleanor by B.Cooney. (Eleanor
Roosevelt).
Little question: — How did she meet FDR?
Big question: How does someone who is so afraid and who comes
from such a sad background become such a famous person?

Analysing Nonfiction Text Structure

When the students understand the text structure, they have a better
chance of determining important information.

Stephanie Harvey (Nonfiction Matters) cites the work from a reading
workshop where the teachers built sentences around the term goose bumps.

* Cause and Effect. Goose bumps make me shiver. When
the temperature drops below 45 degrees, my skin
crinkles into goose bumps.

* Problem and solution. Goose bumps make me shiver. But
they disappear as soon as I cover up with a jacket or
sweater.

* Explanation (Harvey refers to this as Question and
answer). What happens to people when they shiver?

They get goose bumps.
* Compare and contrast. Some people get goose bumps
from fear. Others get goose bumps when they are
. touched emotionally.
© Description. Goose bumps make me shiver. I get little
bumps on my skin. They look like sesame seeds.
* Sequence. Goose bumps make me shiver. First I get
cold, Then I shake all over.
When students know what to look for in terms of text structure, meaning is
grasped more easily.

Strategies to Connect One Paragraph to the Next
Teachers can collect strategies from nonfiction books/magazines, as well as
student samples.
1. Repeat a Word

Paragraphs are linked by a word in the last sentence of a paragraph

being repeated in the first sentence of the next.

The (Light Armoured Vehicle-25) is more than five and a half
feet longer, two feet taller, a foot wider, and more than 17,000 pounds
heavier than a Humvee. The new vehicle was built to get people to battle
quicker and give them more power.

And power this vehicle has: a 25-millimetre chain gun, one
machine gun and another free-hand machine gun on top for the
commander...

T.Andrews “Rough Rider” Boys’ Life March 2000

2. Related Words
Related word, often a synonym repeated.
Some of this year's heroes - New York City rescue workers -
gathered in Hawaii to honour the heroes of decades before.
President Bush paid tribute to the veterans.
‘Remembering Pearl Harbour’ Time for Kids Dec.2001
3. End a Paragraph with a Question
The interesting question is addressed in the next paragraph.
Take the idea of extraterrestrial life - things that don’t live on
Earth. If you've seen any old Martian movies, you know that Mars has
long been a favourite place to locate all types of extraterrestrials. How
come?
“We're interested in Mars because we're next to it,” says Pascal
Lee, planetary scientist at NASA Ames Research Centre. Plus, Mars is
covered with soil and rocks like Earth. “When we look at the landscape of
Mars, it looks familiar. This feeds our imagination,” Lee adds.
N.Finton ‘Visions of Mars" National Geographic for Kids, Mar.2002
4. Begin a Paragraph with a Question
Nearly 3 million people along the East Coast had been ordered
to pack up and get out last week. Tt was the biggest evacuation in U.S.
history.
What evil force could possibly drive so many people from their
homes? ....
‘A Monster Hurricane’ Time for Kids Sept 24, 1999

7. “Another”
*...is another” or “another...is"
Additional supporting evidence.

8. Appositive
Information contained within two commas connects the new ideas to
preceding writing.

Last week, as rain poured down on scorched earth, people tried
to return to their homes in Goma, Democratic Republic of Congo. Many
found they had nowhere to go. Their city was buried under a layer of
hardened lava.

On January 17, Mount Myiragongo, a volcano near Goma,
erupted, sending rivers of red-hot lava into the city ...

“Congo's Devastating Volcano" Time for Kids Feb 1 2002
9. Several to One
The author selects one item from his list in the preceding paragraph,
and elaborates on it.

10. Back and Forth

Author starts with the idea developed in the last paragraph, but goes

on to a new related idea.

Genetic engineering has mostly helped U.S. farmers. But many
scientists are excited about other uses. For example, they are working on
ways to put genes that produce vaccines into bananas and other foods...

N.Fitzgerald “Food: New and Improved" National Geographic for
Kids Nov/Dec 2001
11. Command

Each paragraph begins with a verb. E.g. “Look”, “Check out”, “See”,
“Take a close look".

Sylvia Earle, an oceanographer, was answering questions in an
interview. E.g. “What's your advice for kids who want to explore the
ocean?”

“Start exploring today."

“Look for patterns.”

‘Deep Sea Diver" National Geographic for Kids Sept 2001
12.Same Start

Consecutive paragraphs begin with the same words. We encountered
this when we studied Memoir - When I Was Young in the Mountains by
Cynthia Rylant. The words have to be well-crafted to have the desired
effect.

“Barton also became a scientist.”

“Barton even became a movie star."

NONFICTION LEADS

Question

The author begins by posing a thought provoking, open-ended question.
“Imagine”

The author hooks the reader by drawing him directly into a scene that
evokes another time, place, or situation. The word “imagine” often occurs in
this lead.

Guess who or what

The author immediately snatches the reader's attention by inviting him to
solve a riddle.

Common Reaction

With this strategy the author begins with a generally held impression of her
topic.

Right to the Point

The author begins with one short, clear, declarative sentence that states
the main idea. The curt statement begs for a fuller explanation, which is
found in the remainder of the text.

Series of Intriguing Questions

The writer provides a series of well-designed, open-ended questions that
may appear to be unconnected. The questions urge the reader on, setting up
the expectation that the questions will be answered and that the
relationship that ties them together will be revealed.

Question and Two-Word Questions

The author begins by asking one major question and then breaks this
question down into a series of two-word subquestions.

Hanging On

The author provides a series of clues, which the reader uses to uncover the
identity of the topic.

Past to Present

The author makes a connection with the past. He notes how things once
were and then moves to the present.

---

<!-- page 15 -->

Indisputable Fact

The writer leads off with a statement that is unquestionable; one with which
everyone could agree.

What's Wrong?

There is a problem. The author clearly identifies it in the lead sentence.
Anecdote

The writer shares a short story that relates to the topic.

Inside Someone's Head

With this strategy, the author reveals what someone or something is
thinking.

What's Being Done?

The lead immediately introduces the reader to the issue at hand and the
step(s) taken to address the issue.

Paradox (Something that goes against common sense)

With this strategy, there appears to be a contradiction between what has
been written and what we believe to be true.

NONFICTION CONCLUSIONS

Dreaming of the future

The article concludes with the author wondering about future possibilities
for his topic.

Posing a Question

The author asks a thought-provoking question to get the reader to continue
to think about the topic after the piece has been read.

Ask a Question and Answer It

The author concludes by asking a question and then supplying the answer in
the same paragraph.

“Although”

The author ends the writing by contrasting a piece of information with the
main point of the article. The first word of this conclusion is "although".
“Finally”

Many writers signal the end of their piece by beginning the last paragraph
with the word “finally.”

Help

The author lets the reader know that help is needed.

No Doubt About It

The author ends the piece by drawing one indisputable conclusion about her
topic. Phrases such as “one thing seems certain,” “Without a doubt,” or
“There is no question” may signal the use of this strategy.

Giving Advice

The author ends the piece by providing a bit of advice or a suggestion.
Sharing an Emotion

The author describes the emotional effect the topic has had on him.
What's Next?

The author concludes by telling the reader what the final step in a sequence
of events will be.

---

<!-- page 17 -->

Right Now

The author concludes by bringing the reader up-to-date. He describes the
topic's current condition, often using the word “today” to signal this
strategy.

“No Matter” Plus Draw a Conclusion

The author begins with the words “No matter." Next she draws a conclusion.
Providing a Summary

The author recaps the key points in the article or report in the last
paragraph.

Connecting Beginning and End

The author connects information from the first paragraph to information in
the final paragraph. Often the same words are repeated in both parts of
the report or article.

Quote

The author finds an appropriate quote from her research that can bring
closure to the piece.

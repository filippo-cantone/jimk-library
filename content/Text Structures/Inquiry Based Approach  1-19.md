## 1. The idea: studies become issue-driven

> "A skilled magazine writer can make any subject fascinating, yet in school
> we teach children to write research papers that often end up being lists of
> facts nailed together instead of a legitimate inquiry into a topic."
> — B. Lane, *After the End: Teaching and Learning Creative Revision*

> "Students investigate an issue or pose a question that eventually leads them
> to a thesis statement. They then analyse and shape ideas relevant to the
> thesis into something that is meaningful to them. It is this transformation
> of ideas — how they manipulate and synthesise information — that causes
> students to think critically, to draw conclusions, and to assimilate new
> information into their existing schema."
> — M. McMackin, *Knowing How*

We begin with an issue, thesis statement, or question students want to
investigate. **What is the big question that will bind their work together?**
Shaping the topic takes as much effort as researching it.

Writing non-fiction demands high-level thinking: students use data from
multiple sources, analyse it, reorganise it into organisational structures,
and connect it to integrate new data. All this with passion:

> "Passion is at the heart of inquiry. Interest and curiosity breed
> engagement. We must honour kids' passion and celebrate their individuality.
> Exploring passions, interests, and questions brings the world into focus…
> Passion is contagious."
> — S. Harvey, *Nonfiction Matters*

## 2. Stages of an inquiry

### Exploring topics

The teacher guides students in coming up with stimulating, challenging
questions as they read — and read. As they read, they narrow the search for
a topic.

### Knowing what you know (and don't know)

T-chart:

| What I Know | What I Want to Know |
|---|---|
| | |

### Formulating a research question

Model how some questions from the *What I Want to Know* column are better
research questions than others — the difference between literal and
open-ended questions. If a question could be answered in a word or two, it is
a **little question**. Nonfiction picture books work well in mini-lessons on
big and little questions. E.g. Grade 4 studying Biography with *Eleanor* by
B. Cooney (Eleanor Roosevelt):

- Little question: How did she meet FDR?
- Big question: How does someone who is so afraid, and who comes from such a
  sad background, become such a famous person?

The big question propels the study into her personal life and
accomplishments — elements from the Biography genre study. Big questions lead
to memorable conclusions; little questions lead to lists of isolated facts.
Some questions may prove unanswerable — that is fine.

Point students at their knowledge of expository text structures (previous
mini-lessons): research reports are structured around organisational
patterns — description, sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast,
explanation, problem and solution.

<!-- p.3–4 -->

### Collecting data

Data charts help students list facts, organise them, and write summaries from
notes — easier if they have done the groundwork on expository text
structures. E.g.

- **Description.** Details clarify the event, person, time in history, etc.
- **Sequence.** Paragraphs written chronologically.
- **Compare and contrast.** Similarities and differences discussed.
- **Cause and effect.** A result (effect) is identified, and what led to it
  (causes).
- **Explanation.** Why something happened or how something works.

Students then design their **own** data charts, knowing how to categorise
information:

> Topic:
> Research Question:
> Break down inquiry questions into smaller parts.

Worked set-up — compare and contrast. Topic: raccoons. Research question:
*What beneficial and harmful things do raccoons do while interacting with
humans and the environment?*

| Beneficial Things | Harmful Things | Interesting Facts |
|---|---|---|
| | | |

### Narrowing the search — note-taking rules

Students take notes that answer their research questions:

- Record only important ideas.
- Use key words/phrases.
- Use own words — abbreviations etc.
- Devise own codes.

Model via think-aloud → whole-class share → group work → independent work.

### Recording, organising, drafting

Using the data chart, students decide how to order the information — sometimes
as simple as numbering their notes. Then to first draft; revisit writing
mini-lessons (leads, logical progression of ideas).

### A note on leads (matching writers to strategies)

Good guided-writing work: a small group on leads, endings, transitions.

> "Good leads set up expectations in the reader's mind, and also, like
> flashlights, they help writers to see where they are heading."
> — B. Lane, *After the End*

### Presentation — what will we teach?

- PowerPoint presentations
- Visual presentation
- Publishing
- Video
- Oral presentation

## 3. Analysing nonfiction text structure

Once students understand text structure, they have a better chance of
determining important information. Stephanie Harvey (*Nonfiction Matters*)
cites a reading workshop where teachers built sentences around the term
*goose bumps*:

- **Cause and effect.** *Goose bumps make me shiver. When the temperature
  drops below 45 degrees, my skin crinkles into goose bumps.*
- **Problem and solution.** *Goose bumps make me shiver. But they disappear
  as soon as I cover up with a jacket or sweater.*
- **Explanation** (Harvey calls this question-and-answer). *What happens to
  people when they shiver? They get goose bumps.*
- **Compare and contrast.** *Some people get goose bumps from fear. Others
  get goose bumps when they are touched emotionally.*
- **Description.** *Goose bumps make me shiver. I get little bumps on my
  skin. They look like sesame seeds.*
- **Sequence.** *Goose bumps make me shiver. First I get cold, then I shake
  all over.*

When students know what to look for, meaning is grasped more easily.

## 4. Nonfiction writing craft

E.g. the handouts on Voice in Nonfiction, Nonfiction Leads, Endings.

**Instructional method:**

1. Name the strategy — *What do you think this strategy will be like?*
2. Describe the strategy.
3. Read a sample. (At Wembley, students paste strategy samples into the
   Nonfiction section of their Writer's Notebook.)
4. Students share how they have used it — share time as learning time, not
   every student reading their work.

### Twelve ways to connect one paragraph to the next

Collect examples from nonfiction books, magazines and student samples.

1. **Repeat a word.** A word in the last sentence of a paragraph is repeated
   in the first sentence of the next. *"The (Light Armoured Vehicle-25) is
   more than five and a half feet longer… And power this vehicle has: a
   25-millimetre chain gun…"* — T. Andrews, "Rough Rider", *Boys' Life*,
   March 2000.
2. **Related words.** A synonym repeated. *"Some of this year's heroes — New
   York City rescue workers — gathered in Hawaii to honour the heroes of
   decades before."* — "Remembering Pearl Harbour", *Time for Kids*,
   Dec 2001.
3. **End a paragraph with a question**, answered in the next. *"Take the idea
   of extraterrestrial life… How come?"* — N. Finton, "Visions of Mars",
   *National Geographic for Kids*, Mar 2002.
4. **Begin a paragraph with a question.** *"What evil force could possibly
   drive so many people from their homes? …"* — "A Monster Hurricane",
   *Time for Kids*, Sept 24, 1999.
5. **Contrast.** Words in the new paragraph contrast with the previous —
   e.g. after paragraphs on high achievement: *"Despite the fierce
   competition of the Games, Olympic organisers want participants to relax…"*
   — R. Upadhyay, "Off to the Olympics", *Time for Kids*, Feb 1, 2002.
6. **"Although"** — signals a different idea. *"Although their pride in
   their heritage is strong, these students can't neglect English."* —
   K. Hoffman, "Their Native Tongue", *Time for Kids*, Jan 26, 2001.
7. **"Another…"** — additional supporting evidence. *"Another sticky issue
   was whether countries that took part in slavery should apologise…"* —
   "A Struggle to End Prejudice", *Time for Kids*, Sept 14, 2002.
8. **Appositive** — information within two commas connects to the preceding
   writing. *"On January 17, Mount Nyiragongo, a volcano near Goma,
   erupted…"* — "Congo's Devastating Volcano", *Time for Kids*, Feb 1, 2002.
9. **Several to one.** The author selects one item from the preceding
   paragraph's list and elaborates.
10. **Back and forth.** Start with the last paragraph's idea, move to a new
    related one. *"Genetic engineering has mostly helped U.S. farmers. But
    many scientists are excited about other uses…"* — N. Fitzgerald, "Food:
    New and Improved", *National Geographic for Kids*, Nov/Dec 2001.
11. **Command.** Each paragraph begins with a verb: "Look", "Check out",
    "See". *"Start exploring today." "Look for patterns."* — "Deep Sea
    Diver", *National Geographic for Kids*, Sept 2001.
12. **Same start.** Consecutive paragraphs begin with the same well-crafted
    words — as in memoir *When I Was Young in the Mountains* (C. Rylant).
    *"Barton also became a scientist." "Barton even became a movie star."*

### Nonfiction leads

- **Question.** A thought-provoking, open-ended question.
- **"Imagine".** Draws the reader into a scene from another time or place.
- **Guess who or what.** A riddle.
- **Common reaction.** Opens with a generally held impression of the topic.
- **Right to the point.** One short declarative sentence stating the main
  idea — begging fuller explanation.
- **Series of intriguing questions.** Apparently unconnected; the reader
  reads on expecting answers and the link revealed.
- **Question and two-word questions.** One major question broken into
  two-word sub-questions.
- **Hanging on.** A series of clues; the reader uncovers the topic.
- **Past to present.** How things were, then the present.
- **Indisputable fact.** A statement everyone agrees with.
- **What's wrong?** A problem, identified in the lead sentence.
- **Anecdote.** A short related story.
- **Inside someone's head.** What someone or something is thinking.
- **What's being done?** The issue plus the steps taken.
- **Paradox.** An apparent contradiction with what we believe.

<!-- p.14–15 -->

### Nonfiction conclusions

- **Dreaming of the future.** Wondering about possibilities.
- **Posing a question.** The reader keeps thinking after the piece.
- **Ask a question and answer it** — in the same paragraph.
- **"Although".** Ends by contrasting information with the main point.
- **"Finally".** Signals the end directly.
- **Help.** The reader learns help is needed.
- **No doubt about it.** One indisputable conclusion ("Without a doubt…").
- **Giving advice.** A suggestion.
- **Sharing an emotion.** The topic's effect on the author.
- **What's next?** The final step in a sequence.
- **Right now.** Up to date, often signalled by "today".
- **"No matter" plus a conclusion.**
- **Providing a summary.** Key points recapped.
- **Connecting beginning and end.** Same words repeated in both.
- **Quote.** Research that brings closure.

Ref: *Knowing How* (McMackin). <!-- p.16–17 -->
